- **$\mathcal{V}$** vocabulary set; $|\mathcal{V}|$ is the vocabulary size
- **$\mathcal{S} = \mathcal{V}^S$** space of all possible sequences of length $S$ over the vocabulary
- **$\mathbf{x} = x^1 \dots x^S$** sequence of $S$ tokens, where each token $x^i$ takes values in $\mathcal{V}$
- **$\mathbf{e}_v$** one-hot vector in $\mathbb{R}^{|\mathcal{V}|}$ for token $v \in \mathcal{V}$
- **$m$** mask token $m \notin \mathcal{V}$, represented as the one-hot vector $(0, \dots, 0, 1) \in \mathbb{R}^{|\mathcal{V}|+1}$
- **$\mathbf{x}^{\neq i}$** sub-sequence of $\mathbf{x}$ at all positions except $i$;
- **$p_0$** source distribution (mask or uniform)
- **$p_{\text{mask}}$** mask source distribution: $p_{\text{mask}}(v) = \mathbb{I}(v = m)$
- **$p_{\text{unif}}$** uniform source distribution: $p_{\text{unif}}(v) = \frac{1}{|\mathcal{V}|}$
- **$p_1$** target distribution
- **$p_t$** marginal distribution at time $t \in [0,1]$; the probability path representing the trajectory that transports $p_0$ to $p_1$
- **$p_t(\mathbf{x} | \mathbf{z}) = \prod_{j=1}^S q_t(x^j | z^j)$** factorized conditional probability path given a conditioning variable $z$
- **$q_t(x | z)$** token-wise conditional distribution given $z$.
- **$q_t(x | x_0, x_1)$** source-target token-wise conditional distribution: $q_t(x | x_0, x_1) = \kappa_t \mathbb{I}(x = x_1) + (1 - \kappa_t) \mathbb{I}(x = x_0)$
- **$\kappa_t$** interpolation schedule; boundary conditions are $\kappa_0 = 0$ and $\kappa_1 = 1$, with a natural choice being $\kappa_t = t$
- **$u_t(\mathbf{x})_{\mathbf{y}}$** probability velocity: the instantaneous rate at which the sequence transitions from $\mathbf{x}$ to $\mathbf{y}$ at time $t$
- **$u_t^i(\mathbf{x})_v$** token-wise velocity at position $i$: the instantaneous rate at which the $i$-th token of $\mathbf{x}$ transitions to $v$ at time $t$
- **$u_t(\mathbf{x} | \mathbf{z})_{\mathbf{y}}$** conditional velocity field given $z$; it relates to the marginal velocity via expectation, formulated as $u_t(\mathbf{x})_{\mathbf{y}} = \mathbb{E}_{\substack{\mathbf{z}}}[u_t(\mathbf{x}\mid\mathbf{z})_{\mathbf{y}} \mid X_t = \mathbf{x}] $
- **$u_t(x | x_0, x_1)$** source-target token-wise conditional velocity: $u_t(x | x_0, x_1)_v = \frac{\dot{\kappa}_t}{1 - \kappa_t} \left( \mathbb{I}(v = x_1) - \mathbb{I}(v = x) \right)$
- **$u_{\theta,t}^i(\mathbf{x})_v$** learned velocity field parameterized by $\theta$
- **$P_{\theta,t}(X_1^i | \mathbf{x}_t)$** learned denoiser: at coordinate j, conditional probability that the $i$-th token at time 1 is $\mathbf{e}_j$, given current sequence $\mathbf{x}_t$

[Discrete Flow Matching](#discrete-flow-matching) contains an introduction to Discrete Flow Matching, [Discrete Diffusion](#discrete-diffusion) an introduction to Discrete Diffusion. The reader already familiar with one or the other method can skip the associated section.

### Generating discrete data


Like their continuous analogs, discrete flow matching and diffusion aim at mapping a simple source distribution to a complex target distribution (the actual data we want to generate).
To do so, they create a sequence of intermediate distributions $(p_t)_{t}$ that progressively evolve from source to target distribution. <span style="color: red"> mention probability path or too soon/too complex? </span>



Here, we will deal with discrete data: the space on which the probability distributions are defined is $\mathcal{S} := \mathcal{V}^S$, namely the space of sequences of length $S \in \mathbb{N}$ over a given vocabulary $\mathcal{V}$.
A sequence $x$ in $\mathcal{S}$ is denoted as $\mathbf{x} = x^1 \dots x^S$, the $x^i$'s being called *tokens*.
In the vocabulary, we add a special token: the mask token, denoted as $m$.


The source distribution acts as our starting point for generation and is typically chosen to be either:
- The **mask distribution ($p_{\text{mask}} := \delta_{m\ldots m}$):** All sequences start entirely filled with the mask token $m$.
- The **uniform distribution ($p_{\text{unif}}$):** All tokens in the starting sequence are sampled uniformly across the vocabulary $\mathcal{V}$.

In this post, we will use the mask distribution ($p_{\text{mask}}$) as our running example; adaptations in the case of other distributions are available as toggleable content.

As a first example, consider generating Sudoku grids. The vocabulary is $\mathcal{V} = \{1, 2, \ldots, 9, m\}$, and the sequence length is 81, as there are 81 cases in a grid.
Discrete flow matching and diffusion start from a sample the source distribution, i.e. a fully masked grid here.
The model then iteratively fills this grid, step-by-step, finally reaching a sample from the target distribution (a valid, completed Sudoku board).

### Discrete Flow Matching

#### Setup: probability velocities

In this section we adopt the Flow Matching convention: time goes from $t=0$ to $t=1$, the source distribution is $p_0$, and the target distribution is $p_1$.

In Continuous Flow Matching, a central object is the *velocity field* $u: \mathbb{R}^d \times [0, 1] \to \mathbb{R}^d$, that governs the generating process: samples are obtained as solutions at time $t=1$ of the ODE
$$
\begin{equation}
\begin{cases}
x(0) = x_0 \\
\partial_t x(t) = u(x(t), t), \quad \forall t \in [0,1]
\end{cases}
\end{equation}
$$
and if $p_t$ is defined as the law of the solution $x(t)$ at time $t$ when $x_0 \sim p_0$, we say that $u$ generate $(p_t)$.

Solving an ODE when the state space is discrete is not possible.
Discrete FM relies on a different type of object, a *probability velocity*.

**Definition (Probability Velocity)**:
Let $u : \mathcal{S} \times [0, 1] \to \mathbb{R}^S$, and $(X_t)_{t\in[0,1]}$ be random variables of laws $(p_t)_{t \in [0, 1]}$.
Denote $u_t := u(\cdot, t)$.
We say that $u$ *generates* the probability path $(p_t)_{t \in [0, 1]}$ if
$$
\begin{equation}
\mathbb{P}(X_{t+h} = \mathbf{y} | X_t = \mathbf{x}) = \mathbb{I}(\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{y}) + h u_{t}(\mathbf{x})_{\mathbf{y}} + o(h)
\end{equation}
$$
If we index the state space as $\mathcal{S} = \{\mathbf{y}_1, \dots, \mathbf{y}_{S}\}$ and choose $k$ such that $\mathbf{y}_k = \mathbf{x}$ (the current state), then the full transition-probability vector reads
$$
\begin{equation}
\underbrace{\mathbb{P}(X_{t+h} = \cdot \mid X_t=\mathbf{x})}_{\in \mathbb{R}^{S}}  =
\begin{bmatrix}
  h u_t(\mathbf{x})_{\mathbf{y}_1} \\
  \vdots \\
  h u_t(\mathbf{x})_{\mathbf{y}_{k-1}} \\
  1 + h u_t(\mathbf{x})_{\mathbf{y}_k}  \\
  h u_t(\mathbf{x})_{\mathbf{y}_{k+1}} \\
  \vdots \\
  h u_t(\mathbf{x})_{\mathbf{y}_{S}}
\end{bmatrix}
+ o(h),
\end{equation}
$$

Intuitively, $u_t(\mathbf{x})_{\mathbf{y}}$ is the instantaneous rate at which the random variable $X_t$ transitions from state $\mathbf{x}$ to state $\mathbf{y}$ at time $t$.
Much like the ODE TODO cite ODE can be discretized with Euler's method, the sampling process TODO cite eq can be discretized as
$$
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{x}_{t + h} \sim \text{Cat}\left(\mathbf{e}_{\mathbf{x}_t} + h u_\tau(\mathbf{x}_t)\right)
\end{equation}
$$

The variable stays in state $\mathbf{x}$ with probability $h u_t(\mathbf{x})_{\mathbf{x}}$, or moves to any other state $\mathbf{y} \neq \mathbf{x}$ with probability $h u(\mathbf{x})_{\mathbf{y}}$.
We reach a fundamental point: unlike continuous FM, where sampling is deterministic once $x_0$ has been sampled from $p_0$, **sampling in discrete flow matching is inherently stochastic at each step**.

**Remark (Mass conservation for probability velocities).**
The probability velocity $u$ must preserve probability mass: for TODO cite eq to define a valid probability, for every $t\in [0, 1]$ $u_t$ must satisfy:
1. **Positivity:** $u_t(\mathbf{x})_{\mathbf{y}} \ge 0$ for all $\mathbf{y} \neq \mathbf{x}$.
2. **Normalization:** $\sum_{\mathbf{y} \in \mathcal{S}} u_t(\mathbf{x})_{\mathbf{y}} = 0$ for all $\mathbf{x} \in \mathcal{S}$

{/*
Hence, for each state $\mathbf{x}$,
$u_t(\mathbf{x})_{\mathbf{x}} = -\sum_{\mathbf{y} \neq \mathbf{x}} u_t(\mathbf{x})_{\mathbf{y}}$.

<div>
Positivity guarantees nonnegative off-diagonal transition probabilities:
$$
\begin{equation}
\mathbb{P}(X_{t+h}=\mathbf{y}\mid X_t=\mathbf{x}) = h\,u_t(\mathbf{x})_{\mathbf{y}} + o(h) \ge 0, \qquad \mathbf{y}\neq\mathbf{x}.
\end{equation}
$$
Normalization guarantees total mass conservation:
$$
\begin{equation}
\sum_{\mathbf{y}\in\mathcal{S}} \mathbb{P}(X_{t+h}=\mathbf{y}\mid X_t=\mathbf{x})
= 1 + h\sum_{\mathbf{y}\in\mathcal{S}}u_t(\mathbf{x})_{\mathbf{y}} + o(h) = 1 + o(h).
\end{equation}
$$

In Continuous Flow Matching, we have the guarantee that a velocity field $u_t$ generates a probability path $(p_t)$ if they satisfy the continuity equation $\partial_t p_t + \nabla \cdot (u_t p_t) = 0$.
In Discrete Flow Matching, we also have such a tool: the Kolmogorov Equation.

**Proposition (Kolmogorov Equation)**:
The probability velocity $u_t$ generates $(p_t)$ if and only if they satisfy the Kolmogorov equation, namely
$$
\begin{equation}
\forall \mathbf{y} \in \mathcal{S}, \quad
\frac{d}{dt} p_t(\mathbf{y}) = \sum_{\mathbf{x} \in \mathcal{S}} u_{t}(\mathbf{x})_{\mathbf{y}} \, p_t(\mathbf{x})
\end{equation}
$$

<details>
<summary>Proof</summary>
<div>
We can derive this directly by expanding the marginal probability at $t+h$ using the law of total probability and our velocity definition:

$$
\begin{align}
p_{t+h}(\mathbf{y}) &= \mathbb{P}(X_{t+h} = \mathbf{y}) \\
&= \sum_{\mathbf{x} \in \mathcal{S}} \mathbb{P}(X_{t+h} = \mathbf{y} | X_t = \mathbf{x})\, \mathbb{P}(X_t = \mathbf{x}) \\
&= \sum_{\mathbf{x} \in \mathcal{S}} \left(\mathbb{I}(\mathbf{y} = \mathbf{x}) + h\,u_t(\mathbf{x})_{\mathbf{y}} + o(h)\right) p_t(\mathbf{x}) \\
&= p_t(\mathbf{y}) + h \sum_{\mathbf{x} \in \mathcal{S}} u_t(\mathbf{x})_{\mathbf{y}}\, p_t(\mathbf{x}) + o(h)
\end{align}
$$

Rearranging the terms and taking the limit as $h \to 0$ gives:

$$
\begin{equation}
\lim_{h \to 0} \frac{p_{t+h}(\mathbf{y}) - p_t(\mathbf{y})}{h} = \sum_{\mathbf{x} \in \mathcal{S}} u_t(\mathbf{x})_{\mathbf{y}}\, p_t(\mathbf{x})
\end{equation}
$$

For the reverse implication (if the equation holds, then $u$ generates $p_t$), let $\tilde{p}_t$ denote the true marginal probability path generated by simulating the continuous-time Markov chain with the velocity field $u_t$, starting from the initial distribution $\tilde{p}_0 = p_0$. By the forward implication proven above, $\tilde{p}_t$ strictly satisfies the Kolmogorov forward equation:

$$
\begin{align}
\frac{d}{dt} \tilde{p}_t(\mathbf{y}) &= \sum_{\mathbf{x} \in \mathcal{S}} u_t(\mathbf{x})_{\mathbf{y}}\, \tilde{p}_t(\mathbf{x})
\end{align}
$$

By hypothesis, the given probability path $p_t$ also satisfies this exact same linear ordinary differential equation (ODE) system. Because both $p_t$ and $\tilde{p}_t$ are solutions to the same system of first-order linear ODEs and share the identical initial condition ($p_0 = \tilde{p}_0$), the Picard-Lindelöf uniqueness theorem for ordinary differential equations guarantees that $p_t(\mathbf{y}) = \tilde{p}_t(\mathbf{y})$ for all times $t$ and all states $\mathbf{y} \in \mathcal{S}$. Therefore, the vector field $u_t$ exactly generates the probability path $p_t$.

</div>

Now that we are equipped with adequate definitions of probability paths and velocities for the discrete case, together with a way to check that one is associated to the other, we can state the approach of Discrete Flow Matching, which is the same as Continuous Flow Matching:
1. Build an explicit probability path going from $p_0$ to $p_1$ <span style="color: red"> cite eq </span>

Learning probability velocities taking values in the full sequence space $\mathcal{S}=\mathcal{V}^S$ is cumbersome, as the cardinality of the latter is $|\mathcal{V}|^S$.
To make computations tractable, Discrete Flow Matching makes a **factorization assumption**: the probability path it builds updates each position independently.
as the next proposition shows, this has a fundamental consequence: the probability velocity that generates it can be written as a sum of token-wise contributions.

**Proposition (Factorization):**
Let $(X_t)$ be random variables of laws $p_t$; assume that the probability path $(p_t)$ is  generated by $u_t$.
In addition, assume that the transition probabilities factorize over tokens, namely for $\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{y} \in \mathcal{S}$:
$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathbb{P}(X_{t+h} = \mathbf{y} | X_t = \mathbf{x}) &= \mathbb{P}(X_{t+h}^1 = y_1, X_{t+h}^2 = y_2, \dots, X_{t+h}^S = y_S | X_t = \mathbf{x}) \\
&= \prod_{i=1}^S \mathbb{P}(X_{t+h}^i = y^i | X_t = \mathbf{x})
\end{aligned}
$$
Then, the probability velocity writes as a sum of $S$ *tokenwise* probability velocities $u_t^i$, taking values in $\mathcal{V}$:

$$
\begin{equation}
u_t(\mathbf{x})_{\mathbf{y}} = \sum_{i=1}^S \mathbb{I}(\mathbf{y}^{\neq i} = \mathbf{x}^{\neq i}) u_t^i(\mathbf{x})_{y^i}
\end{equation}
$$


<details>
<summary>Proof</summary>
<div>
By definition, the transition probability for a single token at position $i$ is:
$$
\mathbb{P}(X_{t+h}^i = y^i | X_t = \mathbf{x}) = \mathbb{I}(y^i = x^i) + h u_t^i(\mathbf{x})_{y^i} + o(h)
$$

Substituting this into our factorized joint probability gives:
$$
\mathbb{P}(X_{t+h} = \mathbf{y} | X_t = \mathbf{x}) = \prod_{i=1}^S \left[ \mathbb{I}(y^i = x^i) + h u_t^i(\mathbf{x})_{y^i} + o(h) \right]
$$

Expanding this product and keeping only the first-order terms in $h$, we get:
$$
\mathbb{P}(X_{t+h} = \mathbf{y} | X_t = \mathbf{x}) = \prod_{i=1}^S \mathbb{I}(y^i = x^i) + h \sum_{i=1}^S \left[ \mathbb{I}(\mathbf{y}^{\neq i} = \mathbf{x}^{\neq i}) u_t^i(\mathbf{x})_{y^i} \right] + o(h)
$$
where $\mathbb{I}(\mathbf{y}^{\neq i} = \mathbf{x}^{\neq i})$ equals 1 if $\mathbf{y}$ and $\mathbf{x}$ differ at most at position $i$.

Recognizing that $\prod_{i=1}^S \mathbb{I}(y^i = x^i) = \mathbb{I}(\mathbf{y} = \mathbf{x})$, we can rewrite the expansion as:
$$
\mathbb{P}(X_{t+h} = \mathbf{y} | X_t = \mathbf{x}) = \mathbb{I}(\mathbf{y} = \mathbf{x}) + h \sum_{i=1}^S \mathbb{I}(\mathbf{y}^{\neq i} = \mathbf{x}^{\neq i}) u_t^i(\mathbf{x})_{y^i} + o(h)
$$

By comparing this result with the fundamental definition of the probability velocity $\mathbb{P}(X_{t+h} = \mathbf{y} | X_t = \mathbf{x}) = \mathbb{I}(\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{y}) + h u_t(\mathbf{x})_{\mathbf{y}} + o(h)$, we directly identify:
$$
u_t(\mathbf{x})_{\mathbf{y}} = \sum_{i=1}^S \mathbb{I}(\mathbf{y}^{\neq i} = \mathbf{x}^{\neq i}) u_t^i(\mathbf{x})_{y^i}
$$
</div>
</details>

It follows that for infinitesimally small time steps, each sequence $\mathbf{x}$ is assumed to transition only to states where at most one token differs.
It yields a substantial computational gain: instead of learning one transition object on the full sequence space $\mathcal{S}=\mathcal{V}^S$ (exponential in $S$), we learn $S$ local transition objects, each on the vocabulary space $\mathcal{V}$.

This modelling step implies that at each sampling step, we draw each token independently following:
$$
\begin{equation}
x_{\tau+h}^i \sim \text{Cat}\left(\mathbf{e}_{x_\tau^i} + h u_\tau^i(\mathbf{x}_\tau)\right)
\end{equation}
$$

We are now ready to build the probability path of Discrete Flow Matching!

#### Building the probability path

Similarly to continuous flow matching, there are infinitely many possible choices for the probability path $(p_t)$.
To build one, we borrow the core trick from Conditional Flow Matching (CFM): we first define **conditional** probability paths for which the exact conditional velocity is known.
Then, we show that the resulting global velocity (obtained by marginalizing the conditional velocities) is guaranteed to generate a probability path that transports $p_0$ to $p_1$. All that's left is to learn it as a simple conditional expectation from data, which neural networks know how to do!

The conditioning variable $\mathbf{z} \in \mathcal{Z}^S$ can be either:
* **A target sample:** $\mathbf{z} = \mathbf{x}_1$ (useful when the source $p_0$ is a known, simple distribution like uniform or mask).
* **A source-target pair:** $\mathbf{z} = (\mathbf{x}_0, \mathbf{x}_1)$ (useful when transforming between two arbitrary datasets).

For illustration, we will use $\mathbf{z} = \mathbf{x}_1$.
Having chosen $\mathbf{z}$, we chose a conditional probability path.
In order to have factorized velocities (simpler to learn) as per <span style="color:red"> cite Factorization proposition</span>, we pick the conditional path to be factorized:
$$
\begin{align}
& p_t(\mathbf{x} | \mathbf{z}) = \prod_{j=1}^S q_t(x^j | z^j) \\
&\text{with} \quad
q_t(x^i | x_1^i) = t \mathbb{I}(x^i = x_1^i) + (1 - t) \mathbb{I}(x^i = m)
\end{align}
$$
This means that at time $t$, the $i$-th token $x^i_t$ equals the target token $x_1^i$ with probability $t$, or it remains the mask token $m$ with probability $1 - t$.
The interest of this conditional probability path is that we can derive the associated probability velocity.

**Proposition (Conditional velocity)**:
The conditional velocity driving the transition <span style="color:red"> XXX eqref </span> is:
$$
\begin{equation}
u_t(x^i | x_1^i)_v = \begin{cases}
\frac{1}{1-t} & \text{if } v = x_1^i \text{ and } x^i = m, \\[0.3cm]
-\frac{1}{1-t} & \text{if } v = x^i = m, \\[0.3cm]
0 & \text{otherwise}.
\end{cases}
\end{equation}
$$

Remember that $u$ is a rate of change of probability: $u_t(x^i | x_1^i)_v$ quantifies the instantaneous probability flow from the current token $x^i$ toward token $v$.
In the masked-source setting, the flow progressively removes mass from $m$ and transfers it to the target token $x_1^i$.
Concretely, if the vocabulary has  3 tokens $\{1, 2, 3\}$ and a mask token $m$, and if the conditioning target is $x_1^i = 2$:

$$
u_t(x^i | x_1^i = 2)_v = \begin{array}{c|cccc}
\begin{matrix} x^i  \\[-2pt] \diagdown \\[-2pt] v \end{matrix} & 1 & 2 & 3 & m \\
\hline
1 & 0 & 0 & 0 & 0 \\[0.3cm]
2 & 0 & 0 & 0 & \dfrac{1}{1-t} \\[0.3cm]
3 & 0 & 0 & 0 & 0 \\[0.3cm]
m & 0 & 0 & 0 & -\dfrac{1}{1-t}
\end{array}
$$

$$
\begin{equation}
\frac{d}{dt} q_t(y^{i} \mid x_1^{i}) = \sum_{v \in \mathcal{V} \cup \{m\}} u_{t}(v \mid x_1^{i})_{y^{i}} \, q_t(v \mid x_1^{i})
\end{equation}
$$

Let's expand the continuous-time derivative of the probability path using $q_t(y^i \mid x_1^i) = t \mathbb{I}(y^i = x_1^i) + (1 - t) \mathbb{I}(y^i = m)$:
$$
\begin{equation}
\frac{d}{dt} q_t(y^{i} \mid x_1^{i}) = \mathbb{I}(y^{i} = x_1^{i}) - \mathbb{I}(y^{i} = m)
\end{equation}
$$

Now, let's examine the right-hand side using the analytic formulation of the velocity field $u_{t}(v \mid x_1^{i})_{y^{i}} = \frac{1}{1 - t} \left( \mathbb{I}(y^{i} = x_1^{i}) - \mathbb{I}(y^{i} = v) \right) \mathbb{I}(v = m)$:
$$
\begin{align}
&\sum_{v \in \mathcal{V} \cup \{m\}} u_t(v \mid x_1^i)_{y^i} \, q_t(v \mid x_1^{i}) \\
&= \sum_{v \in \mathcal{V} \cup \{m\}} \frac{1}{1 - t} \left( \mathbb{I}(y^{i} = x_1^{i}) - \mathbb{I}(y^{i} = v) \right) \mathbb{I}(v = m) \, q_t(v \mid x_1^{i}) \\
&= \frac{1}{1 - t} \left( \mathbb{I}(y^{i} = x_1^{i}) - \mathbb{I}(y^{i} = m) \right) q_t(m \mid x_1^{i})
\end{align}
$$

Substituting $q_t(m \mid x_1^{i}) = (1-t)$:
$$
\begin{aligned}
\sum_{v \in \mathcal{V} \cup \{m\}} u_t(v \mid x_1^i)_{y^i} \, q_t(v \mid x_1^{i}) &= \frac{1}{1-t} \left( \mathbb{I}(y^{i} = x_1^{i}) - \mathbb{I}(y^{i} = m) \right) (1-t) \\
&= \mathbb{I}(y^{i} = x_1^{i}) - \mathbb{I}(y^{i} = m)
\end{aligned}
\begin{equation} \end{equation}
$$

This matches the initial continuous-time derivative perfectly, confirming that our specified velocity exacts generates the assumed conditional probability path!

</div>
</details>

**Source-Target Conditioning**

Here, we condition on both the start and end points, so $z^i = (x_0^i, x_1^i)$. The token $x^i$ at time $t$ is simply $x_1^i$ with probability $t$, and $x_0^i$ with probability $1 - t$:
$$
\begin{equation}
q_t(x^i | x_0^i, x_1^i) = t \mathbb{I}(x^i = x_1^i) + (1 - t) \mathbb{I}(x^i = x_0^i)
\end{equation}
$$

The conditional velocity field driving this transition is:
$$
\begin{equation}
u_t(x^i | x_0^i, x_1^i)_v = \begin{cases}
\dfrac{1}{1 - t} & \text{if } v = x_1^i \text{ and } x^i = x_0^i, \\[0.3cm]
-\dfrac{1}{1 - t} & \text{if } v = x^i = x_0^i, \\[0.3cm]
0 & \text{otherwise}.
\end{cases}
\end{equation}
$$

In the source-target setting, the flow moves mass directly from the source token $x_0^i$ toward the target token $x_1^i$, with no mask involved. All rows except the source row are identically zero — once a token is already at a state other than $x_0^i$, no transition occurs. Concretely, if the vocabulary has 3 tokens $\{1, 2, 3\}$, and $x_0^i = 1$, $x_1^i = 2$:

$$
u_t(x^i | x_0^i = 1,\, x_1^i = 2)_v = \begin{array}{c|ccc}
\begin{matrix} x^i \\[-2pt] \diagdown \\[-2pt] v \end{matrix} & 1 & 2 & 3 \\
\hline
1 & -\dfrac{1}{1-t} & \dfrac{1}{1-t} & 0 \\[0.3cm]
2 & 0 & 0 & 0 \\[0.3cm]
3 & 0 & 0 & 0
\end{array}
$$

**Uniform Source**

When $p_0$ is the uniform distribution, we can only condition on the target $z^i = x_1^i$. At time $t$, the token is the target $x_1^i$ with probability $t + (1 - t) \frac{1}{|\mathcal{V}|}$, or is drawn uniformly from other tokens in the vocabulary $\mathcal{V}$ with probability $(1 - t) \frac{1}{|\mathcal{V}|}$:
$$
\begin{equation}
q_t(x^i | x_1^i) = t \mathbb{I}(x^i = x_1^i) + (1 - t) \frac{1}{|\mathcal{V}|}
\end{equation}
$$

The conditional velocity field driving this transition is:
$$
\begin{equation}
u_t(x^i | x_1^i)_v = \begin{cases}
\dfrac{1}{1-t} & \text{if } v = x_1^i \text{ and } x^i \neq x_1^i \\[0.3cm]
-\dfrac{1}{1-t} & \text{if } v = x^i \neq x_1^i \\[0.3cm]
0 & \text{otherwise}
\end{cases}
\end{equation}
$$

Unlike the mask source, every non-target token can transition toward $x_1^i$, since the uniform source places initial mass everywhere. Concretely, if the vocabulary has 3 tokens $\{1, 2, 3\}$ and the conditioning target is $x_1^i = 2$:

$$
u_t(x^i | x_1^i = 2)_v = \begin{array}{c|ccc}
\begin{matrix} x^i \\[-2pt] \diagdown \\[-2pt] v \end{matrix} & 1 & 2 & 3 \\
\hline
1 & -\dfrac{1}{1-t} & \dfrac{1}{1-t} & 0 \\[0.3cm]
2 & 0 & 0 & 0 \\[0.3cm]
3 & 0 & \dfrac{1}{1-t} & -\dfrac{1}{1-t}
\end{array}
$$

#### From conditional to unconditional velocity

Now that we have defined our conditional probability paths and velocities, we need to connect them back to the global, unconditional process.

The overall marginal distribution $p_t$ is obtained simply by marginalizing over all possible conditional paths:
$$
\begin{equation}
p_t(\mathbf{x}) = \sum_{\mathbf{z}} p_t(\mathbf{x} | \mathbf{z}) \pi(\mathbf{z})
\end{equation}
$$
where $\pi(\mathbf{z})$ is the distribution over the conditioning variable $\mathbf{z}$ used during training.
{/* Maybe =choice to be consistent*/}.

We can show that this is a valid probability path from $p_0$ to $p_1$.

<details>
<summary>Proof</summary>
<div>

We verify the two boundary conditions separately:

**At $t=0$**
Recall the conditional path with $\mathbf{z} = \mathbf{x}_1$ and mask source:
$$
q_0(x^i \mid x_1^i) = (1-0)\,\mathbb{I}(x^i = m) = \mathbb{I}(x^i = m).
$$
The value of $x_1^i$ plays no role, so $p_0(\mathbf{x} \mid \mathbf{z})$ does not depend on $\mathbf{z}$:
$$
p_0(\mathbf{x} \mid \mathbf{z}) = \prod_{j=1}^S \mathbb{I}(x^j = m) = p_{\mathrm{mask}}(\mathbf{x}) = p_0(\mathbf{x}).
$$
Marginalizing over $\mathbf{z}$ then trivially gives:
$$
p_{t=0}(\mathbf{x}) = \sum_{\mathbf{z}} p_0(\mathbf{x} \mid \mathbf{z})\,\pi(\mathbf{z}) = p_0(\mathbf{x})\sum_{\mathbf{z}} \pi(\mathbf{z}) = p_0(\mathbf{x}).
$$

**At $t=1$**
We have:
$$
q_1(x^i \mid x_1^i) = \mathbb{I}(x^i = x_1^i)
\implies
p_1(\mathbf{x} \mid \mathbf{z} = \mathbf{x}_1) = \prod_{j=1}^S \mathbb{I}(x^j = x_1^j) = \delta_{\mathbf{x}_1}(\mathbf{x}).
$$
Since $\pi(\mathbf{z}) = p_{\mathrm{data}}(\mathbf{x}_1)$,
the delta collapses the sum directly:
$$
p_{t=1}(\mathbf{x})
= \sum_{\mathbf{x}_1} \delta_{\mathbf{x}_1}(\mathbf{x})\, p_{\mathrm{data}}(\mathbf{x}_1)
= p_{\mathrm{data}}(\mathbf{x})
= p_1(\mathbf{x}).
$$

</div>
</details>

Just as the marginal probability is an average of conditional probabilities, the marginal velocity field $u_t(\mathbf{x})_{\mathbf{y}}$ can be expressed as the expectation of the conditional velocity over the conditioning variable $\mathbf{z}$:
$$
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
u_t(\mathbf{x})_{\mathbf{y}}
&= \mathbb{E}_{\substack{\mathbf{z}}}\!\left[u_t(\mathbf{x}\mid\mathbf{z})_{\mathbf{y}} \mid X_t = \mathbf{x}\right] \\
&= \sum_{\mathbf{z}} u_t(\mathbf{x}\mid\mathbf{z})_{\mathbf{y}}\,\mathbb{P}(Z = \mathbf{z} \mid X_t = \mathbf{x}).
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
$$

**Theorem.** If we define
$$
u_t(\mathbf{x})_{\mathbf{y}} := \mathbb{E}_{\substack{\mathbf{z}}}\!\left[u_t(\mathbf{x}\mid\mathbf{z})_{\mathbf{y}} \mid X_t = \mathbf{x}\right],
$$
then this velocity field generates the induced probability path
$$
p_t(\mathbf{x}) = \sum_{\mathbf{z}} p_t(\mathbf{x}\mid\mathbf{z})\,\pi(\mathbf{z}).
$$

<details>
<summary>Proof</summary>
<div>
From the definition of probability velocity,
$$
\begin{equation}
\mathbb{P}(X_{t+h}=\mathbf{y}\mid X_t=\mathbf{x})
= \mathbb{I}(\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{y}) + h\,u_t(\mathbf{x})_{\mathbf{y}} + o(h).
\end{equation}
$$
Conditioning on $Z$ and using total probability,
$$
\begin{align*}
\mathbb{P}(X_{t+h}=\mathbf{y}\mid X_t=\mathbf{x})
&= \sum_{\mathbf{z}} \mathbb{P}(X_{t+h}=\mathbf{y}, Z=\mathbf{z}\mid X_t=\mathbf{x}) \\
&= \sum_{\mathbf{z}} \mathbb{P}(X_{t+h}=\mathbf{y}\mid X_t=\mathbf{x}, Z=\mathbf{z})\,\mathbb{P}(Z=\mathbf{z}\mid X_t=\mathbf{x}) \\
&= \sum_{\mathbf{z}}\Big(\mathbb{I}(\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{y}) + h\,u_t(\mathbf{x}\mid\mathbf{z})_{\mathbf{y}} + o(h)\Big)\,\mathbb{P}(Z=\mathbf{z}\mid X_t=\mathbf{x}) \\
&= \mathbb{I}(\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{y}) + h\sum_{\mathbf{z}}u_t(\mathbf{x}\mid\mathbf{z})_{\mathbf{y}}\,\mathbb{P}(Z=\mathbf{z}\mid X_t=\mathbf{x}) + o(h).
\end{align*}
\begin{equation} \end{equation}
$$
Hence,
$$
\begin{equation*}
u_t(\mathbf{x})_{\mathbf{y}} = \sum_{\mathbf{z}}u_t(\mathbf{x}\mid\mathbf{z})_{\mathbf{y}}\,\mathbb{P}(Z=\mathbf{z}\mid X_t=\mathbf{x}) = \mathbb{E}_{\substack{\mathbf{z}}}\!\left[u_t(\mathbf{x}\mid\mathbf{z})_{\mathbf{y}} \mid X_t=\mathbf{x}\right].
\end{equation*}
\begin{equation} \end{equation}
$$
Now apply the Kolmogorov equation to each conditional process and average over $\mathbf{z}$:
$$
\begin{align}
\frac{d}{dt}p_t(\mathbf{y})
&= \sum_{\mathbf{z}} \pi(\mathbf{z})\,\frac{d}{dt}p_t(\mathbf{y}\mid\mathbf{z}) \\
&= \sum_{\mathbf{z}}\pi(\mathbf{z})\sum_{\mathbf{x}}u_t(\mathbf{x}\mid\mathbf{z})_{\mathbf{y}}\,p_t(\mathbf{x}\mid\mathbf{z}) \\
&= \sum_{\mathbf{x}}\left(\sum_{\mathbf{z}}u_t(\mathbf{x}\mid\mathbf{z})_{\mathbf{y}}\,\mathbb{P}(Z=\mathbf{z}\mid X_t=\mathbf{x})\right)p_t(\mathbf{x}) \\
&= \sum_{\mathbf{x}} u_t(\mathbf{x})_{\mathbf{y}}\,p_t(\mathbf{x}).
\end{align}
$$
So $u_t$ satisfies the Kolmogorov equation for $p_t$ and therefore generates $p_t$.
</div>
</details>

This is the exact same logic as in continuous FM: once the target field is identified as a
conditional expectation, learning reduces to a regression problem. In continuous spaces, this is typically introduced via the $L^2$ projection view, the marginal velocity is the unique minimizer:
$$
\begin{equation}
u_t(\mathbf{x}) = \underset{v}{\operatorname{argmin}}\ \mathbb{E}\!\left[\left\|u_t(\mathbf{x} \mid \mathbf{z}) - v\right\|^2 \,\Big|\, X_t = \mathbf{x}\right]
\end{equation}
$$
In discrete spaces, since we compare nonneg­ative rate vectors that behave like local probabilities, a KL-style objective is more natural.
More generally, this conditional expectation is the minimizer
for any Bregman divergence, and KL is the Bregman choice adapted to our setting:
$$
\begin{equation}
u_t(\mathbf{x}) = \underset{v}{\operatorname{argmin}}\ \mathbb{E}\!\left[\mathrm{KL}\left(u_t(\mathbf{x} \mid \mathbf{z}),\, v\right) \,\Big|\, X_t = \mathbf{x}\right]
\end{equation}
$$

**Learning with Conditional Velocity Fields**

To train our model, we use the KL divergence to measure the difference between velocity fields. This choice is natural: it arises from the KL divergence between the probability paths generated by the true and learned velocity fields. The ideal discrete flow matching loss minimizes the KL between the true marginal velocity $u_t$ and our learned velocity $u_{\theta,t}$:

$$
\begin{equation}
L_{\text{DFM}}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{\substack{t \sim \mathcal{U}(0,1) \\ \mathbf{x} \sim p_t}}\left[ \mathrm{KL}\left(u_t(\mathbf{x}), u_{\theta,t}(\mathbf{x})\right) \right]
\end{equation}
$$

However, $u_t(\mathbf{x})$ requires marginalizing over the entire dataset, making it computationally intractable. Following the approach of continuous flow matching, we instead use the **conditional discrete flow matching loss**:

$$
\begin{equation}
L_{\text{CDFM}}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{\substack{t \sim \mathcal{U}(0,1) \\ \mathbf{z} \sim \pi \\ \mathbf{x} \sim p_t( \cdot | \mathbf{z})}}\left[ \mathrm{KL}\left(u_t(\mathbf{x} | \mathbf{z}), u_{\theta,t}(\mathbf{x})\right) \right]
\end{equation}
$$

**Theorem.** There exists a constant $C$ independent of $\theta$ such that
$$
L_{\text{CDFM}}(\theta) = L_{\text{DFM}}(\theta) + C.
$$
In particular, both objectives have the same minimizers.

In practice, thanks to our factorization assumption, the loss decomposes as a sum over individual token positions:

$$
\begin{equation}
L_{\text{CDFM}}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{\substack{t \sim \mathcal{U}(0,1) \\ \mathbf{z} \sim \pi \\ \mathbf{x} \sim p_t( \cdot | \mathbf{z})}}\left[ \sum_{i=1}^S \mathrm{KL}\left( u_t(x^i | z^i), u_{\theta,t}^i(\mathbf{x}) \right) \right]
\end{equation}
$$

#### Denoiser

In discrete spaces, directly predicting a continuous vector field or instantaneous transition rates can feel unintuitive. Instead, a much more natural object to learn is a denoiser.

A denoiser simply observes the current noisy, corrupted, or partially masked sequence $\mathbf{x}_t$ at time $t$ and attempts to predict the true, clean target token $X_1^i$ for each position $i$. We denote this learned neural network distribution as $P_{\theta,t}(X_1^i \mid X_t = \mathbf{x})$, also noted as $D_{\theta,t}^i(\mathbf{x})$.

We can directly connect the denoiser and velocity through this simple formula:

$$
\begin{equation}
u_t^i(\mathbf{x})_v = \frac{1}{1 - t} \left( \mathbb{P}(X_1^i = v | X_t = \mathbf{x}) - \mathbb{I}(v = x^i) \right)
\end{equation}
$$

<details>
<summary>Proof</summary>
<div>

In a general configuration, the marginal velocity is the conditional expectation of the conditional velocity:
$$
\begin{equation}
u_t^i(\mathbf{x})_v = \mathbb{E}_{x_1^i \sim \mathbb{P}(X_1^i \mid \mathbf{x})}\!\left[u_t^i(x^i \mid x_1^i)_v\right].
\end{equation}
$$
We now evaluate this expectation explicitly using the conditional velocity for the mask source
<span style="color: red">cite eq</span>:
$$
\begin{equation}
u_t^i(x^i \mid x_1^i)_v = \frac{1}{1-t}\,\mathbb{I}(x^i = m)\left(\mathbb{I}(v = x_1^i) - \mathbb{I}(v = m)\right).
\end{equation}
$$

**Case 1: $x^i \neq m$ (token already unmasked).**
The indicator $\mathbb{I}(x^i = m) = 0$, so $u_t^i(x^i \mid x_1^i)_v = 0$ for all $x_1^i$.
The expectation vanishes, and indeed the target formula also gives zero since
$\mathbb{P}(X_1^i = v \mid X_t = \mathbf{x}) = \mathbb{I}(v = x^i)$ (the clean token is already revealed).

**Case 2: $x^i = m$ (token is masked).**
Taking the expectation over $x_1^i$:
$$
\begin{align}
u_t^i(\mathbf{x})_v
&= \mathbb{E}_{x_1^i}\!\left[\frac{1}{1-t}\left(\mathbb{I}(v = x_1^i) - \mathbb{I}(v = m)\right)\right] \\
&= \frac{1}{1-t}\left(\mathbb{P}(X_1^i = v \mid X_t = \mathbf{x}) - \mathbb{I}(v = m)\right) \\
&= \frac{1}{1-t}\left(\mathbb{P}(X_1^i = v \mid X_t = \mathbf{x}) - \mathbb{I}(v = x^i)\right),
\end{align}
$$
where the last equality uses $x^i = m$. Both cases match the claimed formula.

</div>
</details>

Because the term $\mathbb{I}(v = x^i)$ is a known constant given the current state, learning the velocity field $u$ is mathematically exactly equivalent to learning the denoiser probability $\mathbb{P}(X_1^i = v | X_t = \mathbf{x})$.

Consequently, we can train the denoiser directly. Using the KL divergence between the true clean token distribution and the learned one, the loss simplifies to the standard cross-entropy:

$$
\begin{equation}
L_{\text{CDM}}(\theta) = -\mathbb{E}_{\substack{t \sim \mathcal{U}(0,1) \\ \mathbf{x}_1 \sim p_{\text{data}} \\ \mathbf{x} \sim p_t( \cdot | \mathbf{x}_1)}} \left[ \sum_{i=1}^S \log P_{\theta,t}(X_1^i = x_1^i \mid X_t = \mathbf{x}) \right]
\end{equation}
$$

In the specific context of masked diffusion, we usually restrict the learning process to predict only the tokens that are currently masked. Consequently, the loss is evaluated exclusively on these masked positions and scaled by the appropriate transition rate, becoming:
$$
\begin{equation}
L_{\text{CDM}}(\theta) = -\mathbb{E}_{\substack{t \sim \mathcal{U}(0,1) \\ \mathbf{x}_1 \sim p_{\text{data}} \\ \mathbf{x} \sim p_t( \cdot | \mathbf{x}_1)}} \left[ \sum_{i : x_t^i = m} \frac{1}{1-t} \log P_{\theta,t}(X_1^i = x_1^i \mid X_t = \mathbf{x}) \right]
\end{equation}
$$

### Discrete Diffusion

#### Forward and Reverse Diffusion Processes

We first consider a sentence where we progressively replace each word with a special mask token, transforming the sequence $\mathbf{x}$ into a sequence of masks over time. This forward process is applied independently to each token. Thus, for $s < t$, we can compute the transition probability from time $s$ to $t$ and define the conditional transition matrix $\bar{Q}_{t|s}$ satisfying $q(x_t | x_s) = \text{Cat}(x_t; \ \bar{Q}_{t|s}^\top x_s)$. In matrix form, this is explicitly:
$$
\begin{equation}
\bar{Q}_{t|s} =
\begin{pmatrix}
\frac{\alpha_t}{\alpha_s} & 0 & \dots & 0 & 1 - \frac{\alpha_t}{\alpha_s} \\
0 & \frac{\alpha_t}{\alpha_s} & \dots & 0 & 1 - \frac{\alpha_t}{\alpha_s} \\
\vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots & \vdots \\
0 & 0 & \dots & \frac{\alpha_t}{\alpha_s} & 1 - \frac{\alpha_t}{\alpha_s} \\
0 & 0 & \dots & 0 & 1
\end{pmatrix}
= \frac{\alpha_t}{\alpha_s} I + \left(1 - \frac{\alpha_t}{\alpha_s}\right)\mathbf{1}\mathbf{e}_m^\top
\end{equation}
$$
where $\alpha_t$ is the probability of a token remaining unmasked at time $t$.
Interpretation: if the token at time $s$ is $x_s$, then for $t > s$.:
$$
\begin{equation}
x_t = \begin{cases}
x_s, & \text{with probability } \frac{\alpha_t}{\alpha_s} \\
m, & \text{with probability } 1 - \frac{\alpha_t}{\alpha_s}
\end{cases}
\end{equation}
$$

<details>
<summary>Construction of $\bar{Q}_{t|s}$</summary>
<div>

Let $j \in \{1, 2, \dots, T\}$ be an integer, and let $t \in \{0, \frac{1}{T}, \frac{2}{T}, \dots, 1\}$ denote the discrete time steps of the diffusion process.

We can define the single-step transition matrix $Q_{t}$ (where $t=j/T$) acting on the state space. Since the transition logic is identical for all non-mask tokens, the matrix has a specific structure. Let $\beta_j \in [0, 1]$ be the probability of transitioning to the mask token at time step $j/T$. The transition $x_{(j-1)/T} \to x_{j/T}$ is defined by:
$$
\begin{align}
x_{j/T} &= \begin{cases}
x_{(j-1)/T} & \text{with probability } 1 - \beta_j \\
m & \text{with probability } \beta_j
\end{cases}
\end{align}
$$

We can represent this transition distribution using a Categorical distribution parameterized by a transition matrix $Q_j \in \mathbb{R}^{K \times K}$:
$$
\begin{equation}
q(x_{j/T} | x_{(j-1)/T}) = \text{Cat}(x_{j/T}; \ Q_j^\top x_{(j-1)/T})
\end{equation}
$$
where $x$ vectors are treated as column vectors. The matrix $Q_j$ is given by:
$$
\begin{equation}
Q_j =
\begin{pmatrix}
1-\beta_j & 0 & \dots & 0 & \beta_j \\
0 & 1-\beta_j & \dots & 0 & \beta_j \\
\vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots & \vdots \\
0 & 0 & \dots & 1-\beta_j & \beta_j \\
0 & 0 & \dots & 0 & 1
\end{pmatrix} \in \mathbb{R}^{K \times K}
\end{equation}
$$
Here, the last row corresponds to the mask token $m$, which is an absorbing state (it stays $m$ with probability 1). The first $|\mathcal{V}|$ rows correspond to valid tokens.

We consider the continuous-time limit of this process, where the time steps become infinitesimally small and the transition probabilities are defined by a rate function $\beta(t)$, in order to avoid having a model that only learns to noise and denoise at specific discrete time steps.

The probability that a token remains equal to its initial value $x_0$ at time $t$ can be computed as:
$$
\begin{align}
\mathbb{P}(x_t = x_0) &= \mathbb{P}\left(x_t = x_0 | x_{t-\frac{1}{T}} = x_0\right) \dots \mathbb{P}\left(x_{\frac{1}{T}} = x_0 | x_0 = x_0\right) \\
&= \prod_{i=1}^{tT} (1 - \beta_i) \\
&= \exp\left(\sum_{i=1}^{tT} \log(1 - \beta_i)\right) \\
&\approx \exp\left(-\sum_{i=1}^{tT} \beta_i\right) \quad \text{(for small $\beta_i$)} \\
&\xrightarrow[T \to \infty]{} \exp\left(-\int_0^t \beta(s) \, ds\right)
\end{align}
$$
We note $\alpha_t = \exp\left(-\int_0^t \beta(s) \, ds\right)$ as the probability of a token remaining unmasked at time $t$.

We can define the cumulative transition matrix (or forward marginal) $\bar{Q}_t$ such that:
$$
\begin{equation}
q(x_t | x_0) = \text{Cat}(x_t; \ \bar{Q}_t^\top x_0)
\end{equation}
$$
Since the mask state is absorbing, this matrix essentially summarizes the cumulative probability of not being masked yet. We can write it compactly using the product of the all-ones vector $\mathbf{1}$ and the one-hot mask vector $\mathbf{e}_m$:
$$
\begin{equation}
\bar{Q}_t = \alpha_t I + (1-\alpha_t)\mathbf{1}\mathbf{e}_m^\top \in \mathbb{R}^{K \times K}
\end{equation}
$$
where $I$ is the identity matrix and $\mathbf{1}\mathbf{e}_m^\top$ is a matrix with ones in the $m$-th column and zeros elsewhere.

Now, we want to calculate the transition probability from an intermediate time $s$ to a later time $t$ (where $s < t$). We rely on the Markov property of the diffusion process. This property dictates that the forward marginal transition matrix $\bar{Q}_t$ can be decomposed as the product of the transitions from $0$ to $s$ and from $s$ to $t$:
$$
\begin{equation}
\bar{Q}_t = \bar{Q}_s \bar{Q}_{t|s}
\end{equation}
$$

We want to find the conditional transition matrix $\bar{Q}_{t|s}$ that satisfies this equation. We hypothesize that $\bar{Q}_{t|s}$ has the same structural form as the marginals, parameterized by some survival probability $\gamma \in [0, 1]$:
$$
\begin{equation}
\bar{Q}_{t|s} = \gamma I + (1 - \gamma)\mathbf{1}\mathbf{e}_m^\top
\end{equation}
$$

Substituting the known forms of $\bar{Q}_t$ and $\bar{Q}_s$ into the Markov equation gives:
$$
\begin{equation}
\alpha_t I + (1-\alpha_t)\mathbf{1}\mathbf{e}_m^\top = \left(\alpha_s I + (1-\alpha_s)\mathbf{1}\mathbf{e}_m^\top\right) \left(\gamma I + (1-\gamma)\mathbf{1}\mathbf{e}_m^\top\right)
\end{equation}
$$

Expanding the right side, and using the fact that $\mathbf{e}_m^\top \mathbf{1} = 1$ (since the dot product of a one-hot vector and an all-ones vector is the scalar 1), we obtain:
$$
\begin{aligned}
\bar{Q}_s \bar{Q}_{t|s} &= \alpha_s \gamma I + \alpha_s (1-\gamma)\mathbf{1}\mathbf{e}_m^\top + \gamma (1-\alpha_s)\mathbf{1}\mathbf{e}_m^\top + (1-\alpha_s)(1-\gamma)\mathbf{1}(\mathbf{e}_m^\top \mathbf{1})\mathbf{e}_m^\top
\end{aligned}
\begin{equation} \end{equation}
$$
$$
\begin{align}
&= \alpha_s \gamma I + \left[ \alpha_s(1-\gamma) + \gamma(1-\alpha_s) + (1-\alpha_s)(1-\gamma) \right]\mathbf{1}\mathbf{e}_m^\top \\
&= \alpha_s \gamma I + (1 - \alpha_s \gamma)\mathbf{1}\mathbf{e}_m^\top
\end{align}
$$

By matching the coefficients with our target $\bar{Q}_t = \alpha_t I + (1-\alpha_t)\mathbf{1}\mathbf{e}_m^\top$, we find the required value for $\gamma$:
$$
\begin{equation}
\alpha_s \gamma = \alpha_t \implies \gamma = \frac{\alpha_t}{\alpha_s}
\end{equation}
$$

This derivation mathematically proves that the probability of a token surviving unmasked from time $s$ to $t$, given that it was unmasked at time $s$, is exactly $\frac{\alpha_t}{\alpha_s}$. Plugging $\gamma$ back into our matrix formulation yields:
$$
\begin{equation}
\bar{Q}_{t|s} = \frac{\alpha_t}{\alpha_s} I + \left(1 - \frac{\alpha_t}{\alpha_s}\right)\mathbf{1}\mathbf{e}_m^\top
\end{equation}
$$

Explicitly, the matrix is written as:
$$
\begin{equation}
\bar{Q}_{t|s} =
\begin{pmatrix}
\frac{\alpha_t}{\alpha_s} & 0 & \dots & 0 & 1 - \frac{\alpha_t}{\alpha_s} \\
0 & \frac{\alpha_t}{\alpha_s} & \dots & 0 & 1 - \frac{\alpha_t}{\alpha_s} \\
\vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots & \vdots \\
0 & 0 & \dots & \frac{\alpha_t}{\alpha_s} & 1 - \frac{\alpha_t}{\alpha_s} \\
0 & 0 & \dots & 0 & 1
\end{pmatrix}
\end{equation}
$$

</details>

By taking the first-order Taylor expansion for a small time step $\Delta t$, we define the forward rate matrix $\dot{Q}_t$:
$$
\begin{equation}
\dot{Q}_t = \sigma(t)
\begin{pmatrix}
-1 & 0 & \dots & 0 & 1 \\
0 & -1 & \dots & 0 & 1 \\
\vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots & \vdots \\
0 & 0 & \dots & -1 & 1 \\
0 & 0 & \dots & 0 & 0
\end{pmatrix}
= \sigma(t) (\mathbf{1}\mathbf{e}_m^\top - I)
\end{equation}
$$
where $\sigma(t) = -\frac{\dot{\alpha}_t}{\alpha_t} > 0$ represents the instantaneous masking rate.

To generate data, we define a reverse process that progressively unmasks tokens. The probability of reverting to the clean token $x_0$ yields a reverse matrix $R_{s|t}^{x_0}$:
$$
\begin{equation}
R_{s|t}^{x_0} =
\begin{pmatrix}
 & & & 0 \\
 & I_{|\mathcal{V}|} & & \vdots \\
 & & & 0 \\
0 & \dots \, \frac{\alpha_s - \alpha_t}{1 - \alpha_t} \, \dots & 0 & \frac{1 - \alpha_s}{1 - \alpha_t}
\end{pmatrix}
= I + \frac{\alpha_s - \alpha_t}{1 - \alpha_t} m (x_0 - m)^\top
\end{equation}
$$
where the term $\frac{\alpha_s - \alpha_t}{1 - \alpha_t}$ in the last row appears at the column index corresponding to the clean token $x_0$.
It represents the probability that a masked token $x_t=m$ at time $t$ will jump to the clean token $x_0$ between $t$ and $s$.

Interpretation: if the token at time $t$ is $m$, and $s < t$, then:
$$
\begin{equation}
x_s = \begin{cases}
x_0, & \text{with probability } \frac{\alpha_s - \alpha_t}{1 - \alpha_t} \\
m, & \text{with probability } 1 - \frac{\alpha_s - \alpha_t}{1 - \alpha_t}
\end{cases}
\end{equation}
$$

<div>

We want to estimate the probability that a token at time $s < t$ remains unmasked, given that we know the original token $x_0$ and that at time $t$ the token is masked.

Using Bayes' theorem:
$$
\begin{align}
\mathbb{P}(x_s = x_0 | x_0, x_t = m) &= \frac{\mathbb{P}(x_t = m | x_s = x_0)  \mathbb{P}(x_s = x_0 | x_0)}{\mathbb{P}(x_t = m | x_0)} \\
&= \frac{\left(1 - \frac{\alpha_t}{\alpha_s}\right)  \alpha_s}{1 - \alpha_t} \\
&= \frac{\alpha_s - \alpha_t}{1 - \alpha_t}
\end{align}
$$

We can define the reverse transition distribution $q(x_s | x_t, x_0)$ via a reverse matrix $R_{s|t}^{x_0}$ conditioned on the clean token $x_0$:
$$
\begin{equation}
q(x_s | x_t, x_0) = \text{Cat}(x_s; \ (R_{s|t}^{x_0})^\top x_t)
\end{equation}
$$

Thus, the matrix $R_{s|t}^{x_0}$ is defined as follows.
$$
\begin{align}
R_{s|t}^{x_0} =
\left(
\begin{array}{ccc|c}
 & & & 0 \\
 & I_{|\mathcal{V}|} & & \vdots \\
 & & & 0 \\
\hline
0 & \dots \quad \frac{\alpha_s - \alpha_t}{1 - \alpha_t} \quad \dots & 0 & \frac{1 - \alpha_s}{1 - \alpha_t}
\end{array}
\right) \in \mathbb{R}^{K \times K}
\end{align}
$$
where the term $\frac{\alpha_s - \alpha_t}{1 - \alpha_t}$ in the last row appears at the column index corresponding to the clean token $x_0$.

</div>
</details>

By taking the first-order Taylor expansion for a small time step $\Delta t$, we define the conditional reverse rate matrix $\dot{R}_t^{x_0}$:
$$
\begin{equation}
\dot{R}_t^{x_0} =
\begin{pmatrix}
 & & & 0 \\
 & \mathbf{0}_{|\mathcal{V}|} & & \vdots \\
 & & & 0 \\
0 & \dots \, r_t \, \dots & 0 & -r_t
\end{pmatrix}
= r_t \, m (x_0 - m)^\top
\end{equation}
$$
where $r_t = -\frac{\dot{\alpha}_t}{1 - \alpha_t}$ is the instantaneous unmasking rate. While $\dot{R}_t^{x_0}$ represents the conditional reverse rate assuming the clean target $x_0$ is explicitly known, the true marginal reverse rate $\dot{R}_t(x_t)$ is obtained by taking $\dot{R}_t(x_t) = \mathbb{E}_{x_0 \sim q(\cdot | x_t)} [\dot{R}_t(x_t | x_0)]$.

In a general setting when the forward noising process is not necessarily restricted to mask tokens, the forward and reverse transitions can be defined locally using these instantaneous rates:
$$
\begin{equation}
q(x_{t+\Delta t} = y | x_t) = \mathbb{I}(y = x_t) + \Delta t \, [\dot{Q}_t(x_t)]_y + o(\Delta t)
\end{equation}
$$
$$
\begin{equation}
p(x_{t-\Delta t} = y | x_t) = \mathbb{I}(y = x_t) + \Delta t \, [\dot{R}_t(x_t)]_y + o(\Delta t)
\end{equation}
$$

We can show that the instantaneous reverse rate matrix $\dot{R}_t$ is defined as:
$$
\begin{equation}
[\dot{R}_t(x_t)]_y =
\begin{cases}
[\dot{Q}_t(y)]_{x_t} \frac{q(x_t = y)}{q(x_t)} & \text{if } y \neq x_t \\
- \sum_{y' \neq x_t} [\dot{R}_t(x_t)]_{y'} & \text{if } y = x_t
\end{cases}
\end{equation}
$$

<summary>Derivation of $\dot{R}_t$</summary>
<div>

We want to find the reverse instantaneous transition rate $[\dot{R}_t(x_t)]_y$ for transitioning to state $y \neq x_t$ backwards over an infinitesimal time step $\Delta t$, given the forward rate matrix $\dot{Q}_t$.

Using Bayes' theorem and the local forward transition $q(x_t | x_{t-\Delta t} = y)$:
$$
\begin{align}
p(x_{t-\Delta t} = y | x_t) &= \frac{q(x_t | x_{t-\Delta t} = y) q(x_{t-\Delta t} = y)}{q(x_t)} \\
&= \frac{\big(\Delta t [\dot{Q}_t(y)]_{x_t} + o(\Delta t)\big) \big(q(x_t = y) + O(\Delta t)\big)}{q(x_t)} \\
&= \Delta t \left( [\dot{Q}_t(y)]_{x_t} \frac{q(x_t = y)}{q(x_t)} \right) + o(\Delta t)
\end{align}
$$

By identifying this result with the definition of the reverse local transition $p(x_{t-\Delta t} = y | x_t) = \Delta t [\dot{R}_t(x_t)]_y + o(\Delta t)$ for $y \neq x_t$, we obtain the off-diagonal entries. The diagonal elements are then constrained by the requirement that the rows of a valid continuous-time transition rate matrix must sum to zero.

#### Training

For a discrete-time masked diffusion process, we define a path-wise generative model by approximately reversing the forward transitions step by step along the entire noising trajectory. The training objective has a single clean interpretation: we want to minimize the KL divergence between the distribution induced by the forward process $q(x_{0:T})$ and the one induced by the learned backward process $p_\theta(x_{0:T})$:

$$
\begin{equation}
\min_\theta\; \mathrm{KL}\!\Big(q(x_{0:T}),\, p_\theta(x_{0:T})\Big)
\end{equation}
$$

Since the entropy of the data distribution $H(q)$ is constant w.r.t. $\theta$, we can show that:

$$
\begin{equation}
\min_\theta\; \mathrm{KL}\!\Big(q(x_{0:T}),\, p_\theta(x_{0:T})\Big)
\;\iff\;
\max_\theta\; \mathbb{E}_{q(x_0)}\!\left[\text{ELBO}(x_0)\right]
\end{equation}
$$

where the per-sample ELBO is defined as:

$$
\begin{equation}
\text{ELBO}(x_0) = \mathbb{E}_{x_{1:T} \sim q(\cdot | x_0)} \left[ \log p_\theta(x_{0:T}) - \log q(x_{1:T}|x_0) \right]
\end{equation}
$$

<details>
<summary>Equivalence of KL minimization and ELBO maximization</summary>
<div>

Expand the KL over the full joint by factoring $q(x_{0:T}) = q(x_0)\, q(x_{1:T}|x_0)$
and $p_\theta(x_{0:T}) = p_\theta(x_0)\, p_\theta(x_{1:T}|x_0)$:

$$
\begin{align}
\mathrm{KL}\!\Big(q(x_{0:T}),\, p_\theta(x_{0:T})\Big)
&= \mathbb{E}_{q(x_{0:T})}\!\left[\log \frac{q(x_{0:T})}{p_\theta(x_{0:T})}\right] \\
&= \mathbb{E}_{q(x_0)}\!\left[\mathbb{E}_{q(x_{1:T}|x_0)}\!\left[\log \frac{q(x_0)\,q(x_{1:T}|x_0)}{p_\theta(x_{0:T})}\right]\right] \\
&= \mathbb{E}_{q(x_0)}\!\left[\log q(x_0)\right]
+ \mathbb{E}_{q(x_0)}\!\left[\mathbb{E}_{q(x_{1:T}|x_0)}\!\left[\log q(x_{1:T}|x_0) - \log p_\theta(x_{0:T})\right]\right] \\
&= -H(q) - \mathbb{E}_{q(x_0)}\!\left[\text{ELBO}(x_0)\right]
\end{align}
$$

Since $H(q) = -\mathbb{E}_{q(x_0)}[\log q(x_0)]$ does not depend on $\theta$, minimizing
the KL is exactly equivalent to maximizing $\mathbb{E}_{q(x_0)}[\text{ELBO}(x_0)]$.

</div>
</details>

<details>
<summary>Why ELBO lower-bounds $\log p_\theta(x_0)$</summary>
<div>

Multiply and divide inside the expectation by $p_\theta(x_{1:T}|x_0)$:

$$
\begin{align}
\text{ELBO}(x_0)
&= \mathbb{E}_{q(x_{1:T}|x_0)}\!\left[\log p_\theta(x_{0:T}) - \log q(x_{1:T}|x_0)\right] \\
&= \mathbb{E}_{q(x_{1:T}|x_0)}\!\left[\log p_\theta(x_0) + \log p_\theta(x_{1:T}|x_0) - \log q(x_{1:T}|x_0)\right] \\
&= \log p_\theta(x_0) - \mathbb{E}_{q(x_{1:T}|x_0)}\!\left[\log \frac{q(x_{1:T}|x_0)}{p_\theta(x_{1:T}|x_0)}\right]
\end{align}
$$

which gives the exact identity:

$$
\begin{equation}
\log p_\theta(x_0) = \text{ELBO}(x_0) + \mathrm{KL}\!\Big(q(x_{1:T}|x_0),\, p_\theta(x_{1:T}|x_0)\Big)
\end{equation}
$$

Since $\mathrm{KL} \geq 0$, the bound $\log p_\theta(x_0) \geq \text{ELBO}(x_0)$ follows
immediately, with equality iff the reverse trajectories perfectly recover the forward process.

</div>
</details>

The ELBO for a general discrete diffusion process is given by:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\text{ELBO}(x_0) = - \mathrm{KL}(q( \cdot | x_0) , p_{\text{source}}) - \int_0^1 \mathbb{E}_{x_t \sim q( \cdot | x_0)} \left[ \mathrm{KL}(\dot{R}_t(x_t | x_0) , \dot{R}_{\theta,t}(x_t)) \right] dt
\end{aligned}
\begin{equation}\end{equation}
$$

<details>
<summary>Training Loss Proof</summary>
<div>

We start with the known inequality for variational inference:
$$
\begin{equation}
    \log p_\theta(x_0) \geq \text{ELBO}(x_0),
\end{equation}
$$
where the evidence lower bound is defined as:
$$
\begin{equation}
    \text{ELBO}(x_0) = \mathbb{E}_{\substack{x_{1:T} \sim q( \cdot | x_0)}} \left[ \log p_\theta(x_{0:T}) - \log q(x_{1:T}|x_0) \right],
\end{equation}
$$
and $x_{j:T} = (x_{j/T}, x_{(j+1)/T}, \dots, x_1)$ denotes the sequence of states from time $j/T$ to $t=1$.

The forward process follows a Markovian structure:
$$
\begin{aligned}
    q(x_{1:T}|x_0) = \prod_{j=1}^{T} q(x_{j/T} | x_{(j-1)/T}, x_{(j-2)/T}, \dots, x_0) = \prod_{j=1}^{T} q(x_{j/T} | x_{(j-1)/T}, x_0)
\end{aligned}
\begin{equation} \end{equation}
$$
where the last equality holds because the forward process is memoryless given the previous states.

Similarly, the reverse model decomposes as:
$$
\begin{equation}
    p_\theta(x_{0:T}) = p_\theta(x_1) \prod_{j=1}^{T} p_\theta(x_{(j-1)/T} | x_{j/T}).
\end{equation}
$$

Substituting the Markovian decompositions into the ELBO:
$$
\begin{aligned}
    \text{ELBO}(x_0) &= \mathbb{E}_{\substack{x_{1:T} \sim q( \cdot | x_0)}} \left[ \log p_\theta(x_1) \right] \\
    &\quad + \sum_{j=1}^{T} \mathbb{E}_{\substack{x_{1:T} \sim q( \cdot | x_0)}} \left[ \log p_\theta(x_{(j-1)/T} | x_{j/T}) - \log q(x_{j/T} | x_{(j-1)/T}, x_0) \right]
\end{aligned}
\begin{equation} \end{equation}
$$

Using Bayes' theorem, we can rewrite the forward process conditional:
$$
\begin{aligned}
    \log q(x_{j/T} | x_{(j-1)/T}, x_0) = \log q(x_{(j-1)/T} | x_{j/T}, x_0) + \log q(x_{j/T} | x_0) - \log q(x_{(j-1)/T} | x_0).
\end{aligned}
\begin{equation} \end{equation}
$$

Substituting Bayes' rule into the ELBO and observing that consecutive terms telescope:
$$
\begin{aligned}
    \text{ELBO}(x_0) &= \mathbb{E}_{\substack{x_{1:T} \sim q( \cdot | x_0)}} \left[ \log q(x_1 | x_0) - \log q(x_0 | x_0) \right] \\
    &\quad + \mathbb{E}_{\substack{x_{1:T} \sim q( \cdot | x_0)}} \left[ \log p_\theta(x_1) \right] + \sum_{j=1}^{T} \mathbb{E}_{\substack{x_{1:T} \sim q( \cdot | x_0)}} \left[ \log p_\theta(x_{(j-1)/T} | x_{j/T}) - \log q(x_{(j-1)/T} | x_{j/T}, x_0) \right]
\end{aligned}
\begin{equation} \end{equation}
$$

Note that $\log q(x_0 | x_0) = \log(1) = 0$, so:
$$
\begin{aligned}
    \text{ELBO}(x_0) &= - \mathbb{E}_{\substack{x_{1:T} \sim q( \cdot | x_0)}} \left[ \log q(x_1 | x_0) - \log p_\theta(x_1) \right] \\
    &\quad - \sum_{j=1}^{T} \mathbb{E}_{\substack{x_{1:T} \sim q( \cdot | x_0)}} \left[ \log q(x_{(j-1)/T} | x_{j/T}, x_0) - \log p_\theta(x_{(j-1)/T} | x_{j/T}) \right]
\end{aligned}
\begin{equation} \end{equation}
$$

We can simplify the expectations over the joint distribution $q(x_{1:T}|x_0)$ by marginalizing:

For the first term:
$$
\begin{aligned}
    \mathbb{E}_{\substack{x_{1:T} \sim q( \cdot | x_0)}} \left[ \log q(x_1 | x_0) - \log p_\theta(x_1) \right] = \mathbb{E}_{\substack{x_1 \sim q( \cdot | x_0)}} \left[ \log q(x_1 | x_0) - \log p_\theta(x_1) \right]
\end{aligned}
\begin{equation} \end{equation}
$$

For the sum, we marginalize over all intermediate variables:
$$
\begin{aligned}
    &\mathbb{E}_{\substack{x_{1:T} \sim q( \cdot | x_0)}} \left[ \log q(x_{(j-1)/T} | x_{j/T}, x_0) - \log p_\theta(x_{(j-1)/T} | x_{j/T}) \right] \\
    &= \mathbb{E}_{\substack{x_{j/T} \sim q( \cdot | x_0)}} \left[ \mathbb{E}_{\substack{x_{(j-1)/T} \sim q( \cdot | x_{j/T}, x_0)}} \left[ \log q(x_{(j-1)/T} | x_{j/T}, x_0) - \log p_\theta(x_{(j-1)/T} | x_{j/T}) \right] \right]
\end{aligned}
\begin{equation} \end{equation}
$$

Therefore, the ELBO becomes:
$$
\begin{aligned}
    \text{ELBO}(x_0) &= - \mathrm{KL}\left(q(x_1|x_0) , p_\theta(x_1)\right) \\
    &\quad - \sum_{j=1}^{T} \mathbb{E}_{\substack{x_{j/T} \sim q( \cdot | x_0)}} \left[ \mathrm{KL}\left(q(x_{(j-1)/T} | x_{j/T}, x_0) , p_\theta(x_{(j-1)/T} | x_{j/T})\right) \right]
\end{aligned}
\begin{equation} \end{equation}
$$

**Conclusion**

For a general discrete diffusion process acting on tokens, we start from the full ELBO derived earlier:
$$
\begin{align}
    \text{ELBO}(x_0) &= - \mathrm{KL}\left(q(x_1 | x_0) , p_\theta(x_1)\right) \\
    &\quad - \sum_{j=1}^{T} \mathbb{E}_{\substack{x_{j/T} \sim q( \cdot | x_0)}} \left[ \mathrm{KL}\left(q(x_{(j-1)/T} | x_{j/T}, x_0) , p_\theta(x_{(j-1)/T} | x_{j/T})\right) \right]
\end{align}
$$

Let $\Delta t = 1/T$, $t = j/T$, and $s = (j-1)/T$. We denote the token transition rate vectors of the denoiser forward process and the reverse model as $\dot{R}_t(x_t | x_0)$ and $\dot{R}_{\theta,t}(x_t)$ respectively. For a small time step $\Delta t$, the probability of transitioning to a token $y \in \mathcal{V} \cup \{m\}$ is approximated by:
$$
\begin{align}
    q(y | x_t, x_0) &\approx \mathbb{I}(y = x_t) + \Delta t [\dot{R}_t(x_t | x_0)]_y \\
    p_\theta(y | x_t) &\approx \mathbb{I}(y = x_t) + \Delta t [\dot{R}_{\theta,t}(x_t)]_y
\end{align}
$$
where $[\dot{R}(x)]_y \geq 0$ for $y \neq x$, and the diagonal entries ensure normalization: $[\dot{R}(x)]_x = - \sum_{y \neq x} [\dot{R}(x)]_y$.

We expand the KL divergence term inside the sum by summing over the vocabulary:
$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathrm{KL}\left(q( \cdot | x_t, x_0) , p_\theta( \cdot | x_t)\right) = \sum_{y \in \mathcal{V} \cup \{m\}} q(y | x_t, x_0) \log \frac{q(y | x_t, x_0)}{p_\theta(y | x_t)}
\end{aligned}
\begin{equation} \end{equation}
$$

Separating the sum into the case where the token changes ($y \neq x_t$) and stays the same ($y = x_t$):

1. For $y \neq x_t$:
$$
\begin{aligned}
    \Delta t [\dot{R}_t(x_t | x_0)]_y \log \frac{\Delta t [\dot{R}_t(x_t | x_0)]_y}{\Delta t [\dot{R}_{\theta,t}(x_t)]_y} = \Delta t [\dot{R}_t(x_t | x_0)]_y \log \frac{[\dot{R}_t(x_t | x_0)]_y}{[\dot{R}_{\theta,t}(x_t)]_y}
\end{aligned}
\begin{equation} \end{equation}
$$

2. For $y = x_t$:
Using the approximation $\log \frac{1+a}{1+b} \approx a-b$:
$$
\begin{aligned}
    (1 + \Delta t [\dot{R}_t(x_t | x_0)]_{x_t}) \log \frac{1 + \Delta t [\dot{R}_t(x_t | x_0)]_{x_t}}{1 + \Delta t [\dot{R}_{\theta,t}(x_t)]_{x_t}}
    &\approx 1 \cdot (\Delta t [\dot{R}_t(x_t | x_0)]_{x_t} - \Delta t [\dot{R}_{\theta,t}(x_t)]_{x_t}) \\
    &= \Delta t \left( - \sum_{y \neq x_t} [\dot{R}_t(x_t | x_0)]_y + \sum_{y \neq x_t} [\dot{R}_{\theta,t}(x_t)]_y \right)
\end{aligned}
\begin{equation} \end{equation}
$$

Combining these terms, the KL divergence becomes:
$$
\begin{aligned}
    \mathrm{KL} \approx \Delta t \sum_{y \neq x_t} \left( [\dot{R}_t(x_t | x_0)]_y \log \frac{[\dot{R}_t(x_t | x_0)]_y}{[\dot{R}_{\theta,t}(x_t)]_y} - [\dot{R}_t(x_t | x_0)]_y + [\dot{R}_{\theta,t}(x_t)]_y \right)
\end{aligned}
\begin{equation} \end{equation}
$$
This expression corresponds to $\Delta t \cdot \mathrm{KL}(\dot{R}_t(x_t | x_0) , \dot{R}_{\theta,t}(x_t))$, the generalized KL divergence between the instantaneous transition rate vectors for the token $x_t$.

Substituting this back into the ELBO, the sum over $j$ becomes a Riemann sum. Taking the limit as $T \to \infty$:
$$
\begin{aligned}
    \text{ELBO}(x_0) = - \mathrm{KL}\left(q(x_1 | x_0) , p_\theta(x_1)\right) - \int_0^1 \mathbb{E}_{\substack{x_t \sim q( \cdot | x_0)}} \left[ \mathrm{KL}\left(\dot{R}_t(x_t | x_0) , \dot{R}_{\theta,t}(x_t)\right) \right] dt
\end{aligned}
\begin{equation} \end{equation}
$$
Note that $p_\theta(x_1)$ corresponds to the known base distribution $p_{\text{source}}$ (typically the fully masked state or uniform distribution).

Ignoring constants independent of $\theta$, the general KL objective can be written explicitly as:

$$
\begin{aligned}
L_t(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{\substack{x_0 \sim p_{\text{data}} \\ x_t \sim q( \cdot |x_0)}} \left[ \sum_{y \neq x_t} \left( [\dot{R}_{\theta,t}(x_t)]_y - [\dot{R}_t(x_t | x_0)]_y \log [\dot{R}_{\theta,t}(x_t)]_y \right) \right]
\end{aligned}
\begin{equation} \end{equation}
$$

In the specific case of masked diffusion, we can express the loss in terms of the denoiser output $D_{\theta,t}(x_t)$:

$$
\begin{align}
L(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{\substack{\mathbf{x}_0 \sim p_{\text{data}} \\ x_t^i \sim q( \cdot | x_0^i)}} \sum_{i=1}^{S} \left[ \mathbb{I}(x_t^i = m) \left( -\frac{\dot{\alpha}_t}{1 - \alpha_t} \mathbf{x}_0^{i \top} \log D_{\theta,t}^i(\mathbf{x}_t) \right) \right]
\end{align}
$$

We start from (cf. previous proof, <span style="color: red"> cite eq </span> ):

$$
\begin{aligned}
    \text{ELBO}(x_0) &= - \mathrm{KL}\left(q(x_1|x_0) , p_\theta(x_1)\right) \\
    &\quad - \sum_{j=1}^{T} \mathbb{E}_{\substack{x_{j/T} \sim q( \cdot | x_0)}} \left[ \mathrm{KL}\left(q(x_{(j-1)/T} | x_{j/T}, x_0) , p_\theta(x_{(j-1)/T} | x_{j/T})\right) \right]

Since $x_1 = m$ (fully masked at $t=1$), we have $\mathrm{KL}(q(x_1|x_0) , p_\theta(x_1)) = 0$. Therefore:
$$
\begin{aligned}
\text{ELBO}(x_0) = - \sum_{j=1}^{T} \mathbb{E}_{\substack{x_{j/T} \sim q( \cdot | x_0)}} \left[ \mathrm{KL}\left(q(x_{(j-1)/T} | x_{j/T}, x_0) , p_\theta(x_{(j-1)/T} | x_{j/T})\right) \right]
\end{aligned}
\begin{equation} \end{equation}
$$

The KL divergence can be expanded as a sum over all possible values of $x_{(j-1)/T} \in \mathcal{V} \cup \{m\}$:
$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathrm{KL}\left(q(x_{(j-1)/T} | x_{j/T}, x_0) , p_\theta(x_{(j-1)/T} | x_{j/T})\right) = \sum_{x_{(j-1)/T} \in \mathcal{V} \cup \{m\}} q(x_{(j-1)/T} | x_{j/T}, x_0) \log \frac{q(x_{(j-1)/T} | x_{j/T}, x_0)}{p_\theta(x_{(j-1)/T} | x_{j/T})}
\end{aligned}
\begin{equation} \end{equation}
$$

From previous definitions <span style="color: red"> cite eq </span>, we recall, for $x_t = m$:
$$
\begin{aligned}
q(x_s | x_t, x_0) = \left[ \frac{\alpha_s - \alpha_t}{1 - \alpha_t} \mathbb{I}(x_s = x_0) + \frac{1 - \alpha_s}{1 - \alpha_t} \mathbb{I}(x_s = m) \right]
\end{aligned}
\begin{equation} \end{equation}
$$

We now specialize to the discrete times $s=(j-1)/T$ and $t=j/T$. The reverse model is $p_\theta(x_s| x_t)=q(x_s| x_t,D_\theta(x_t,t))$, so when $x_t=m$ we have:
$$
\begin{align}
    p_\theta(x_s=v| x_t=m) &= \frac{\alpha_s-\alpha_t}{1-\alpha_t}\,D_\theta(v| x_t,t)\quad (v\in\mathcal{V}), \\
    p_\theta(x_s=m| x_t=m) &= \frac{1-\alpha_s}{1-\alpha_t}.
\end{align}
$$

When $x_t\neq m$, both $q(x_s| x_t,x_0)$ and $p_\theta(x_s| x_t)$ put all their mass on $x_s=x_t=x_0$, so the KL term is zero.

Hence only the case $x_t=m$ contributes. Therefore:
$$
\begin{aligned}
    \mathrm{KL}\!\left(q(x_s| x_t,x_0)\,\|\,p_\theta(x_s| x_t)\right)
    &= \mathbb{I}(x_t=m)\,
    q(x_s| x_t,x_0)\,
    \log\frac{q(x_s| x_t,x_0)}{p_\theta(x_s| x_t)} \\
    &= \mathbb{I}(x_t=m)\,
    \left[ \frac{\alpha_s-\alpha_t}{1-\alpha_t}\mathbb{I}(x_s=x_0)
    + \frac{1-\alpha_s}{1-\alpha_t}\mathbb{I}(x_s=m) \right] \\
    &\quad \log\frac{\left[ \frac{\alpha_s-\alpha_t}{1-\alpha_t}\mathbb{I}(x_s=x_0)
    + \frac{1-\alpha_s}{1-\alpha_t}\mathbb{I}(x_s=m) \right]}{
    \left[ \frac{\alpha_s-\alpha_t}{1-\alpha_t}D_\theta(x_s| x_t,t)\mathbb{I}(x_s\neq m)
    + \frac{1-\alpha_s}{1-\alpha_t}\mathbb{I}(x_s=m) \right]} \\
    &= \mathbb{I}(x_t=m)\Bigg[
    \frac{\alpha_s-\alpha_t}{1-\alpha_t}\log\frac{\frac{\alpha_s-\alpha_t}{1-\alpha_t}}
    {\frac{\alpha_s-\alpha_t}{1-\alpha_t}D_\theta(x_0| x_t,t)}
    +\frac{1-\alpha_s}{1-\alpha_t}\log\frac{\frac{1-\alpha_s}{1-\alpha_t}}
    {\frac{1-\alpha_s}{1-\alpha_t}}
    \Bigg] \\
    &= \mathbb{I}(x_t=m)\,\frac{\alpha_s-\alpha_t}{1-\alpha_t}\,
    \big(-\log D_\theta(x_0| x_t,t)\big).
\end{aligned}
\begin{equation} \end{equation}
$$

Plugging this into the ELBO yields:
$$
\begin{aligned}
    \text{ELBO}(x_0)
    = \sum_{j=1}^T \mathbb{E}_{\substack{x_{j/T} \sim q( \cdot | x_0)}}
    \left[ \mathbb{I}(x_{j/T}=m)\,\frac{\alpha_{(j-1)/T}-\alpha_{j/T}}{1-\alpha_{j/T}}
    \,x_0^\top \log D_\theta(x_{j/T}, j/T)\right].
\end{aligned}
\begin{equation} \end{equation}
$$

Passing to the continuous-time limit $T \to \infty$ with $t=j/T$, $s=(j-1)/T$, and $\alpha_s-\alpha_t \approx -\dot{\alpha}_t\,\frac{1}{T}$, the sum is a Riemann sum:
$$
\begin{aligned}
    \text{ELBO}(x_0)
    = \int_0^1 \mathbb{E}_{\substack{x_t \sim q( \cdot | x_0)}}\left[
    \mathbb{I}(x_t=m)\,\frac{-\dot{\alpha}_t}{1-\alpha_t}\,x_0^\top\logD_\theta(x_t,t)
    \right]dt,
\end{aligned}
\begin{equation} \end{equation}
$$
which matches the sequence-level training loss defined previously.

#### Concrete Score

In continuous diffusion, the score function $\nabla \log p_t(x)$ dictates the deterministic reverse ODE:
$$
\begin{equation}
dx = \left[h(x, t) - \tfrac{1}{2}g(t)^2 \,\nabla \log p_t(x)\right]dt
\end{equation}
$$
which has the same marginal densities as the time-reversed stochastic process. In a discrete state space, there is no notion of a continuous gradient. However, one can write the sampling equation for the reverse process. Indeed, remembering from <span style="color: red"> cite eq </span> that:
$$
\begin{equation}
s_t(x)_y = \frac{[\dot{R}_t(x)]_y}{[\dot{Q}_t(x)]_y}
\end{equation}
$$
we can directly write the sampling used with a learned concrete score:
$$
\begin{equation}
\mathbb{P}\!\left(X_{t-h} = y | X_t = x\right) = \mathbb{I}(x=y) + h\,[\dot{Q}_t(x)]_y\,s_{\theta,t}(x)_y + o(h)
\end{equation}
$$

Then, we can express the loss <span style="color: red"> cite eq </span> in terms of concrete score:
$$
\begin{aligned}
L_t(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{\substack{x_0 \sim p_0 \\ x_t \sim q( \cdot |x_0)}} \left[ \sum_{y \neq x_t} [\dot{Q}_t(x_t)]_y \left( s_{\theta,t}(x_t)_y - \frac{p_t(y|x_0)}{p_t(x_t|x_0)} \log s_{\theta,t}(x_t)_y \right) \right] + C
\end{aligned}
\begin{equation} \end{equation}
$$

### Equivalence with Discrete Flow Matching

While discrete diffusion and discrete flow matching share similar mathematical foundations, their primary difference lies in the time convention. In discrete diffusion, $t = 0$ corresponds to clean data, and $t = 1$ corresponds to the fully masked state. Discrete flow matching uses the exact opposite convention.

#### Probability velocity field and reverse rate matrix

We can perfectly translate between the two frameworks using a simple time reversal: $t_{\text{FM}} = 1 - t_{\text{DD}}$. Under this time change, the velocity field in flow matching directly corresponds to the entries of the reverse rate matrix in discrete diffusion:

$$
\begin{equation}
u_{1-t}^{\text{FM}} = \dot{R}_t
\end{equation}
$$

<details>
<summary>Proof</summary>
<div>

We start from the infinitesimal reverse-time transition of the discrete diffusion process <span style="color: red">(**ref your eq**)</span>:

$$
\begin{equation}
p(x_{t-\Delta t} = y \mid x_t) = \mathbb{I}(y = x_t) + \Delta t \, [\dot{R}_t(x_t)]_y + o(\Delta t)
\end{equation}
$$

**Step 1: variable change $t' = 1 - t$.** We have $t = 1 - t'$, so $x_t = x_{1-t'}$ and $x_{t - \Delta t} = x_{1 - t' - \Delta t}$. The rate evaluates at $\dot{R}_{1-t'}$. The equation rewrites as:

$$
\begin{equation}
p(x_{1-t'-\Delta t} = y \mid x_{1-t'}) = \mathbb{I}(y = x_{1-t'}) + \Delta t \, [\dot{R}_{1-t'}(x_{1-t'})]_y + o(\Delta t)
\end{equation}
$$

**Step 2: identify the FM process.** Since $t_{\text{FM}} = 1 - t_{\text{DD}}$, we identify $x^{\text{FM}}_{t'} = x_{1-t'}$. We deduce $x^{\text{FM}}_{t' + \Delta t} = x_{1 - t' - \Delta t}$.

Substituting directly into the equation above:

$$
\begin{equation}
p(x^{\text{FM}}_{t' + \Delta t} = y \mid x^{\text{FM}}_{t'}) = \mathbb{I}(y = x^{\text{FM}}_{t'}) + \Delta t \, [\dot{R}_{1-t'}(x^{\text{FM}}_{t'})]_y + o(\Delta t)
\end{equation}
$$

This is exactly the infinitesimal characterization of a CTMC with velocity field $u_{t'}^{\text{FM}} = \dot{R}_{1-t'}$. By uniqueness of the Kolmogorov forward equation for bounded generators, this fully determines the law of $x^{\text{FM}}$.

</div>
</details>

#### Training

In discrete flow matching, the matching loss at flow time $1-t$ is simply the negative log-likelihood of the clean token:
$$
\begin{equation}
L_{\text{FM}}(1-t) = -\mathbb{E}\left[ \log \mu_\theta^i(\mathbf{x}_t, t)_{x_1^i} \right]
\end{equation}
$$

Since both losses use the same denoiser $\mu_\theta^i(\mathbf{x}_t, t)$, we can compare them directly:

$$
\begin{equation}
L_{\text{DD}}(t) \approx \frac{-\dot{\alpha}_t}{1 - \alpha_t} \, L_{\text{FM}}(1-t)
\end{equation}
$$

<details>
<summary>Derivation</summary>
<div>
We decompose the flow matching loss using the law of total expectation:
$$
\begin{aligned}
L_{\text{FM}}(1-t) = (1 - \alpha_t) \cdot \mathbb{E}\left[ -\log \mu_\theta^i(\mathbf{x}_t, t)_{x_1^i} \mid x_t^i = m \right] + \alpha_t \cdot \mathbb{E}\left[ -\log \mu_\theta^i(\mathbf{x}_t, t)_{x_1^i} \mid x_t^i \neq m \right]
\end{aligned}
\begin{equation} \end{equation}
$$

Since a well-trained denoiser can trivially reconstruct unmasked positions by copying the input, the second term vanishes, giving:
$$
\begin{equation}
L_{\text{FM}}(1-t) \approx (1 - \alpha_t) \cdot \mathbb{E}\left[ -\log \mu_\theta^i(\mathbf{x}_t, t)_{x_1^i} \mid x_t^i = m \right]
\end{equation}
$$

Similarly, applying the law of total expectation to the discrete diffusion loss extracts the masking probability $(1 - \alpha_t)$, which cancels with the denominator:
$$
\begin{aligned}
L_{\text{DD}}(t) = (1 - \alpha_t) \cdot \frac{-\dot{\alpha}_t}{1 - \alpha_t} \cdot \mathbb{E}\left[ -\log \mu_\theta^i(\mathbf{x}_t, t)_{x_1^i} \mid x_t^i = m \right] = -\dot{\alpha}_t \cdot \mathbb{E}\left[ -\log \mu_\theta^i(\mathbf{x}_t, t)_{x_1^i} \mid x_t^i = m \right]
\end{aligned}
\begin{equation} \end{equation}
$$

Both expressions share the same conditional expectation. Eliminating it yields the exact relationship (up to the vanishing unmasked-loss approximation).

#### Planner aware path learning

Vanilla sampling in masked discrete diffusion and flow matching is computationally expensive. It requires $(1/h) \times S$ sequential steps, where $h$ is the time step size and $S$ is the length of the sequence. At every single micro-step, we must compute and draw from probability vectors of size $|\mathcal{V}|$ for all tokens. For example, with a step size of $h = 0.01$ and a sequence length of $S = 3000$, we would need an impractical $300,000$ steps to generate a single sample.

To make generation tractable, the standard approach used in the industry for large masked diffusion models is **planner-guided sampling**. Instead of relying on infinitesimal continuous-time steps, we fix a manageable number of total generation steps, denoted as $N$. At each step, the algorithm aggressively decodes and fixes exactly $S/N$ tokens.

**Algorithm: Planner-Guided Sampling**

**Input:** Planner strategy, trained denoiser $D_\theta$, total number of steps $N$
1. **Initialize:** $\mathbf{x}_0 \leftarrow (m, \dots, m)$ (a sequence of $S$ mask tokens)
2. **For** $k = 0, 1, \dots, N - 1$:
    * **Plan:** Sample candidate tokens $\mathbf{z} \sim D_\theta(\mathbf{x}_k)$
    * **Select:** A "planner" evaluates these candidate predictions based on a specific criterion. It chooses a set of positions $\mathcal{I}$ where $|\mathcal{I}| = S/N$ to reveal.
    * **Denoise:** Create $\mathbf{x}_{k+1}$ by setting $x_{k+1}^i = z^i$ for all $i \in \mathcal{I}$, keeping the rest as masks
3. **Return** fully generated sequence $\mathbf{x}_N$

To make this concrete, let's look at how we actually choose these tokens and why that choice forces us to rethink how we train the model. A simple and widely used strategy is the soft greedy planner. Instead of unmasking tokens at random, it selects the positions where the denoiser is most confident in its predictions. To prevent the generation process from becoming rigidly deterministic, we relax this strict selection into a soft probability distribution using a softmax function with a temperature parameter $\tau$:
$$
\begin{equation}
G_\phi^\tau(\mathbf{z}, \mathbf{x}_t)_j \propto \exp\left(\frac{1}{\tau} \log D_\theta^j(\mathbf{x}_t)_{z^j}\right)
\end{equation}
$$
This formulation assigns higher selection probabilities to the tokens the model is sure about. As $\tau \to \infty$, this reduces to uniform random sampling. As $\tau \to 0$, it converges to a strict, hard selection of only the most confident token.

For a concrete example, suppose we have two masked positions where the denoiser predicts the target tokens with confidences of $0.8$ and $0.2$. Setting the temperature to $\tau = 1/2$, the planner mathematically scales these probabilities by a power of $1/\tau = 2$ and normalizes them:
$$
D_\theta(\mathbf{x}_t)_{\mathbf{z}} = \begin{pmatrix} 0.8 \\ 0.2 \end{pmatrix} \quad \implies \quad G_\phi^{1/2}(\mathbf{z}, \mathbf{x}_t) = \frac{1}{0.8^2 + 0.2^2} \begin{pmatrix} 0.8^2 \\ 0.2^2 \end{pmatrix} \approx \begin{pmatrix} 0.941 \\ 0.059 \end{pmatrix}
$$
The initial confidence gap is heavily amplified, strongly favoring to denoise next the more certain prediction while still preserving a small element of stochasticity.

However, introducing a planner-aware sampling process creates a mismatch between training and inference. During training, the standard objective masks tokens uniformly at random across all positions and orderings, so the denoiser learns to predict any token from any arbitrary subset of revealed context. At inference, however, the planner selectively reveals tokens along an optimized path, meaning the denoiser operates on structured, informative contexts it was never explicitly trained on. Paradoxically, this makes training strictly harder than inference: the model must learn to denoise under the worst-case random maskings, whereas the planner later guides it through the most favorable ones.

To fix this discrepancy, we need a loss function that makes the model aware of the planner during the training phase. By safely detaching gradients through the planner to maintain numerical stability, we can achieve this by simply reweighting our standard objective. We assign higher loss weights to the positions the planner is most likely to select. The resulting Planner-Aware Path Learning (PAPL) loss is the standard continuous masked diffusion cross-entropy, mathematically augmented with these planner-derived weights:
$$
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{\text{PAPL}}(\theta) = - \mathbb{E}_{t, \mathbf{x}_0, \mathbf{x}_t} \left[ \sum_{ix_t^i = m} \frac{1}{t}(1 + \alpha w^i) \log D_\theta^i(\mathbf{x}_t)_{x_0^i} \right]
\end{equation}
$$
Here, $w^i$ represents the normalized weight of position $i$ assigned by the planner, and $\alpha$ is a hyperparameter that controls the strength of this planner guidance.
