Flow Matching (FM) and diffusion are the state-of-the-art frameworks for continuous data generation, particularly images. This blog post is a pedagogical introduction to their adaptation to discrete data.

The [first section](#discrete-flow-matching) contains an introduction to Discrete Flow Matching. We then present Discrete Diffusion through its two common perspectives: the [variational perspective](#variational-discrete-diffusion) and the [score-based perspective](#score-based-discrete-diffusion). The connection between all these viewpoints is made explicit in the [last section](#equivalence-between-viewpoints).

### Generating discrete data

Like their continuous analogs, Discrete Flow Matching and Discrete Diffusion aim at mapping a simple source distribution to a complex target distribution (the actual data we want to generate).
To do so, they create a sequence of intermediate distributions $(p_t)_{t}$ that progressively evolve from source to target distribution.

In this blog post, we deal with discrete data: the space on which the probability distributions are defined is $\mathcal{S} := \mathcal{V}^S$, namely the space of sequences of length $S \in \mathbb{N}$ over a given vocabulary $\mathcal{V}$.
A sequence $\mathbf{x}$ in $\mathcal{S}$ is denoted as $\mathbf{x} = x^1 \dots x^S$, the $x^i$'s being called *tokens*.
In the vocabulary, we add a special token: the mask token, denoted as $m$.

The source distribution acts as our starting point for generation and is typically chosen to be either:
- The **mask distribution ($p_{\text{mask}} := \delta_{m\ldots m}$):** All sequences start entirely filled with the mask token $m$; $\delta$ is the Dirac delta distribution.
- The **uniform distribution ($p_{\text{unif}}$):** All tokens in the starting sequence are sampled uniformly across the vocabulary $\mathcal{V}$.

In this post, *we use the mask distribution ($p_{\text{mask}}$) as our running example*; adaptations in the case of other distributions are available as toggleable content.

As a first example, consider generating Sudoku grids. The vocabulary is $\mathcal{V} = \{1, 2, \ldots, 9, m\}$, and the sequence length is 81, as there are 81 cases in a grid.
Discrete Flow Matching and Discrete Diffusion start from a sample from the source distribution, i.e. a fully masked grid here.
The model then iteratively fills this grid, step-by-step, finally reaching a sample from the target distribution (a valid, completed Sudoku board).

### Discrete Flow Matching

#### Setup: probability velocities

In this section we adopt the Flow Matching convention: time goes from $t=0$ to $t=1$, the source distribution is thus denoted $p_0$ while the target distribution is $p_1$.

In *Continuous* Flow Matching, a central object is the *velocity field* $u: \mathbb{R}^d \times [0, 1] \to \mathbb{R}^d$, that governs the generating process: samples are obtained as solutions at time $t=1$ of the Ordinary Differential Equation (ODE)
$$
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:flow-ode}
\begin{cases}
x(0) = x_0 \\
\partial_t x(t) = u(x(t), t), \quad \forall t \in [0,1]
\end{cases}
\end{equation}
$$
and if $p_t$ is defined as the law of the ODE solution $x(t)$ at time $t$ when $x_0 \sim p_0$, we say that $u$ generates the probability path $(p_t)_{t \in [0, 1]}$.

Here comes the first distinction between discrete and Continuous FM: as solving an ODE when the state space is discrete is not possible, discrete FM relies on a different type of object: the *probability velocity*.

<Statement kind="Definition" label="probability-velocity" title="Probability velocity $u_t$">
Let $u : \mathcal{S} \times [0, 1] \to \mathbb{R}^S$, and $(X_t)_{t\in[0,1]}$ be random variables of laws $(p_t)_{t \in [0, 1]}$.
Denote $u_t := u(\cdot, t)$.
In the discrete case, we say that $u$ *generates* the probability path $(p_t)_{t \in [0, 1]}$ if
$$
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:prob-velocity}
\mathbb{P}(X_{t+h} = \mathbf{y} | X_t = \mathbf{x}) = \mathbb{I}(\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{y}) + h u_{t}(\mathbf{x})_{\mathbf{y}} + o(h)
\end{equation}
$$
</Statement>
If we index the state space as $\mathcal{S} = \{\mathbf{y}_1, \dots, \mathbf{y}_{|\mathcal{S}|}\}$ and choose $k$ such that $\mathbf{y}_k = \mathbf{x}$ (the current state), then the full transition-probability vector reads
$$
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:transition-vector}
\underbrace{\mathbb{P}(X_{t+h} = \cdot | X_t=\mathbf{x})}_{\in \mathbb{R}^{|\mathcal{S}|}}  =
\begin{bmatrix}
  h u_t(\mathbf{x})_{\mathbf{y}_1} \\
  \vdots \\
  h u_t(\mathbf{x})_{\mathbf{y}_{k-1}} \\
  1 + h u_t(\mathbf{x})_{\mathbf{y}_k}  \\
  h u_t(\mathbf{x})_{\mathbf{y}_{k+1}} \\
  \vdots \\
  h u_t(\mathbf{x})_{\mathbf{y}_{|\mathcal{S}|}}
\end{bmatrix}
+ o(h),
\end{equation}
$$

As $t$ increases to $t+h$, the variable $\mathbf{x}_t$ stays in state $\mathbf{x}$ with probability $1 + h u_t(\mathbf{x})_{\mathbf{x}}$, or moves to any other state $\mathbf{y} \neq \mathbf{x}$ with probability $h u(\mathbf{x})_{\mathbf{y}}$.
We reach a fundamental point: unlike Continuous FM, where sampling is deterministic once $x_0$ has been sampled from $p_0$, **sampling in Discrete Flow Matching is inherently stochastic at each step**. This explains why we are allowed to take $p_0 = \delta_{m\ldots m}$ and still produce different outputs $x_1$, which would not be possible with a deterministic sampling.

<Statement kind="Proposition" label="probability-velocity-mass-conservation" title="Mass conservation for probability velocities">
The probability velocity $u$ must preserve probability mass: for @eq:prob-velocity to define a valid probability, for every $t\in [0, 1]$ $u_t$ must satisfy:
1. **Positivity:** $u_t(\mathbf{x})_{\mathbf{y}} \ge 0$ for all $\mathbf{y} \neq \mathbf{x}$.
$$
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:velocity-positivity}
u_t(\mathbf{x})_{\mathbf{y}} \ge 0, \qquad \mathbf{y} \neq \mathbf{x}.
\end{equation}
$$
2. **Normalization:** $\sum_{\mathbf{y} \in \mathcal{S}} u_t(\mathbf{x})_{\mathbf{y}} = 0$ for all $\mathbf{x} \in \mathcal{S}$ (and so $u_t(\mathbf{x})_{\mathbf{x}} \le 0$).
$$
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:velocity-normalization}
\sum_{\mathbf{y} \in \mathcal{S}} u_t(\mathbf{x})_{\mathbf{y}} = 0.
\end{equation}
$$
</Statement>


<details>
<summary>Justification</summary>
<div>
Positivity guarantees nonnegative off-diagonal transition probabilities:
$$
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:velocity-positivity-proof}
\mathbb{P}(X_{t+h}=\mathbf{y}| X_t=\mathbf{x}) = h\,u_t(\mathbf{x})_{\mathbf{y}} + o(h) \ge 0, \qquad \mathbf{y}\neq\mathbf{x}.
\end{equation}
$$
Normalization guarantees total mass conservation:
$$
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:velocity-normalization-proof}
\sum_{\mathbf{y}\in\mathcal{S}} \mathbb{P}(X_{t+h}=\mathbf{y}| X_t=\mathbf{x})
= 1 + h\sum_{\mathbf{y}\in\mathcal{S}}u_t(\mathbf{x})_{\mathbf{y}} + o(h) = 1 + o(h).
\end{equation}
$$
</div>
</details>


We can interpret $u_t(\mathbf{x})_{\mathbf{y}}$ as the instantaneous rate at which the random variable $X_t$ transitions from state $\mathbf{x}$ to state $\mathbf{y}$ at time $t$.
Much like the ODE @eq:flow-ode can be discretized with Euler's method, the sampling process @eq:prob-velocity can be discretized as
$$
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:euler-sampling}
\mathbf{x}_{t + h} \sim \text{Cat}\left(\mathbf{e}_{\mathbf{x}_t} + h u_t(\mathbf{x}_t)\right)
\end{equation}
$$
where $\mathbf{e}_{\mathbf{x}} \in \mathbb{R}^{|\mathcal{S}|}$ is the one-hot vector corresponding to state $\mathbf{x} \in \mathcal{S}$, and $\text{Cat}$ denotes the categorical distribution.
As $t$ increases to $t+h$, the variable $\mathbf{x}_t$ stays in state $\mathbf{x}$ with probability $1 + h u_t(\mathbf{x})_{\mathbf{x}}$, or moves to any other state $\mathbf{y} \neq \mathbf{x}$ with probability $h u_t(\mathbf{x})_{\mathbf{y}}$.
We reach a fundamental point: unlike Continuous FM, where sampling is deterministic once $x_0$ has been sampled from $p_0$, **sampling in Discrete Flow Matching is inherently stochastic at each step**.
This explains why we are able to take $p_0 = \delta_{m\ldots m}$ and still produce different outputs $x_1$, which would not be possible with a deterministic sampling.

In Continuous Flow Matching, we have the guarantee that a velocity field $u_t$ generates a probability path $(p_t)$ if the pair $(p_t, u_t)$ satisfies the continuity equation $\partial_t p_t + \nabla \cdot (u_t p_t) = 0$.
The equivalent in the discrete setting is called the Kolmogorov Equation:

<Statement kind="Proposition" label="kolmogorov-equation" title="Kolmogorov equation">
The probability velocity $u_t$ generates $(p_t)$ in the sense of @eq:prob-velocity if and only if the pair $(u_t, p_t)$ satisfies the Kolmogorov equation:
$$
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:kolmogorov-equation}
\forall \mathbf{y} \in \mathcal{S}, \quad
\frac{d}{dt} p_t(\mathbf{y}) = \sum_{\mathbf{x} \in \mathcal{S}} u_{t}(\mathbf{x})_{\mathbf{y}} \, p_t(\mathbf{x})
\end{equation}
$$
</Statement>

<details>
<summary>Proof</summary>
<div>
We can derive this directly by expanding the marginal probability at $t+h$ using the law of total probability and our velocity definition:

$$
\begin{align}
p_{t+h}(\mathbf{y}) &= \mathbb{P}(X_{t+h} = \mathbf{y})  \label{eq:kolmogorov-proof-1} \\
&= \sum_{\mathbf{x} \in \mathcal{S}} \mathbb{P}(X_{t+h} = \mathbf{y} | X_t = \mathbf{x})\, \mathbb{P}(X_t = \mathbf{x})  \label{eq:kolmogorov-proof-2} \\
&= \sum_{\mathbf{x} \in \mathcal{S}} \left(\mathbb{I}(\mathbf{y} = \mathbf{x}) + h\,u_t(\mathbf{x})_{\mathbf{y}} + o(h)\right) p_t(\mathbf{x})  \label{eq:kolmogorov-proof-3} \\
&= p_t(\mathbf{y}) + h \sum_{\mathbf{x} \in \mathcal{S}} u_t(\mathbf{x})_{\mathbf{y}}\, p_t(\mathbf{x}) + o(h) \label{eq:kolmogorov-proof-4}
\end{align}
$$

Rearranging the terms and taking the limit as $h \to 0$ gives:

$$
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:kolmogorov-limit}
\lim_{h \to 0} \frac{p_{t+h}(\mathbf{y}) - p_t(\mathbf{y})}{h} = \sum_{\mathbf{x} \in \mathcal{S}} u_t(\mathbf{x})_{\mathbf{y}}\, p_t(\mathbf{x})
\end{equation}
$$

For the reverse implication (if the equation holds, then $u$ generates $p_t$), let $\tilde{p}_t$ denote the true marginal probability path that the velocity field $u_t$ generates, starting from the initial distribution $\tilde{p}_0 = p_0$. By the forward implication proven above, $\tilde{p}_t$ satisfies the Kolmogorov equation.

{/*
$$
\begin{align}
\frac{d}{dt} \tilde{p}_t(\mathbf{y}) &= \sum_{\mathbf{x} \in \mathcal{S}} u_t(\mathbf{x})_{\mathbf{y}}\, \tilde{p}_t(\mathbf{x}) \label{eq:kolmogorov-tilde-1}
\end{align}
$$
*/}

By hypothesis, the given probability path $p_t$ also satisfies this exact same linear ordinary differential equation (ODE). Because both $p_t$ and $\tilde{p}_t$ are solutions to the same first-order linear ODEs and share the identical initial condition ($p_0 = \tilde{p}_0$), the Picard-Lindelöf uniqueness theorem guarantees that $p_t(\mathbf{y}) = \tilde{p}_t(\mathbf{y})$ for all times $t$ and all states $\mathbf{y} \in \mathcal{S}$. Therefore, the vector field $u_t$ exactly generates the probability path $p_t$.

</div>
</details>

Now that we are equipped with adequate definitions of probability paths and velocities for the discrete case, together with a way to check that one is associated to the other, we can state the approach of Discrete Flow Matching, which is in fact the same as Continuous Flow Matching:
1. Build an explicit probability path $(p_t)$ going from $p_0$ to $p_1$ (@eq:marginal-path) using a conditioning variable, conditional probability paths and conditional velocities.
2. Build a probability velocity $u_t$ generating $(p_t)$ (@eq:marginal-velocity-def-2)
3. Provide an explicit loss to learn $u_t$ as a neural network (@eq:cdfm-2)

Before embarking on this task, we make an important modelling choice.
Learning probability velocities taking values in the full sequence space $\mathcal{S}=\mathcal{V}^S$ is cumbersome, as the cardinality of the latter is $|\mathcal{V}|^S$, e.g. $11^{81}$ for the sudoku example!
To make computations tractable, Discrete Flow Matching makes a **factorization assumption**: the probability path it builds updates each token in the sequence independently.
As the next proposition shows, this has a fundamental consequence: the probability velocity that generates it can be written as a sum of token-wise contributions.

<Statement kind="Proposition" label="factorization" title="Factorized velocity">
Let $(X_t)$ be random variables of laws $p_t$, and assume that the transition probabilities factorize over tokens, namely for $\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{y} \in \mathcal{S}$:
$$
\begin{align}
\mathbb{P}(X_{t+h} = \mathbf{y} | X_t = \mathbf{x}) &:= \mathbb{P}(X_{t+h}^1 = y_1, X_{t+h}^2 = y_2, \dots, X_{t+h}^S = y_S | X_t = \mathbf{x})  \label{eq:factorized-path-1} \\
&= \prod_{i=1}^S \mathbb{P}(X_{t+h}^i = y^i | X_t = \mathbf{x}) \label{eq:factorized-path-2}
\end{align}
$$

Suppose that there is a probability velocity $u_t$ generating $(p_t)$.
Then, the probability velocity writes as a sum of $S$ *tokenwise* probability velocities $u_t^i$, from $\mathcal{V}^S$ to $\mathcal{V}$:

$$
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:factorized-velocity}
u_t(\mathbf{x})_{\mathbf{y}} = \sum_{i=1}^S \mathbb{I}(\mathbf{y}^{\neq i} = \mathbf{x}^{\neq i}) u_t^i(\mathbf{x})_{y^i}
\end{equation}
$$
where $\mathbf{x}^{\neq i} \in \mathcal{V}^{S-1}$ denotes the sequence except $\mathbf{x}$ except its $i$-th element $x^i$.
</Statement>


<details>
<summary>Proof</summary>
<div>
By definition, the transition probability for a single token at position $i$ is:
$$
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:token-transition}
\mathbb{P}(X_{t+h}^i = y^i | X_t = \mathbf{x}) = \mathbb{I}(y^i = x^i) + h u_t^i(\mathbf{x})_{y^i} + o(h)
\end{equation}
$$

Substituting this into our factorized joint probability, we get:
$$
\begin{align}
\mathbb{P}(X_{t+h} = \mathbf{y} | X_t = \mathbf{x}) &= \prod_{i=1}^S \left[ \mathbb{I}(y^i = x^i) + h u_t^i(\mathbf{x})_{y^i} + o(h) \right]  \label{eq:factorized-path-2-1} \\
&\hspace{-8em}{}= \prod_{i=1}^S \mathbb{I}(y^i = x^i) + h \sum_{i=1}^S \left[ \mathbb{I}(\mathbf{y}^{\neq i} = \mathbf{x}^{\neq i}) u_t^i(\mathbf{x})_{y^i} \right] + o(h)  \label{eq:factorized-path-2-2} \\
&\hspace{-8em}{}= \mathbb{I}(\mathbf{y} = \mathbf{x}) + h \sum_{i=1}^S \mathbb{I}(\mathbf{y}^{\neq i} = \mathbf{x}^{\neq i}) u_t^i(\mathbf{x})_{y^i} + o(h) \label{eq:factorized-path-2-3}
\end{align}
$$
where $\mathbb{I}(\mathbf{y}^{\neq i} = \mathbf{x}^{\neq i})$ equals 1 if $\mathbf{y}$ and $\mathbf{x}$ differ at most at position $i$, and we used $\prod_{i=1}^S \mathbb{I}(y^i = x^i) = \mathbb{I}(\mathbf{y} = \mathbf{x})$.

By comparing this result with the definition of the probability velocity (@eq:prob-velocity), we directly identify:
$$
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:factorized-velocity-2}
u_t(\mathbf{x})_{\mathbf{y}} = \sum_{i=1}^S \mathbb{I}(\mathbf{y}^{\neq i} = \mathbf{x}^{\neq i}) u_t^i(\mathbf{x})_{y^i}
\end{equation}
$$
</div>
</details>

It yields a substantial computational gain: instead of learning one transition object $u$ on the full sequence space $\mathcal{S}=\mathcal{V}^S$ (exponential in $S$), we learn $S$ local transition objects $u^i$, each on the vocabulary space $\mathcal{V}$.

Equipped with the links between probability paths and velocities, with our simplifying assumption on independence, we are now ready to build the probability path of Discrete Flow Matching!

#### Building the probability path

Similarly to Continuous Flow Matching, there are infinitely many possible choices for a probability path $(p_t)$ going from source $p_0$ to target $p_1$.
To build a specific one, we borrow the core idea from Continuous Flow Matching: we first define **conditional** probability paths for which the exact conditional velocity is known and easy to compute.
Then, we show that the resulting global velocity (obtained by marginalizing the conditional velocities) is guaranteed to generate a probability path that transports $p_0$ to $p_1$. All that's left is to learn it as a simple conditional expectation from data, which neural networks know how to do!

The conditioning variable $\mathbf{z} \in \mathcal{Z}^S$ that we use to build conditional paths and velocities can be either:
* **A target sample:** $\mathbf{z} = \mathbf{x}_1$, with $\mathcal{Z} = \mathcal{V}$ (useful when the source $p_0$ is a known, simple distribution like uniform or mask).
* **A source-target pair:** $\mathbf{z} = (\mathbf{x}_0, \mathbf{x}_1)$, with $\mathcal{Z} = \mathcal{V} \times \mathcal{V}$ (useful when transforming between two arbitrary datasets).

To simplify the exposition, we will use $\mathbf{z} = \mathbf{x}_1$.
Having chosen $\mathbf{z}$, we chose a conditional probability path.
In order to have factorized velocities (simpler to learn) as per @eq:factorized-velocity, we pick the conditional path to be factorized:
$$
\begin{align}
& p_t(\mathbf{x} | \mathbf{x_1}) = \prod_{i=1}^S  \big( \underbrace{ t \mathbb{I}(x^i = x_1^i) + (1 - t) \mathbb{I}(x^i = m) } _{:= q_t(x^j | x_1^j)} \big)  \label{eq:mask-path-1} \\
\end{align}
$$
This choice simply means that at time $t$, the $i$-th token $x^i_t$ equals the target token $x_1^i$ with probability $t$, or it is the mask token $m$ with probability $1 - t$:
$p_0(\cdot | \mathbf{x}_0)$ is a Dirac at the all-masked sequence, while $p_1(\cdot | \mathbf{x}_1)$ is a Dirac at $\mathbf{x}_1$.
The interest of this conditional probability path is that we can derive the associated probability velocity.

Since we've made the factorization assumption, we can derive the conditional velocity only token-wise $u_t(x^i | x_1^i)$. We say it generates the token-wise conditional path $q_t(x^i | x_1^i)$ if it satisfies token-wise Kolmogorov equation:
$$
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:token-kolmogorov}
\frac{d}{dt} q_t(y^{i} | x_1^{i}) = \sum_{v \in \mathcal{V} } u_{t}(v | x_1^{i})_{y^{i}} \, q_t(v | x_1^{i})
\end{equation}
$$
Then, if @eq:token-kolmogorov holds for all tokens $x_i \in \mathcal{V}$ and all $i \in \{1, \dots, S\}$, we can show that the conditional velocity defined as $u_t(\mathbf{x} | \mathbf{x}_1)_{\mathbf{y}} := \sum_{i=1}^S \mathbb{I}(\mathbf{y}^{\neq i} = \mathbf{x}^{\neq i}) u_t(x^i | x_1^i)_{y^i}$ generates the conditional path $p_t(\mathbf{x} | \mathbf{x}_1)$, as per @eq:kolmogorov-equation.

<details>
<summary>Proof</summary>
<div>

We verify that the global path and the global velocity satisfy the Kolmogorov equation. Since the conditional path is factorized, we have:
$$
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:conditional-factorized-path}
p_t(\mathbf{y} | \mathbf{x}_1) = \prod_{i=1}^S q_t(y^i | x_1^i).
\end{equation}
$$

Differentiating this product gives:
$$
\begin{align}
\frac{d}{dt}p_t(\mathbf{y} | \mathbf{x}_1)
&= \frac{d}{dt}\prod_{i=1}^S q_t(y^i | x_1^i) \label{eq:global-kolmogorov-proof-1} \\
&= \sum_{i=1}^S
\left(\frac{d}{dt}q_t(y^i | x_1^i)\right)
\prod_{j\neq i} q_t(y^j | x_1^j). \label{eq:global-kolmogorov-proof-2}
\end{align}
$$

Using the token-wise Kolmogorov equation @eq:token-kolmogorov, this becomes:
$$
\begin{align}
\frac{d}{dt}p_t(\mathbf{y} | \mathbf{x}_1)
&= \sum_{i=1}^S
\left[
\sum_{v\in\mathcal{V}}
u_t(v | x_1^i)_{y^i}\,q_t(v | x_1^i)
\right]
\prod_{j\neq i} q_t(y^j | x_1^j). \label{eq:global-kolmogorov-proof-3}
\end{align}
$$

We now compute the right-hand side of the global Kolmogorov equation using the factorized velocity @eq:factorized-velocity:
$$
\begin{align}
&\sum_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{S}} u_t(\mathbf{x} | \mathbf{x}_1)_{\mathbf{y}}\,p_t(\mathbf{x} | \mathbf{x}_1) \label{eq:global-kolmogorov-proof-4} \\
&= \sum_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{S}}
\left[
\sum_{i=1}^S \mathbb{I}(\mathbf{y}^{\neq i}=\mathbf{x}^{\neq i})\,u_t(x^i | x_1^i)_{y^i}
\right]
\prod_{j=1}^S q_t(x^j | x_1^j) \label{eq:global-kolmogorov-proof-5} \\
&= \sum_{i=1}^S
\sum_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{S}}
\mathbb{I}(\mathbf{y}^{\neq i}=\mathbf{x}^{\neq i})\,u_t(x^i | x_1^i)_{y^i}
\prod_{j=1}^S q_t(x^j | x_1^j). \label{eq:global-kolmogorov-proof-6}
\end{align}
$$

For a fixed coordinate $i$, the indicator $\mathbb{I}(\mathbf{y}^{\neq i}=\mathbf{x}^{\neq i})$ forces $x^j=y^j$ for every $j\neq i$. Therefore, the sum over $\mathbf{x}$ reduces to a sum over the single free token $x^i=v$:
$$
\begin{align}
&\sum_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{S}}
\mathbb{I}(\mathbf{y}^{\neq i}=\mathbf{x}^{\neq i})\,u_t(x^i | x_1^i)_{y^i}
\prod_{j=1}^S q_t(x^j | x_1^j) \label{eq:global-kolmogorov-proof-7} \\
&= \sum_{v\in\mathcal{V}}
u_t(v | x_1^i)_{y^i}\,q_t(v | x_1^i)
\prod_{j\neq i} q_t(y^j | x_1^j). \label{eq:global-kolmogorov-proof-8}
\end{align}
$$

Plugging this back into @eq:global-kolmogorov-proof-6 gives exactly @eq:global-kolmogorov-proof-3. Hence:
$$
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:global-kolmogorov-proof-9}
\frac{d}{dt}p_t(\mathbf{y} | \mathbf{x}_1)
=
\sum_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{S}} u_t(\mathbf{x} | \mathbf{x}_1)_{\mathbf{y}}\,p_t(\mathbf{x} | \mathbf{x}_1).
\end{equation}
$$

The pair $(p_t(\cdot | \mathbf{x}_1), u_t(\cdot | \mathbf{x}_1))$ satisfies the Kolmogorov equation.

</div>
</details>

<Statement kind="Proposition" label="conditional-velocity" title="Conditional velocity $u_t(x^i | x_1^i)$">
The token-wise $u_t(x^i | x_1^i)$ conditional velocity generating the token-wise conditional path $q_t(x^i | x_1^i)$ @eq:mask-path-1 is given by:
$$
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:mask-velocity}
u_t(x^i | x_1^i)_v = \begin{cases}
\frac{1}{1-t} & \text{if } v = x_1^i \text{ and } x^i = m, \\[0.3cm]
-\frac{1}{1-t} & \text{if } v = x^i = m, \\[0.3cm]
0 & \text{otherwise}.
\end{cases}
\end{equation}
$$
</Statement>

Remember that $u$ is a rate of change of probability: $u_t(x^i | x_1^i)_v$ quantifies the instantaneous probability flow from the current token $x^i$ toward any token $v$.
In the masked-source setting, the flow progressively removes mass from $m$ and transfers it to the target token $x_1^i$; if $x^i_t$ differs from $m$, then the conditional velocity is 0: the token remains at its value until the end.
As an example, if the vocabulary has 4 tokens $\{A, B, C, m\}$, and if the conditioning target is $x_1^i = B$, then the conditional velocity field is given by:
{/* */}
$$
u_t(x^i | x_1^i = B)_v = \begin{array}{c|cccc}
\begin{matrix} x^i  \\[-2pt] \diagdown \\[-2pt] v \end{matrix} & A & B & C & m \\
\hline
A & 0 & 0 & 0 & 0 \\[0.1cm]
B & 0 & 0 & 0 & \tfrac{1}{1-t} \\[0.1cm]
C & 0 & 0 & 0 & 0 \\[0.1cm]
m & 0 & 0 & 0 & -\tfrac{1}{1-t}
\end{array}
$$

<details>
<summary>Proof of Proposition <Ref label="prop:conditional-velocity" /></summary>
<div>

We need to verify that the token-wise velocity defined in @eq:mask-velocity satisfies the token-wise Kolmogorov equation @eq:token-kolmogorov. We start with the left-hand side.

Since $q_t(y^i | x_1^i) := t \mathbb{I}(y^i = x_1^i) + (1 - t) \mathbb{I}(y^i = m)$,
$$
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:mask-path-derivative}
\frac{d}{dt} q_t(y^{i} | x_1^{i}) = \mathbb{I}(y^{i} = x_1^{i}) - \mathbb{I}(y^{i} = m)
\end{equation}
$$

Now, let's examine the right-hand side using the analytic formulation of the velocity field $u_{t}(v | x_1^{i})_{y^{i}} = \frac{1}{1 - t} \left( \mathbb{I}(y^{i} = x_1^{i}) - \mathbb{I}(y^{i} = m) \right) \mathbb{I}(v = m)$:
$$
\begin{align}
&\sum_{v \in \mathcal{V} } u_t(v | x_1^i)_{y^i} \, q_t(v | x_1^{i})  \label{eq:mask-velocity-rhs-1} \\
&= \sum_{v \in \mathcal{V} } \tfrac{1}{1 - t} \left( \mathbb{I}(y^{i} = x_1^{i}) - \mathbb{I}(y^{i} = m) \right) \mathbb{I}(v = m) \, q_t(v | x_1^{i})  \label{eq:mask-velocity-rhs-2} \\
&= \tfrac{1}{1 - t} \left( \mathbb{I}(y^{i} = x_1^{i}) - \mathbb{I}(y^{i} = m) \right) q_t(m | x_1^{i})  \label{eq:mask-velocity-rhs-3} \\
&=  \mathbb{I}(y^{i} = x_1^{i}) - \mathbb{I}(y^{i} = m) \label{eq:mask-velocity-rhs-4}
\end{align}
$$

since $q_t(m | x_1^{i}) = (1-t)$.
Hence $(u_t(\cdot | x_1^i), q_t(\cdot | x_1^i))$ satisfies the Kolmogorov equation.


</div>
</details>


<details>
<summary>Values of conditional velocities for source target conditioning, and uniform source</summary>
<div>

**Source-Target Conditioning**:
Here, we condition on both the start and end points, so $z^i = (x_0^i, x_1^i)$. The token $x^i$ at time $t$ is simply chosen to be $x_1^i$ with probability $t$, and $x_0^i$ with probability $1 - t$:
$$
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:pair-path}
q_t(x^i | x_0^i, x_1^i) = t \mathbb{I}(x^i = x_1^i) + (1 - t) \mathbb{I}(x^i = x_0^i)
\end{equation}
$$

The conditional velocity field driving this transition is:
$$
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:pair-velocity}
u_t(x^i | x_0^i, x_1^i)_v = \begin{cases}
\tfrac{1}{1 - t} & \text{if } v = x_1^i \text{ and } x^i = x_0^i, \\
-\tfrac{1}{1 - t} & \text{if } v = x^i = x_0^i, \\
0 & \text{otherwise}.
\end{cases}
\end{equation}
$$

In the source-target setting, the flow moves mass directly from the source token $x_0^i$ toward the target token $x_1^i$, with no mask involved. All rows except the source row are identically zero — once a token is already at a state other than $x_0^i$, no transition occurs. Concretely, if the vocabulary has 3 tokens $\{1, 2, 3\}$, and $x_0^i = 1$, $x_1^i = 2$:

$$
u_t(x^i | x_0^i = 1,\, x_1^i = 2)_v = \begin{array}{c|ccc}
\begin{matrix} x^i \\[-2pt] \diagdown \\[-2pt] v \end{matrix} & 1 & 2 & 3 \\
\hline
1 & -\dfrac{1}{1-t} & \dfrac{1}{1-t} & 0 \\[0.3cm]
2 & 0 & 0 & 0 \\[0.3cm]
3 & 0 & 0 & 0
\end{array}
$$

**Uniform Source**: When $p_0$ is the uniform distribution, we can only condition on the target $z^i = x_1^i$. At time $t$, the token is the target $x_1^i$ with probability $t + (1 - t) \frac{1}{|\mathcal{V}|}$, or is drawn uniformly from other tokens in the vocabulary $\mathcal{V}$ with probability $(1 - t) \frac{1}{|\mathcal{V}|}$:
$$
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:uniform-path}
q_t(x^i | x_1^i) = t \mathbb{I}(x^i = x_1^i) + (1 - t) \frac{1}{|\mathcal{V}|}
\end{equation}
$$

The conditional velocity field driving this transition is:
$$
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:uniform-velocity}
u_t(x^i | x_1^i)_v = \begin{cases}
\tfrac{1}{1-t} & \text{if } v = x_1^i \text{ and } x^i \neq x_1^i \\[0.3cm]
-\tfrac{1}{1-t} & \text{if } v = x^i \neq x_1^i \\[0.3cm]
0 & \text{otherwise}
\end{cases}
\end{equation}
$$

Unlike the mask source, every non-target token can transition toward $x_1^i$, since the uniform source places initial mass everywhere. Concretely, if the vocabulary has 3 tokens $\{1, 2, 3\}$ and the conditioning target is $x_1^i = 2$:

$$
u_t(x^i | x_1^i = 2)_v = \begin{array}{c|ccc}
\begin{matrix} x^i \\[-2pt] \diagdown \\[-2pt] v \end{matrix} & 1 & 2 & 3 \\
\hline
1 & -\tfrac{1}{1-t} & \dfrac{1}{1-t} & 0 \\[0.1cm]
2 & 0 & 0 & 0 \\[0.1cm]
3 & 0 & \tfrac{1}{1-t} & -\tfrac{1}{1-t}
\end{array}
$$

#### From conditional to unconditional velocity

Now that we have defined our conditional probability paths and velocities, we can use them to build global, unconditional ones.
The overall distribution $p_t$ is obtained by marginalizing the conditional paths over the conditioning variable $\mathbf{z}$:
$$
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:marginal-path}
p_t(\mathbf{x}) := \sum_{\mathbf{z}} p_t(\mathbf{x} | \mathbf{z}) \pi(\mathbf{z})
\end{equation}
$$
where $\pi(\mathbf{z})$ is the distribution of $\mathbf{z}$.
In our case, $\mathbf{z} = \mathbf{x}_1$ and $\pi= p_1$; as the proof below shows, the path defined by marginalization indeed goes from $p_0$ to $p_1$.

<details>
<summary>Proof that path goes from $p_0$ to $p_1$</summary>
<div>

We verify the two boundary conditions separately:

**At $t=0$**:
Recall the conditional path with $\mathbf{z} = \mathbf{x}_1$ and mask source:
$$
q_0(x^i | x_1^i) = \mathbb{I}(x^i = m).
$$
The value of $x_1^i$ plays no role, so $p_0(\mathbf{x} | \mathbf{z})$ does not depend on $\mathbf{z}$:
$$
p_0(\mathbf{x} | \mathbf{z}) = \prod_{j=1}^S \mathbb{I}(x^j = m) = p_{\mathrm{mask}}(\mathbf{x}) = p_0(\mathbf{x}).
$$
Marginalizing over $\mathbf{z}$ then gives:
$$
p_{t=0}(\mathbf{x}) = \sum_{\mathbf{z}} p_0(\mathbf{x} | \mathbf{z})\,\pi(\mathbf{z}) = p_0(\mathbf{x})\sum_{\mathbf{z}} \pi(\mathbf{z}) = p_0(\mathbf{x}).
$$

**At $t=1$**:
We have:
$$
\begin{align}
q_1(x^i | x_1^i) &= \mathbb{I}(x^i = x_1^i)  \label{eq:endpoint-path-1} \\
p_1(\mathbf{x} | \mathbf{x}_1) &= \prod_{j=1}^S \mathbb{I}(x^j = x_1^j) = \mathbb{I}(\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{x}_1) \label{eq:endpoint-path-2}
\end{align}
$$
Since $\pi(\mathbf{z}) = p_{\mathrm{data}}(\mathbf{x}_1)$,
the indicator collapses the sum directly:
$$
p_{t=1}(\mathbf{x})
= \sum_{\mathbf{x}_1} \mathbb{I}(\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{x}_1)\, p_{\mathrm{data}}(\mathbf{x}_1)
= p_{\mathrm{data}}(\mathbf{x})
= p_1(\mathbf{x}).
$$

</div>
</details>

We now arrive to the heart of Discrete Flow Matching: building a velocity that transports $p_0$ to $p_1$.

<Statement kind="Theorem" label="fundamental-theorem-discrete-fm" title="Fundamental Theorem of Discrete FM">
The probability velocity obtained by taking the conditional expectation of the conditional velocities,
$$
\begin{align}
u_t(\mathbf{x})_{\mathbf{y}}
:= \mathbb{E}_{\substack{\mathbf{z}}}\!\left[u_t(\mathbf{x}|\mathbf{z})_{\mathbf{y}} | X_t = \mathbf{x}\right]  \label{eq:marginal-velocity-def-2}
&= \sum_{\mathbf{z}} u_t(\mathbf{x}|\mathbf{z})_{\mathbf{y}}\,\mathbb{P}(\mathbf{z} | X_t = \mathbf{x}) \, \label{eq:marginal-velocity-def-3}
\end{align}
$$
generates the probability path $p_t(\mathbf{x}) = \sum_{\mathbf{z}} p_t(\mathbf{x}|\mathbf{z})\,\pi(\mathbf{z})$.
</Statement>

<details>
<summary>Proof</summary>
<div>
We want to show
$$
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:marginal-velocity}
\mathbb{P}(X_{t+h}=\mathbf{y}| X_t=\mathbf{x})
= \mathbb{I}(\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{y}) + h\,u_t(\mathbf{x})_{\mathbf{y}} + o(h).
\end{equation}
$$
Introducing $Z$ and using the law of total probability,
$$
\begin{align}
\mathbb{P}(X_{t+h}=\mathbf{y}| X_t=\mathbf{x}) \label{eq:marginal-velocity-proof-1}
&= \sum_{\mathbf{z}} \mathbb{P}(X_{t+h}=\mathbf{y}, Z=\mathbf{z}| X_t=\mathbf{x}) \label{eq:marginal-velocity-proof-2} \\
&\hspace{-8em}{}= \sum_{\mathbf{z}} \mathbb{P}(X_{t+h}=\mathbf{y}| X_t=\mathbf{x}, Z=\mathbf{z})\,\mathbb{P}(Z=\mathbf{z}| X_t=\mathbf{x}) \label{eq:marginal-velocity-proof-3} \\
&\hspace{-8em}{}= \sum_{\mathbf{z}}\Big(\mathbb{I}(\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{y}) + h\,u_t(\mathbf{x}|\mathbf{z})_{\mathbf{y}} + o(h)\Big)\,\mathbb{P}(Z=\mathbf{z}| X_t=\mathbf{x}) \label{eq:marginal-velocity-proof-4} \\
&\hspace{-8em}{}= \mathbb{I}(\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{y}) + h\sum_{\mathbf{z}}u_t(\mathbf{x}|\mathbf{z})_{\mathbf{y}}\,\mathbb{P}(Z=\mathbf{z}| X_t=\mathbf{x}) + o(h) \label{eq:marginal-velocity-proof-5} \\
&\hspace{-8em}{}= \mathbb{I}(\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{y}) + h\,u_t(\mathbf{x})_{\mathbf{y}} + o(h). \label{eq:marginal-velocity-proof-6}
\end{align}
$$

**Alternative proof**: apply the Kolmogorov equation to each conditional process and average over $\mathbf{z}$:
$$
\begin{align}
\frac{d}{dt}p_t(\mathbf{y}) \label{eq:marginal-kolmogorov-1}
&= \sum_{\mathbf{z}} \pi(\mathbf{z})\,\frac{d}{dt}p_t(\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{z})  \label{eq:marginal-kolmogorov-2} \\
&= \sum_{\mathbf{z}}\pi(\mathbf{z})\sum_{\mathbf{x}}u_t(\mathbf{x}|\mathbf{z})_{\mathbf{y}}\,p_t(\mathbf{x}|\mathbf{z})  \label{eq:marginal-kolmogorov-3} \\
&= \sum_{\mathbf{x}}\left(\sum_{\mathbf{z}}u_t(\mathbf{x}|\mathbf{z})_{\mathbf{y}}\,\mathbb{P}(Z=\mathbf{z}| X_t=\mathbf{x})\right)p_t(\mathbf{x})  \label{eq:marginal-kolmogorov-4} \\
&= \sum_{\mathbf{x}} u_t(\mathbf{x})_{\mathbf{y}}\,p_t(\mathbf{x}). \label{eq:marginal-kolmogorov-5}
\end{align}
$$
So $u_t$ satisfies the Kolmogorov equation for $p_t$ and therefore generates $p_t$.
</div>
</details>

{/* If needed we can add the same figure u,ucond,p,pcond as the one in the CFM blogpost, because it's still valid.*/}

This theorem follows the exact same logic as in Continuous FM, and has the same practical consequence: since the target field is identified as a conditional expectation, it can be learned through a regression problem.

In continuous spaces, this is typically introduced via the $L^2$ projection view, as the conditional expectation solves
$$
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:l2-proj}
u_t(\mathbf{x}) = \underset{u \text{ measurable}}{\operatorname{argmin}}\ \mathbb{E}_{\substack{\mathbf{z} \sim \pi \\ \mathbf{x}_t \sim p_t(\cdot | \mathbf{z})}} \!\left[\left\|u_t(\mathbf{x}_t | \mathbf{z}) - u(\mathbf{x}_t)\right\|^2 \right]
\end{equation}
$$
In discrete spaces, since we compare nonneg­ative rate vectors that behave like local probabilities, a Kullback-Leibler style objective is more natural.
More generally, this conditional expectation is the minimizer
for any Bregman divergence, and KL<a href="#dfm-note-4" class="dfm-note-ref">4</a> is the Bregman choice adapted to our setting:
$$
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:kl-proj}
u_t(\mathbf{x}) ={} \underset{u}{\operatorname{argmin}}\ \mathbb{E}_{\substack{\mathbf{z} \sim \pi \\ \mathbf{x}_t \sim p_t(\cdot | \mathbf{z})}} \!\left[ \mathrm{KL} \left(u(\mathbf{x}_t | \mathbf{z}), u(\mathbf{x}_t)\right)  \right]
\end{equation}
$$

When applying it to probability velocities, we only compare the off-diagonal entries of $u_t(\mathbf{x})$, i.e. the rates $u_t(\mathbf{x})_{\mathbf{y}}$ for $\mathbf{y}\neq\mathbf{x}$.
These vectors are indeed positive (cf. @eq:velocity-positivity), and we can skip the diagonal entry since it is fixed by the other values, cf. @eq:velocity-normalization.

The exact conditional expectation is approximated by a neural network $u_{\theta,t}$, trained by minimizing over $\theta$:
$$
\begin{align}
L_{\text{CDFM}}(\theta)
&= \mathbb{E}_{\substack{t \sim \mathcal{U}(0,1) \\ \mathbf{z} \sim \pi \\ \mathbf{x}_t \sim p_t( \cdot | \mathbf{z})}}\left[ \mathrm{KL}\left(u_t(\mathbf{x}_t | \mathbf{z}), u_{\theta,t}(\mathbf{x}_t)\right) \right]  \label{eq:cdfm-2} \\
& =\mathbb{E}_{\substack{t \sim \mathcal{U}(0,1) \\ \mathbf{z} \sim \pi \\ \mathbf{x}_t \sim p_t( \cdot | \mathbf{z})}}\left[ \sum_{i=1}^S \mathrm{KL}\left( u_t(x^i_t | z^i), u_{\theta,t}^i(\mathbf{x}_t) \right) \right] \label{eq:cdfm-3}
\end{align}
$$

{/* **Learning with Conditional Velocity Fields**

To train our model, we use the KL divergence to measure the difference between velocity fields. The loss that minimizes the KL between the true marginal velocity $u_t$ and our learned velocity $u_{\theta,t}$ is:

$$
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:dfm-loss}
L_{\text{DFM}}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{\substack{t \sim \mathcal{U}(0,1) \\ \mathbf{x}_t \sim p_t}}\left[ \mathrm{KL}\left(u_t(\mathbf{x}_t), u_{\theta,t}(\mathbf{x}_t)\right) \right]
\end{equation}
$$

However, $u_t(\mathbf{x}_t)$ requires marginalizing over the entire dataset, making it computationally intractable. Following the approach of Continuous Flow Matching, we instead use the **conditional Discrete Flow Matching loss**:

$$
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:cdfm-loss}
L_{\text{CDFM}}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{\substack{t \sim \mathcal{U}(0,1) \\ \mathbf{z} \sim \pi \\ \mathbf{x}_t \sim p_t( \cdot | \mathbf{z})}}\left[ \mathrm{KL}\left(u_t(\mathbf{x}_t | \mathbf{z}), u_{\theta,t}(\mathbf{x}_t)\right) \right]
\end{equation}
$$

**Theorem.** There exists a constant $C$ independent of $\theta$ such that
$$

$$
In particular, both objectives have the same minimizers. */}


#### Learning with denoiser parameterization


In Continuous Flow Matching withs straight interpolation paths $x_t = (1-t) x_0 + tx_1$, learning the ideal velocity $\mathbb{E}[x_1 - x_0 | x_t=x]$ is mathematically equivalent to learning the ideal denoiser $\mathbb{E}[x_1 | x_t = x]$, since the two are linked via $\mathbb{E}[x_1 - x_0 | x_t=x] = \frac{1}{1 - t} ( \mathbb{E}[x_1 | x_t = x] - x)$.
It turns out the same type of denoiser-velocity equivalence holds in the discrete case:
$$
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:velocity-posterior}
u_t^i(\mathbf{x})_v = \frac{1}{1 - t} \left( \mathbb{P}(X_1^i = v | X_t = \mathbf{x}) - \mathbb{I}(v = x^i) \right)
\end{equation}
$$
where $\mathbb{P}(X_1^i = v | X_t = \mathbf{x})$ can be interpreted as a denoiser: it gives the probability of the $i$-th token in the final sequence $\mathbf{x}_1$ being $v$, given the full noisy sequence $\mathbf{x}_t$.

{/* As we saw in @eq:velocity-positivity and @eq:velocity-normalization, velocity fields follow very similar properties to probability distributions, and the KL divergence is a natural way to compare them.
Since it is more intuitive to think in terms of probabilities rather than velocities, we can reparameterize the velocity field $u_t^i(\mathbf{x})$ in terms of a denoiser $D_{\theta,t}^i(\mathbf{x}) = P_{\theta,t}(X_1^i | X_t = \mathbf{x})$. It corresponds to the probability distribution over clean tokens at position $i$, given the current noisy sequence $\mathbf{x}_t$. <span style="color: red">Discuss this argument</span> */}


<details>
<summary>Proof of velocity-denoiser equivalence</summary>
<div>

We write the proof for the masked source and start from @eq:mask-velocity.
In a compact form, it can be rewritten as:
$$
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:mask-velocity-token}
u_t^i(x^i | x_1^i)_v = \frac{1}{1-t}\,\mathbb{I}(x^i = m)\left(\mathbb{I}(v = x_1^i) - \mathbb{I}(v = m)\right).
\end{equation}
$$

**Case 1: $x^i \neq m$ (token already unmasked).**

The indicator $\mathbb{I}(x^i = m)=0$, so $u_t^i(x^i | x_1^i)_v = 0$ for all $x_1^i, v$
and hence $u_t^i(x)_v = 0$.

It remains to check that the target formula also gives zero. By Bayes' rule,
$$
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:posterior-bayes}
\mathbb{P}(X_1^i = v | X_t = \mathbf{x})
=
\frac{\mathbb{P}(X_t = \mathbf{x} | X_1^i = v)\,\mathbb{P}(X_1^i = v)}
{\mathbb{P}(X_t = \mathbf{x})}.
\end{equation}
$$
Under the conditional probability path,
$$
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:mask-token-path}
q_t(x^i | x_1^i)
=
t\,\mathbb{I}(x^i = x_1^i) + (1-t)\,\mathbb{I}(x^i = m) = t\,\mathbb{I}(x^i = x_1^i)
\end{equation}
$$
since $x^i \neq m$.
Therefore, if $v \neq x^i$, then
$$
\begin{align}
\mathbb{P}(X_t = \mathbf{x} |X_1^i = v) \label{eq:posterior-zero-1}
&= \label{eq:posterior-zero-2}
q_t(x^i | v)\,\mathbb{P}(X_t^{\neq i} = \mathbf{x}^{\neq i} |X_1^i = v)  \label{eq:posterior-zero-3} \\
&= 0. \label{eq:posterior-zero-4}
\end{align}
$$
and hence $\mathbb{P}(X_1^i = v |X_t = \mathbf{x})=0$.
Since the conditional probabilities sum to one over $v$ , we conclude that
$$
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:posterior-copy}
\mathbb{P}(X_1^i = v |X_t = \mathbf{x})=\mathbb{I}(v = x^i).
\end{equation}
$$
Substituting into the target formula gives
$$
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:velocity-zero}
\frac{1}{1-t}
\left(
\mathbb{P}(X_1^i = v |X_t = \mathbf{x}) - \mathbb{I}(v = x^i)
\right)
= 0.
\end{equation}
$$
Thus both expressions coincide in the case $x^i \neq m$.

**Case 2: $x^i = m$ (token is masked).**
Taking the expectation over $x_1^i$:
$$
\begin{align}
u_t^i(\mathbf{x})_v \label{eq:velocity-posterior-proof-1}
&= \mathbb{E}_{x_1^i}\!\left[\frac{1}{1-t}\left(\mathbb{I}(v = x_1^i) - \mathbb{I}(v = m)\right)\right]  \label{eq:velocity-posterior-proof-2} \\
&= \frac{1}{1-t}\left(\mathbb{P}(X_1^i = v | X_t = \mathbf{x}) - \mathbb{I}(v = m)\right)  \label{eq:velocity-posterior-proof-3} \\
&= \frac{1}{1-t}\left(\mathbb{P}(X_1^i = v | X_t = \mathbf{x}) - \mathbb{I}(v = x^i)\right), \label{eq:velocity-posterior-proof-4}
\end{align}
$$
where the last equality uses $x^i = m$. Both cases match the claimed formula.

</div>
</details>

Because the term $\mathbb{I}(v = x^i)$ is a known constant given the current state, learning the velocity field $u$ is mathematically exactly equivalent to learning the denoiser probability $\mathbb{P}(X_1^i = v | X_t = \mathbf{x})$.

Consequently, we can train a denoiser $P_{\theta, t}$ directly. Using the KL divergence between the true clean token distribution and the learned one, the loss simplifies to the standard cross-entropy:

$$
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:cdm-loss}
L_{\text{CDM}}(\theta) = -\mathbb{E}_{\substack{t \sim \mathcal{U}(0,1) \\ \mathbf{x}_1 \sim p_{\text{data}} \\ \mathbf{x} \sim p_t( \cdot | \mathbf{x}_1)}} \left[ \sum_{i=1}^S \log P_{\theta,t}(X_1^i = x_1^i | X_t = \mathbf{x}) \right]
\end{equation}
$$

<details>
<summary>Derivation</summary>

<div>

{/* We want to learn the denoiser by minimizing the KL divergence between the true posterior $P_t(X_1^i | X_t = \mathbf{x})$ and the learned one:

$$
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:posterior-kl}
L(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{\substack{t \sim \mathcal{U}(0,1) \\ \mathbf{x} \sim p_t}}\left[ \sum_{i=1}^S \mathrm{KL}\left(P_t(X_1^i | X_t = \mathbf{x}),\, P_{\theta,t}(X_1^i | X_t = \mathbf{x})\right) \right]
\end{equation}
$$
*/}

By the same argument as in @eq:kl-proj, the true denoiser is the KL projection of the conditional clean-token distribution:
$P_t(X_1^i | X_t = \mathbf{x})$ minimizes in $P$
$$
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:denoiser-kl-proj}
\mathbb{E}_{\substack{\mathbf{x}_1 \sim p_{\text{data}} \\ \mathbf{x} \sim p_t(\cdot | \mathbf{x}_1)}} \!\left[ \mathrm{KL}\left(P_t(X_1^i | X_t = \mathbf{x},\, \mathbf{x}_1),\, P(X_1^i | X_t = \mathbf{x})\right) \right]
\end{equation}
$$

Thus, we want to minimize:

$$
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:posterior-kl-cond}
L(\theta) \equiv \mathbb{E}_{\substack{t \sim \mathcal{U}(0,1) \\ \mathbf{x}_1 \sim p_{\text{data}} \\ \mathbf{x} \sim p_t( \cdot | \mathbf{x}_1)}} \left[ \sum_{i=1}^S \mathrm{KL}\left(P_t(X_1^i | X_t = \mathbf{x},\, \mathbf{x}_1),\, P_{\theta,t}(X_1^i | X_t = \mathbf{x})\right) \right]
\end{equation}
$$

Given $\mathbf{x}_1$, the true conditional posterior is a one-hot: $P_t(X_1^i | X_t = \mathbf{x}, \mathbf{x}_1) = \mathbf{e}_{x_1^i}$. The KL between a one-hot and any distribution reduces to a single log term:

$$
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:onehot-kl}
\mathrm{KL}\left(\mathbf{e}_{x_1^i},\, P_{\theta,t}(X_1^i | \mathbf{x})\right) = -\log P_{\theta,t}(X_1^i = x_1^i | \mathbf{x})
\end{equation}
$$

which gives directly:

$$
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:cdm-loss-2}
L_{\text{CDM}}(\theta) = -\mathbb{E}_{\substack{t \sim \mathcal{U}(0,1) \\ \mathbf{x}_1 \sim p_{\text{data}} \\ \mathbf{x} \sim p_t( \cdot | \mathbf{x}_1)}} \left[ \sum_{i=1}^S \log P_{\theta,t}(X_1^i = x_1^i | X_t = \mathbf{x}) \right]
\end{equation}
$$

### Variational Discrete Diffusion

Continuous space Diffusion models learn to generate data, e. g. images, by reversing a forward process that gradually corrupts data into pure noise through the addition of Gaussian noise.
In Discrete Diffusion, an analogous approach generates sequences by reversing a masking process that progressively replaces tokens with a mask token.

#### Forward Process

We first consider the forward process: we start from a clean sequence $\mathbf{x_0}$, and we progressively replace each token by the mask token, transforming the sequence into $\mathbf{x_1} = mm...m$.
Because each token is masked independently, the forward process factorizes over tokens, and we restrict to the case of a single token in the following.
We build the framework first in discrete time, with $T \in \mathbb{N}$ noising steps, and then take the continuous-time limit $T \to \infty$ to obtain time-continuous discrete space diffusion models. This avoids tying the model to a fixed number of noise steps.

In Continuous Denoising Diffusion Probabilistic Models (DDPM), we start by defining a noising process over $T$ steps, where each step slightly corrupts the signal by adding Gaussian noise:

$$
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:ddpm-step}
x_{j/T} = \sqrt{1 - \beta_j}\, x_{(j-1)/T} + \sqrt{\beta_j}\,\varepsilon, \qquad \varepsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I)
\end{equation}
$$

where $\beta_j \in [0,1]$ controls the amount of noise injected at step $j$.
In discrete space diffusion, the forward corruption takes a simpler binary form: at each step, a token either survives unchanged with probability $1 - \beta_j$, or is irreversibly replaced by the mask token $m$ with probability $\beta_j$:

$$
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:mdm-step}
x_{j/T} = \begin{cases}
x_{(j-1)/T}, & \text{with probability } 1 - \beta_j \\
m, & \text{with probability } \beta_j
\end{cases}
\end{equation}
$$

**Going to the continuous-time limit** ($T \to \infty$):
In continuous space diffusion, considering, for $t \in [0, 1]$, the result $x_t \in \mathbb{R}^d$ of applying applying $tT$ forward steps naturally makes the cumulative schedule $\alpha_t := \prod_{i=1}^{tT}(1 - \beta_i)$ appear.
Indeed, one has that the law of $x_t$ given its starting point $x_0$, namely the *forward marginal*, $q(x_t | x_0)$, equals $\mathcal{N}(x_t;\ \sqrt{\alpha_t}\, x_0,\ (1 - \alpha_t) I)$.
The cumulative schedule controls the signal-to-noise ratio, i.e how much of the clean data $x_0$ remains in $x_t$.
We derive its analogous counterpart in discrete space, where the same product gives the *survival probability* of a token.
Fix time $t = j/T$ for a given $j \in \mathbb{N}$.
By the Markov property, the probability that a token has survived up to time $t$ satisfies:

$$
\begin{align}
q(x_t = x_0 | x_0)
&= q(x_{j/T} = x_0 | x_0) \label{eq:survival-prob-1} \\
&= q(x_{j/T} = x_0 | x_{(j-1)/T} = x_0)\,
   q(x_{(j-1)/T} = x_0 | x_0) \label{eq:survival-prob-2} \\
&= (1-\beta_j)\,
   q(x_{(j-1)/T} = x_0 | x_0) \label{eq:survival-prob-3}
\end{align}
$$
which thus equals $\prod_{i=1}^{j} (1-\beta_i)$ by recursion.

To make the continuous-time limit rigorous, we introduce a continuous masking-rate function $\beta : [0,1] \to \mathbb{R}_+$.
When we discretize time over $T$ steps with stepsize $\Delta t = 1/T$, we obtained discrete masking rates $\beta_i := \beta(i/T)\Delta t = \frac{1}{T}\beta\left(\frac{i}{T}\right)$.

Then, the forward marginal equals, for $j=tT$,

$$
\begin{align}
q(x_t = x_0 | x_0)
&= \prod_{i=1}^{j}
\left(1- \tfrac{1}{T}\beta\left(\tfrac{i}{T}\right)\right) \label{eq:survival-prob-5} \\
&= \exp\left(
\sum_{i=1}^{j}
\log\left(1-\tfrac{1}{T}\beta\left(\tfrac{i}{T}\right)\right)
\right) \label{eq:survival-prob-6} \\
&\approx \exp\left(
-\sum_{i=1}^{j}
\tfrac{1}{T}\beta\left(\tfrac{i}{T}\right)
\right) \label{eq:survival-prob-7} \\
&\xrightarrow[T\to\infty]{}
\exp\left(-\int_0^t \beta(s)\, ds\right). \label{eq:survival-prob-8}
\end{align}
$$
identifying a Riemann sum and using $j = Tt$.

This computation leads us to define the noise schedule $\alpha_t$ that gives the probability of a token remaining unmasked at time $t$, as

$$
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:alpha-def}
\alpha_t = \exp\!\left(-\int_0^t \beta(s)\, ds\right)
\end{equation}
$$

We deduce the following properties on $\alpha_t$ from the derivation above:
- $\alpha_0 = 1$: at time $t=0$, all tokens are unmasked.
- $\alpha_1 = 0$: at time $t=1$, all tokens are masked.
- $\alpha_t$ is decreasing in $t$: as time increases, the probability of a token remaining unmasked decreases.

It turns out that we can then compute the transition probabilities between any two timesteps $s < t$:
$$
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:forward-transition}
x_t = \begin{cases}
x_s, & \text{with probability } \frac{\alpha_t}{\alpha_s} \\
m, & \text{with probability } 1 - \frac{\alpha_t}{\alpha_s}
\end{cases}
\end{equation}
$$

<details>
<summary>Proof</summary>
<div>

By the Markov property, the marginal $q(x_t | x_0)$ factorizes through any intermediate time $s$:

$$
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:marginal-recursion}
q(x_t | x_0) = \sum_{x_s} q(x_t | x_s)\, q(x_s | x_0)
\end{equation}
$$

Using $q(x_s | x_0) = \alpha_s\, \mathbb{I}(x_s = x_0) + (1 - \alpha_s)\, \mathbb{I}(x_s = m)$, the sum simplifies to:

$$
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:marginal-split}
q(x_t | x_0) = \alpha_s\, q(x_t | x_s = x_0) + (1 - \alpha_s)\, q(x_t | x_s = m)
\end{equation}
$$

Since masking is irreversible, $q(x_t | x_s = m) = \mathbb{I}(x_t = m)$.
Writing $q(x_t | x_s = x_0) = a\, \mathbb{I}(x_t = x_0) + (1 - a)\, \mathbb{I}(x_t = m)$, for $x_t = x_0$, @eq:marginal-split becomes:  $\alpha_t = \alpha_s \cdot a$, so $a = \dfrac{\alpha_t}{\alpha_s}$

Therefore, for an unmasked token $x_s \neq m$ (since the probabilities sum to 1):

$$
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:masked-transition}
q(x_t | x_s) = \frac{\alpha_t}{\alpha_s}\, \mathbb{I}(x_t = x_s) + \left(1 - \frac{\alpha_t}{\alpha_s}\right) \mathbb{I}(x_t = m)
\end{equation}
$$

</div>
</details>

#### Reverse Process

Generating data requires a reverse process that progressively unmasks tokens to produce a clean sequence. We first derive the exact reverse transition conditioned on the initial clean token $x_0$.

<Statement kind="Lemma" label="masked-diffusion-bridge" title="Conditional reverse transitions $q(x_s | x_t,x_0)$">
For Masked Diffusion, $x_t = m$ and $s<t$, we have:
$$
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:bridge-mask}
q(x_s | x_t, x_0) = \left[ \frac{\alpha_s - \alpha_t}{1 - \alpha_t} \mathbb{I}(x_s = x_0) + \frac{1 - \alpha_s}{1 - \alpha_t} \mathbb{I}(x_s = m) \right]
\end{equation}
$$
Equivalently:
$$
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:bridge-mask-sampling}
x_s = \begin{cases}
x_0, & \text{with probability } \dfrac{\alpha_s - \alpha_t}{1 - \alpha_t} \\
m, & \text{with probability } \dfrac{1 - \alpha_s}{1 - \alpha_t}
\end{cases}
\end{equation}
$$
</Statement>

<details>
<summary>Proof</summary>
<div>

We use Bayes' theorem to compute the probability that a masked token at time $t$ reverts to the clean token $x_0$ by time $s < t$:
$$
\begin{align}
\mathbb{P}(x_s = x_0 | x_0, x_t = m) &= \frac{\mathbb{P}(x_t = m | x_s = x_0)\, \mathbb{P}(x_s = x_0 | x_0)}{\mathbb{P}(x_t = m | x_0)}  \label{eq:bridge-bayes-1} \\
&= \frac{\left(1 - \dfrac{\alpha_t}{\alpha_s}\right) \alpha_s}{1 - \alpha_t}  \label{eq:bridge-bayes-2} \\
&= \frac{\alpha_s - \alpha_t}{1 - \alpha_t} \label{eq:bridge-bayes-3}
\end{align}
$$
We conclude using that $x_s$ is either at state $x_0$ or at state $m$ with probabilities that sum to 1.

</div>
</details>

However, during generation, the clean token $x_0$ is unknown: the quantity we actually need in order to sample backward is the reverse transition $q(x_s | x_t)$, not $q(x_s | x_t, x_0)$. We obtain the former by marginalizing the latter over all possible clean tokens, weighted by their probability $\mathbb{P}(X_0 = v | X_t=x_t)$ (the true denoiser). We denote this posterior by
$$
D_t(x_t)_v := \mathbb{P}(X_0=v | X_t=x_t),
\qquad
D_t^i(\mathbf{x}_t)_v := \mathbb{P}(X_0^i=v | \mathbf{X}_t=\mathbf{x}_t).
$$

<Statement kind="Lemma" label="masked-diffusion-denoiser-bridge" title="Reverse transitions $q(x_s | x_t)$">
For Masked Diffusion and timesteps $s<t$:
$$
\begin{align}
    q(x_s=v| x_t=m) &= \frac{\alpha_s-\alpha_t}{1-\alpha_t}\,\mathbb{P}(X_0=v | X_t=x_t)\quad (v\in\mathcal{V}\setminus\{m\}),  \label{eq:bridge-denoiser-1} \\
    q(x_s=m| x_t=m) &= \frac{1-\alpha_s}{1-\alpha_t}. \label{eq:bridge-denoiser-2}
\end{align}
$$
</Statement>

<details>
<summary>Proof</summary>
<div>

By the law of total probability:
$$
\begin{align}
q(x_s=y | x_t=m)
&= \sum_{v\in\mathcal{V}} q(x_s=y | x_t=m, x_0=v)\,\mathbb{P}(X_0=v | X_t=x_t). \label{eq:denoiser-bridge-proof-1}
\end{align}
$$

If $y\in\mathcal{V}$, @eq:bridge-mask gives:
$$
\begin{align}
q(x_s=y | x_t=m)
&= \sum_{v\in\mathcal{V}}
\frac{\alpha_s-\alpha_t}{1-\alpha_t}\mathbb{I}(y=v)\,\mathbb{P}(X_0=v | X_t=x_t) \label{eq:denoiser-bridge-proof-token-1} \\
&= \frac{\alpha_s-\alpha_t}{1-\alpha_t}\,\mathbb{P}(X_0=y | X_t=x_t). \label{eq:denoiser-bridge-proof-token-2}
\end{align}
$$

For the mask token, @eq:bridge-mask gives a term independent of $v$, hence:
$$
\begin{align}
q(x_s=m | x_t=m)
&= \sum_{v\in\mathcal{V}}
\frac{1-\alpha_s}{1-\alpha_t}\,\mathbb{P}(X_0=v | X_t=x_t) \label{eq:denoiser-bridge-proof-mask-1} \\
&= \frac{1-\alpha_s}{1-\alpha_t}, \label{eq:denoiser-bridge-proof-mask-2}
\end{align}
$$
because $\mathbb{P}(X_0=\cdot | X_t=x_t)$ is a probability distribution over $\mathcal{V}$.

</div>
</details>

This directly gives the sampling process from a fully masked sequence toward a clean sequence: for $s<t$, we sample each still masked position $i$ as
$$
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:masked-diffusion-denoiser-sampling}
x_s^i \sim \mathrm{Cat}\left(
\frac{\alpha_s-\alpha_t}{1-\alpha_t}\mathbb{P}(X_0^i=\cdot | \mathbf{X}_t=\mathbf{x}_t)
+ \frac{1-\alpha_s}{1-\alpha_t}\mathbf{e}_m
\right).
\end{equation}
$$
When a token is already unmasked, the reverse process simply keeps it fixed.
Note that this is very similar to the approach followed in DDPM: in continuous space, given a noisy image at time $t$, we predict the clean image (or the added noise) that could have generated it and use it to take one denoising step from time $t$ to $s$; while in discrete space, for each masked token in the sentence, we predict the distribution of clean tokens that are likely to be at this position and use these predictions to sample one denoising step from time $t$ to $s$.

#### Training

We learn the reverse process by training a neural network whose generated trajectories align with those of the forward process. In Variational Diffusion, this is done by minimizing the KL divergence between the distribution induced by the (known) forward process $q(x_{0:T})$ and the one induced by the learned backward process $p_\theta(x_{0:T})$:

$$
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:path-kl}
\min_\theta\; \mathrm{KL}\!\Big(q(x_{0:T}),\, p_\theta(x_{0:T})\Big)
\end{equation}
$$

We can show that:

$$
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:path-kl-2}
\min_\theta\; \mathrm{KL}\!\Big(q(x_{0:T}),\, p_\theta(x_{0:T})\Big)
\;\iff\;
\max_\theta\; \mathbb{E}_{q(x_0)}\!\left[\text{ELBO}_{\theta}(x_0)\right]
\end{equation}
$$

where the per-sample ELBO is defined as:

$$
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:elbo}
\text{ELBO}_{\theta}(x_0) = \mathbb{E}_{x_{1:T} \sim q(\cdot | x_0)} \left[ \log p_\theta(x_{0:T}) - \log q(x_{1:T}|x_0) \right]
\end{equation}
$$

<details>
<summary>Equivalence of KL minimization and ELBO maximization</summary>
<div>

Expand the KL by factoring $q(x_{0:T}) = q(x_0)\, q(x_{1:T}|x_0)$:

$$
\begin{align}
\mathrm{KL}\!\Big(q(x_{0:T}),\, p_\theta(x_{0:T})\Big) &= \mathbb{E}_{q(x_{0:T})}\!\left[\log \frac{q(x_{0:T})}{p_\theta(x_{0:T})}\right]  \label{eq:kl-elbo-proof-2} \\

&\hspace{-8em}{}= \mathbb{E}_{q(x_0)}\!\left[\log q(x_0)\right] + \mathbb{E}_{q(x_0)}\!\left[\mathbb{E}_{q(x_{1:T}|x_0)}\!\left[\log q(x_{1:T}|x_0) - \log p_\theta(x_{0:T})\right]\right]  \label{eq:kl-elbo-proof-4} \\
&\hspace{-8em}{}= -H(q) - \mathbb{E}_{q(x_0)}\!\left[\text{ELBO}_{\theta}(x_0)\right] \label{eq:kl-elbo-proof-6}
\end{align}
$$

Since $H(q) = -\mathbb{E}_{q(x_0)}[\log q(x_0)]$ does not depend on $\theta$, minimizing
the KL is exactly equivalent to maximizing $\mathbb{E}_{q(x_0)}[\text{ELBO}_{\theta}(x_0)]$.

</div>

The ELBO for a Discrete Diffusion process is given by:

$$
\begin{align}
\text{ELBO}_{\theta}(x_0) ={}& - \mathrm{KL}(q(x_1 | x_0) , p_{\text{source}})  \label{eq:discrete-elbo-1} \\
& - \sum_{j=1}^{T} \mathbb{E}_{x_{j/T} \sim q( \cdot | x_0)} \left[ \mathrm{KL}\!\left(q\!\left(x_{(j-1)/T} | x_{j/T}, x_0\right),\, p_\theta\!\left(x_{(j-1)/T} | x_{j/T}\right)\right) \right]  \label{eq:discrete-elbo-2} \notag
\end{align}
$$
</Statement>

<details>
<summary>Proof</summary>
<div>

The forward process follows a Markovian structure:

$$
\begin{align}
    q(x_{1:T}|x_0) &= \prod_{j=1}^{T} q(x_{j/T} | x_{(j-1)/T}, x_{(j-2)/T}, \dots, x_0)  \label{eq:forward-markov-1} \\
    &= \prod_{j=1}^{T} q(x_{j/T} | x_{(j-1)/T}, x_0) \label{eq:forward-markov-2}
\end{align}
$$

where the last equality holds because the forward process is memoryless given the previous states. Similarly, the reverse model decomposes as:

$$
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:reverse-factorization}
    p_\theta(x_{0:T}) = p_\theta(x_1) \prod_{j=1}^{T} p_\theta(x_{(j-1)/T} | x_{j/T}).
\end{equation}
$$

Substituting into the ELBO:

$$
\begin{align}
    \text{ELBO}_{\theta}(x_0) &= \mathbb{E}_{\substack{x_{1:T} \sim q( \cdot | x_0)}} \left[ \log p_\theta(x_1) \right]  \label{eq:elbo-expand-1} \\
    &\quad + \sum_{j=1}^{T} \mathbb{E}_{\substack{x_{1:T} \sim q( \cdot | x_0)}} \left[ \log p_\theta(x_{(j-1)/T} | x_{j/T}) - \log q(x_{j/T} | x_{(j-1)/T}, x_0) \right]  \label{eq:elbo-expand-2} \notag
\end{align}
$$

Using Bayes' theorem, we can rewrite the forward process conditional:

$$
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:elbo-sum}
\begin{aligned}
    \log q(x_{j/T} | x_{(j-1)/T}, x_0) &= \log q(x_{(j-1)/T} | x_{j/T}, x_0) + \log q(x_{j/T} | x_0) \\
    &\quad - \log q(x_{(j-1)/T} | x_0)
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
$$

Substituting into the ELBO and observing that consecutive terms telescope:

$$
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:elbo-mask}
\begin{aligned}
    \text{ELBO}_{\theta}(x_0) &= \mathbb{E}_{\substack{x_{1:T} \sim q( \cdot | x_0)}} \left[ \log q(x_1 | x_0) - \log q(x_0 | x_0) \right] \\
    &\quad + \mathbb{E}_{\substack{x_{1:T} \sim q( \cdot | x_0)}} \left[ \log p_\theta(x_1) \right] \\
    &\quad + \sum_{j=1}^{T} \mathbb{E}_{\substack{x_{1:T} \sim q( \cdot | x_0)}} \left[ \log p_\theta(x_{(j-1)/T} | x_{j/T}) - \log q(x_{(j-1)/T} | x_{j/T}, x_0) \right]
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
$$

Since $\log q(x_0 | x_0) = \log(1) = 0$:

$$
\begin{align}
    \text{ELBO}_{\theta}(x_0) &= - \mathbb{E}_{\substack{x_{1:T} \sim q( \cdot | x_0)}} \left[ \log q(x_1 | x_0) - \log p_\theta(x_1) \right] \notag \\
    &\quad - \sum_{j=1}^{T} \mathbb{E}_{\substack{x_{1:T} \sim q( \cdot | x_0)}} \big[ \log q(x_{(j-1)/T} | x_{j/T}, x_0) - \log p_\theta(x_{(j-1)/T} | x_{j/T}) \big] \label{eq:elbo-rewrite}
\end{align}
$$

We simplify by marginalizing over all intermediate variables. For the first term:

$$
\begin{align}
    &\mathbb{E}_{\substack{x_{1:T} \sim q( \cdot | x_0)}} \left[ \log q(x_1 | x_0) - \log p_\theta(x_1) \right] \label{eq:elbo-continuous} \\
    &= \mathbb{E}_{\substack{x_1 \sim q( \cdot | x_0)}} \left[ \log q(x_1 | x_0) - \log p_\theta(x_1) \right] \notag
\end{align}
$$

For each term in the sum:

$$
\begin{align}
    &\mathbb{E}_{\substack{x_{1:T} \sim q( \cdot | x_0)}} \left[ \log q(x_{(j-1)/T} | x_{j/T}, x_0) - \log p_\theta(x_{(j-1)/T} | x_{j/T}) \right]  \label{eq:posterior-exp-1} \\
    &= \mathbb{E}_{\substack{x_{j/T} \sim q( \cdot | x_0)}} \bigg[ \mathbb{E}_{\substack{x_{(j-1)/T} \sim q( \cdot | x_{j/T}, x_0)}}  \label{eq:posterior-exp-2} \\
    &\qquad \left[ \log q(x_{(j-1)/T} | x_{j/T}, x_0) - \log p_\theta(x_{(j-1)/T} | x_{j/T}) \right] \bigg] \notag
\end{align}
$$

Therefore:

$$
\begin{align}
    \text{ELBO}_{\theta}(x_0) &= - \mathrm{KL}\left(q(x_1|x_0) , p_{\text{source}}\right)  \label{eq:discrete-elbo-proof-1} \\
    &\quad - \sum_{j=1}^{T} \mathbb{E}_{\substack{x_{j/T} \sim q( \cdot | x_0)}} \left[ \mathrm{KL}\left(q(x_{(j-1)/T} | x_{j/T}, x_0) , p_\theta(x_{(j-1)/T} | x_{j/T})\right) \right]  \label{eq:discrete-elbo-proof-2} \notag
\end{align}
$$

where the first KL term is a constant independent of $\theta$, since $p_\theta(x_1) = p_{\text{source}}$ is a fixed base distribution.

</div>
</details>

The sum of KL terms corresponds to a forward trajectory where $x_0, x_{1/T}, \ldots, x_1$ is sampled from the noising process, and at each step the learned reverse transition $p_\theta(x_{(j-1)/T} | x_{j/T})$ is trained to match the reverse transition conditioned on the initial clean token $x_0$: $q(x_{(j-1)/T} | x_{j/T}, x_0)$.
Using this ELBO for training therefore requires learning the transitions $p_\theta(x_s | x_t)$ for all times $s<t$.

However, because this is a complicated function of two variables, we use @eq:bridge-denoiser-1 to rewrite these transitions in terms of the denoiser $\mathbb{P}(X_0 = v | X_t=x_t)$. Since this denoiser is also intractable, we approximate it by a learned denoiser $D_{\theta,t}(x_t) \in \Delta(\mathcal{V})$.
(For simplicity, we consider $[D_{\theta,t}(x_t)]_m = 0$.)
This definition naturally extends to each position $i$ if we consider sequences: $[D_{\theta,t}^i(\mathbf{x}_t)]_v \approx \mathbb{P}(X_0^i = v | \mathbf{X}_t = \mathbf{x}_t)$.

Equivalently, the learned reverse transition is obtained by replacing the true denoiser in @eq:bridge-denoiser-1 with the learned one $D_{\theta,t}$:
$$
\begin{align}
    p_\theta(x_s=v| x_t=m) &= \frac{\alpha_s-\alpha_t}{1-\alpha_t}\,[D_{\theta,t}(x_t)]_v\quad (v\in\mathcal{V}\setminus\{m\}),  \label{eq:bridge-model-1} \\
    p_\theta(x_s=m| x_t=m) &= \frac{1-\alpha_s}{1-\alpha_t}. \label{eq:bridge-model-2}
\end{align}
$$
The following proposition rewrites the ELBO term entirely in terms of this denoiser.

<Statement kind="Proposition" label="masked-diffusion-training-loss" title="Masked Diffusion training loss">
In the specific case of Masked Diffusion, we can express the loss in terms of the denoiser output $D_{\theta,t}(\mathbf{x}_t) = (D_{\theta,t}^1(\mathbf{x}_t), \ldots, D_{\theta,t}^S(\mathbf{x}_t)) \in \Delta(\mathcal{V})^S$:

$$
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:mdm-loss}
L(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{\substack{\mathbf{x}_0 \sim p_{\text{data}} \\ x_t^i \sim q( \cdot | x_0^i)}} \sum_{i=1}^{S} \left[ \mathbb{I}(x_t^i = m) \left( -\frac{\dot{\alpha}_t}{1 - \alpha_t} \mathbf{x}_0^{i \top} \log D_{\theta,t}^i(\mathbf{x}_t) \right) \right]
\end{equation}
$$
</Statement>

<details>
<summary>Proof</summary>
<div>

We write the proof only for one token, starting from @eq:discrete-elbo-1. Since $x_1 = m$ (fully masked at $t=1$), we have $\mathrm{KL}(q(x_1|x_0) , p_\theta(x_1)) = 0$. Therefore:
$$
\begin{align}
&\text{ELBO}_{\theta}(x_0) = {} \label{eq:mdm-elbo-no-source} \\
&- \sum_{j=1}^{T} \mathbb{E}_{\substack{x_{j/T} \sim q( \cdot | x_0)}} \left[ \mathrm{KL}\left(q(x_{(j-1)/T} | x_{j/T}, x_0) , p_\theta(x_{(j-1)/T} | x_{j/T})\right) \right] \notag
\end{align}
$$

The KL divergence can be expanded as a sum over all possible values of $x_{(j-1)/T} \in \mathcal{V}$:
$$
\begin{align}
& \mathrm{KL}\left(q(x_{(j-1)/T} | x_{j/T}, x_0) , p_\theta(x_{(j-1)/T} | x_{j/T})\right) \label{eq:bridge-kl} \\
& \quad = \sum_{x_{(j-1)/T} \in \mathcal{V}} q(x_{(j-1)/T} | x_{j/T}, x_0) \log \frac{q(x_{(j-1)/T} | x_{j/T}, x_0)}{p_\theta(x_{(j-1)/T} | x_{j/T})} \notag
\end{align}
$$

When $x_t\neq m$, both $q(x_s| x_t,x_0)$ and $p_\theta(x_s| x_t)$ put all their mass on $x_s=x_t=x_0$, so the KL term is zero.

Hence only the case $x_t=m$ contributes. Therefore, using @eq:bridge-mask together with the learned reverse transition @eq:bridge-model-1 and @eq:bridge-model-2,

$$
\begin{align}
    &\mathrm{KL}\!\left(q(\cdot| x_t,x_0)\,\|\,p_\theta(\cdot| x_t)\right) \notag \\
    &= \mathbb{I}(x_t=m)
    \sum_{x_s \in \mathcal{V}\cup\{m\}}
    q(x_s| x_t,x_0)\,
    \log\frac{q(x_s| x_t,x_0)}{p_\theta(x_s| x_t)} \label{eq:bridge-kl-proof-2} \\
    &= \mathbb{I}(x_t=m)
    \sum_{x_s \in \mathcal{V}\cup\{m\}}
    \left[
        \frac{\alpha_s-\alpha_t}{1-\alpha_t}\mathbb{I}(x_s=x_0)
        + \frac{1-\alpha_s}{1-\alpha_t}\mathbb{I}(x_s=m)
    \right] \label{eq:bridge-kl-proof-3} \\
    &\quad \log
    \frac{
        \frac{\alpha_s-\alpha_t}{1-\alpha_t}\mathbb{I}(x_s=x_0)
        + \frac{1-\alpha_s}{1-\alpha_t}\mathbb{I}(x_s=m)
    }{
        \frac{\alpha_s-\alpha_t}{1-\alpha_t}[D_{\theta,t}(x_t)]_{x_s}\mathbb{I}(x_s\neq m)
        + \frac{1-\alpha_s}{1-\alpha_t}\mathbb{I}(x_s=m)
    } \notag \\
    &= \mathbb{I}(x_t=m)\Bigg[
    \frac{\alpha_s-\alpha_t}{1-\alpha_t}
    \log\frac{\frac{\alpha_s-\alpha_t}{1-\alpha_t}}
    {\frac{\alpha_s-\alpha_t}{1-\alpha_t}[D_{\theta,t}(x_t)]_{x_0}}
    +\frac{1-\alpha_s}{1-\alpha_t}
    \underbrace{\log\frac{\frac{1-\alpha_s}{1-\alpha_t}}
    {\frac{1-\alpha_s}{1-\alpha_t}}}_{=0}
    \Bigg] \label{eq:bridge-kl-proof-4} \\
    &= \mathbb{I}(x_t=m)\,\frac{\alpha_s-\alpha_t}{1-\alpha_t}\,
    \big(-\log [D_{\theta,t}(x_t)]_{x_0}\big). \label{eq:bridge-kl-proof-5}
\end{align}
$$

Plugging this into the ELBO yields:
$$
\begin{align}
&\text{ELBO}_{\theta}(x_0) = {} \label{eq:mdm-elbo-sum-1} \\
&\sum_{j=1}^T \mathbb{E}_{\substack{x_{j/T} \sim q( \cdot | x_0)}} \left[
    \mathbb{I}(x_{j/T}=m)\,\frac{\alpha_{(j-1)/T}-\alpha_{j/T}}{1-\alpha_{j/T}}
    \,x_0^\top \log D_{\theta, j/T}(x_{j/T})\right]. \notag
\end{align}
$$

Passing to the continuous-time limit $T \to \infty$ with $t=j/T$, $s=(j-1)/T$, and $\alpha_s-\alpha_t \approx -\dot{\alpha}_t\,\frac{1}{T}$, the sum is a Riemann sum:
$$
\begin{align}
    \text{ELBO}_{\theta}(x_0) \label{eq:mdm-elbo-ct-1}
    = \int_0^1 \mathbb{E}_{\substack{x_t \sim q( \cdot | x_0)}}\left[ \label{eq:mdm-elbo-ct-2}
    \mathbb{I}(x_t=m)\,\frac{-\dot{\alpha}_t}{1-\alpha_t}\,x_0^\top\log D_{\theta,t}(x_t) \label{eq:mdm-elbo-ct-3}
    \right]dt, \label{eq:mdm-elbo-ct-4}
\end{align}
$$
which matches the sequence-level training loss defined previously.

</details>

### Score-based Discrete Diffusion

We now turn to the score-based viewpoint: predict a score function, i.e. the direction pointing toward more likely samples at noise level $t$, and follow it to move from noise back to data. In Continuous space Diffusion, the sampling process is given by the reverse-time SDE $dx_t = [f_t(x_t) - g_t^2\nabla_x\log p_t(x_t)]dt + g_td\bar{W}_t$, where $f_t$ and $g_t$ are known and $W_t$ is a Wiener process. The score $\nabla \log p_t(x)$ is then the only quantity that we need to learn to generate new samples from $p_0(x)$.

We follow the same path in discrete space. Since there is no SDE here, we work with the first-order expansion of $q(x_{t+\Delta t} | x_t)$ as $\Delta t \to 0$, which plays the role of $dx_t$. We then reverse it to obtain $p(x_{t-\Delta t} | x_t)$, and identify the discrete object analogous to the score $\nabla \log p_t(x)$ that the model needs to learn.

#### Forward Process

In score-based discrete diffusion, the discrete forward process is modeled as a continuous-time Markov chain (CTMC), characterized locally by a *forward rate matrix* $\dot{Q}_t \in \mathbb{R}^{|\mathcal{V}| \times |\mathcal{V}|}$.

<Statement kind="Definition" label="forward-rate-matrix" title="Forward rate matrix $\dot{Q}_t$">
A forward rate matrix $\dot{Q}_t$ is the infinitesimal generator of the CTMC. For a small time increment $\Delta t$, it defines the transition probabilities through:

$$
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:forward-rate}
q(x_{t+\Delta t} = y | x_t) = \mathbb{I}(y = x_t) + \Delta t\, [\dot{Q}_t(x_t)]_y + o(\Delta t)
\end{equation}
$$

where $[\dot{Q}_t(x_t)]_y$ denotes the entry of $\dot{Q}_t$ at row $x_t$ and column $y$.
</Statement>

{/* The matrix $\dot{Q}_t$ plays the same role for the discrete forward process as $f_t$ plays for the continuous one.  */}
In Continuous space Diffusion, the SDE $dx_t = f_t(x_t)\,dt + g_t\,dW_t$ says that, over an infinitesimal time $dt$, a particle at $x_t$ moves by a deterministic drift $f_t(x_t)\,dt$ plus an independent Gaussian increment of standard deviation $g_t\sqrt{dt}$. Here, $[\dot{Q}_t(x_t)]_y$ is the instantaneous rate at which a particle at state $x_t$ jumps to state $y$: over a small interval $\Delta t$, the probability of such a jump is $\Delta t\,[\dot{Q}_t(x_t)]_y$ to first order.<a href="#dfm-note-1" class="dfm-note-ref">1</a>

For example, in the variance-preserving Gaussian diffusion, the coefficients are often chosen as $f_t(x_t) = -\frac{1}{2}\beta(t)x_t$ and $g_t = \sqrt{\beta(t)}$, where $\beta(t)$ is a noise schedule. In the masked discrete space Diffusion, we make an analogous choice by specifying the rate at which unmasked tokens jump to the mask state:

$$
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:q-dot-def}
\dot{Q}_t
= \sigma(t) (\mathbf{1}\mathbf{e}_m^\top - I)
= \sigma(t)
\begin{pmatrix}
-1 & 0 & \dots & 0 & 1 \\
0 & -1 & \dots & 0 & 1 \\
\vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots & \vdots \\
0 & 0 & \dots & -1 & 1 \\
0 & 0 & \dots & 0 & 0
\end{pmatrix},
\end{equation}
$$

where $\sigma(t) > 0$ is the instantaneous masking rate. Thus, each unmasked token jumps to $m$ at rate $\sigma(t)$, while the mask state is absorbing (i.e it never comes back again to a clean token).


For Gaussian diffusion in continuous spaces, we can sample intermediate points $x_t$ from the forward process in a simulation-free manner by directly sampling from the closed-form expression $q(x_t | x_0) = \mathcal{N}\!\left(x_t;\ \sqrt{\alpha_t}\,x_0,\ (1-\alpha_t)I\right)$, where $\alpha_t = \exp\!\left(-\int_0^t \beta(s)\,ds\right)$ is the cumulative noise schedule. Similarly, we can define $\bar{Q}_{t|0}$ the transition matrix from time $0$ to time $t$, so that $q(x_t | x_0)=\mathrm{Cat}(x_t;\ \bar{Q}_{t|0}^\top x_0)$. We denote $\alpha_t = \exp\!\left(-\int_0^t \sigma(s)\,ds\right)$ the cumulative masking schedule, then $\bar{Q}_{t|0}$ takes the following closed form:

$$
\begin{align}
\bar{Q}_{t|0}
&= \alpha_t I + (1-\alpha_t)\mathbf{1}\mathbf{e}_m^\top \\
&=
\begin{pmatrix}
\alpha_t & 0 & \dots & 0 & 1-\alpha_t \\
0 & \alpha_t & \dots & 0 & 1-\alpha_t \\

#### Reverse Process



In the previous section, we have described the forward process of score-based discrete diffusion models. To generate new data samples, we need to reverse the forward process. 
In Continuous space Diffusion, the reverse process is obtained by reversing the forward SDE: $ dx_t = \left(f_t(x_t) - g_t^2 \nabla \log p_t(x_t)\right)dt + g_t\,d\bar{W}_t $, where $\bar{W}_t$ is a time-reversed Brownian motion. In discrete space, we similarly reverse the forward Markov chain to obtain the reverse process, which is also a continuous-time Markov chain.

<Statement kind="Proposition" label="reverse-rate" title="Reverse rate $\dot{R}_t$">
Interestingly, the reverse process is also a continuous-time Markov chain, and its transition probabilities can be expressed as:
$$
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:reverse-rate}
p(x_{t-\Delta t} = y | x_t) = \mathbb{I}(y = x_t) + \Delta t \, [\dot{R}_t(x_t)]_y + o(\Delta t)
\end{equation}
$$

Where the generator matrix $\dot{R}_t$ is defined as:
$$
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:reverse-rate-mask}
[\dot{R}_t(x_t)]_y =
\begin{cases}
[\dot{Q}_t(y)]_{x_t} \frac{q(X_t = y)}{q(X_t = x_t)} & \text{if } y \neq x_t \\
- \sum_{y' \neq x_t} [\dot{R}_t(x_t)]_{y'} & \text{if } y = x_t
\end{cases}
\end{equation}
$$
</Statement>

<details>
<summary>Proof</summary>
<div>

$$

\end{align}

For $y = x_t$, we use the fact that probabilities sum to one, so the rows of a valid transition rate matrix must sum to zero, leading to $[\dot{R}_t(x_t)]_{x_t} = - \sum_{y' \neq x_t} [\dot{R}_t(x_t)]_{y'}$.

In Continuous space Diffusion, the score function $\nabla \log p_t(x)$ governs the reverse SDE $ dx_t = \left(f_t(x_t) - g_t^2 \nabla \log p_t(x_t)\right)dt + g_t\,d\bar{W}_t $. Thus, to sample from the reverse process, we only need to learn the score function $s_{\theta,t}(x_t) = \nabla \log p_t(x_t)$. This is at the core of score-based diffusion models.

We define the discrete space analog as the **concrete score**, that is the ratio of marginal probabilities between a candidate destination state and the current state:
$$
s_t(x_t)_y \;=\; \frac{q(X_t = y)}{q(X_t = x_t)} \quad (y \neq x_t).
$$
Recalling @eq:reverse-rate-mask, the concrete score is precisely the quantity that converts the (known) forward rate $\dot{Q}_t$ into the reverse rate $\dot{R}_t$:
$$

\begin{equation}
\label{eq:concrete-score}
s_t(x_t)_y \;=\; \frac{[\dot{R}_t(x_t)]_y}{[\dot{Q}_t(x_t)]_y}.
\end{equation}
$$
Then the reverse process can be simulated using the concrete score as follows:
$$
\begin{align}
\label{eq:score-sampling}
x_{t-h} &\sim \mathrm{Cat}\left(
\mathbf{e}_{x_t}
+ h\,\dot{R}_t(x_t)
+ o(h)
\right) \\
&=
\mathrm{Cat}\left(
\mathbf{e}_{x_t}
+ h\,\big([\dot{Q}_t(x_t)]_{\cdot}\odot s_t(x_t)_{\cdot}\big)
+ o(h)
\right), \label{eq:score-sampling-cat}
\end{align}
$$

where $\odot$ denotes the elementwise product. The forward rate $\dot{Q}_t$ is known because it is a design choice of the noising process. The concrete score $s_t$ (or equivalently the reverse rate $\dot{R}_t$), however, depends on the noisy-data marginals $q(X_t=\cdot)$ which are unknown. This is why, in practice, we approximate $s_t$ with a neural network $s_{\theta, t}$.


#### Training

It remains to design a tractable training loss for the concrete score. As in Continuous space Diffusion, where denoising score matching exploits that the marginal score is a conditional expectation of the (tractable) conditional score: $\nabla \log p_t(x_t) = \mathbb{E}_{x_0 \sim q(\cdot | x_t)}[\nabla \log p_t(x_t | x_0)]$, the same identity holds here. Defining the (tractable) conditional concrete score $s_t(x_t | x_0)_y \;:=\; p_t(y | x_0)/p_t(x_t | x_0)$, we have for $y \neq x_t$:

$$
\begin{align}
s_t(x_t)_y \;=\; \frac{p_t(y)}{p_t(x_t)}
&\;=\; \frac{\sum_{x_0} p_t(y|x_0)\,p(x_0)}{p_t(x_t)} \label{eq:concrete-score-cond-exp-1} \\
&\;=\; \sum_{x_0} \frac{p_t(y|x_0)}{p_t(x_t|x_0)}\;
\underbrace{\frac{p_t(x_t|x_0)\,p(x_0)}{p_t(x_t)}}_{q(x_0 | x_t)\ \text{by Bayes}} \label{eq:concrete-score-cond-exp-2} \\
&\;=\; \mathbb{E}_{x_0 \sim q(\cdot|x_t)}\!\left[\,s_t(x_t|x_0)_y\,\right] \label{eq:concrete-score-cond-exp-3}
\end{align}
$$
<a href="#dfm-note-3" class="dfm-note-ref">3</a>

This gives the characterization:<a href="#dfm-note-4" class="dfm-note-ref">4</a>

$$
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:score-kl-argmin}
s_t \;=\; \underset{s}{\operatorname{argmin}}\ \mathbb{E}_{\substack{x_0 \sim p_0 \\ x_t \sim q(\cdot|x_0)}}\!\Bigg[ \sum_{y \neq x_t} \mathrm{KL}\!\left(s_t(x_t | x_0)_y,\, s(x_t)_y\right) \Bigg]
\end{equation}
$$

Approximating $s_t$ by a neural network $s_{\theta,t}$ and weighting each term by the forward rate $[\dot{Q}_t(x_t)]_y$ (which leaves the minimizer unchanged) yields the loss:

$$
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:score-kl-loss}
L_t(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{\substack{x_0 \sim p_0 \\ x_t \sim q(\cdot|x_0)}} \Bigg[ \sum_{y \neq x_t} [\dot{Q}_t(x_t)]_y\, \mathrm{KL}\!\left(s_t(x_t | x_0)_y,\, s_{\theta,t}(x_t)_y\right) \Bigg]
\end{equation}
$$

Expanding the KL and dropping $\theta$-independent terms gives the equivalent explicit form:

<Statement kind="Proposition" label="concrete-score-matching" title="Concrete score matching $s_t$">
The concrete score $s_t$ is the minimizer (over $s_{\theta,t}$) of
$$
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:score-loss-prop}
L_t(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{\substack{x_0 \sim p_0 \\ x_t \sim q( \cdot |x_0)}} \Bigg[ \sum_{y \neq x_t} [\dot{Q}_t(x_t)]_y \bigg( s_{\theta,t}(x_t)_y - \frac{p_t(y|x_0)}{p_t(x_t|x_0)} \log s_{\theta,t}(x_t)_y \bigg) \Bigg] + C
\end{equation}
$$
where $C$ is a constant independent of $\theta$.
</Statement>

### Equivalence Between Viewpoints

#### Equivalence Between the Two Discrete Diffusion Perspectives

Discrete diffusion models can be framed from two different perspectives: variational inference and score matching. These two viewpoints, while appearing different, are in fact equivalent. We now demonstrate this equivalence, by first showing they use their sampling schemes are the same, and then that their training objectives are equivalent.

__Sampling:__ We first connect the two views of Discrete Diffusion at the sampling level. The reverse rate for score-based discrete diffusion can be written in terms of the true denoiser $D_t^i(\mathbf{x})_v=\mathbb{P}(X_0^i=v | \mathbf{X}_t=\mathbf{x})$:

$$
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:denoiser-reverse-rate}
\dot{R}_t^i(\mathbf{x})_v
=
\mathbb{I}(x^i=m)\,
\frac{-\dot{\alpha}_t}{1-\alpha_t}
\left(
D_t^i(\mathbf{x})_v
- \mathbb{I}(v=m)
\right).
\end{equation}
$$

Then, we show that the two discrete diffusion frameworks are equivalent by showing that their sampling schemes are the same, i.e. connecting @eq:masked-diffusion-denoiser-sampling and @eq:score-sampling-cat. Starting from @eq:masked-diffusion-denoiser-sampling and taking $s=t-h$ and letting $h \to 0$, for a masked token $x_t^i=m$:

$$
\begin{align}
x_{t-h}^i
&\sim \mathrm{Cat}\left(
\frac{\alpha_{t-h}-\alpha_t}{1-\alpha_t}D_t^i(\mathbf{x}_t)
+ \frac{1-\alpha_{t-h}}{1-\alpha_t}\mathbf{e}_m
\right) \label{eq:masked-denoiser-euler-1} \\
&= \mathrm{Cat}\left(
\frac{-h\dot{\alpha}_t + o(h)}{1-\alpha_t}D_t^i(\mathbf{x}_t)
+ \frac{1-\alpha_t + h\dot{\alpha}_t + o(h)}{1-\alpha_t}\mathbf{e}_m
\right) \label{eq:masked-denoiser-euler-2} \\
&= \mathrm{Cat}\left(
\mathbf{e}_m
+ h\,\frac{-\dot{\alpha}_t}{1-\alpha_t}
\underbrace{\left(D_t^i(\mathbf{x}_t)-\mathbf{e}_m\right)}_{
\frac{1-\alpha_t}{-\dot{\alpha}_t}\dot{R}_t^i(\mathbf{x}_t)
}
+ o(h)
\right) \label{eq:masked-denoiser-euler-3} \\
&= \mathrm{Cat}\left(
\mathbf{e}_{x_t^i}
+ h\,\dot{R}_t^i(\mathbf{x}_t)
+ o(h)
\right). \label{eq:masked-denoiser-euler-4}
\end{align}
$$
using $\alpha_{t-h} - \alpha_t = -h\dot{\alpha}_t + o(h)$ in the second line.

If $x_t^i\neq m$, both samplers keep the token fixed. Thus @eq:masked-diffusion-denoiser-sampling is the finite-step version of the sampler @eq:score-sampling-cat.

__Training Objectives__: Now we turn to the corresponding training objectives. The [variational perspective](#variational-discrete-diffusion) gives the ELBO loss @eq:discrete-elbo-1 (which can be rewritten as @eq:mdm-loss for Masked Diffusion), while the [score-based perspective](#score-based-discrete-diffusion) gives the concrete score matching loss @eq:score-loss-prop. We now show that these two objectives are in fact the same: taking the continuous-time limit of the variational ELBO produces, after a change of variable, exactly the concrete score matching loss @eq:score-loss-prop.

Starting from the variational loss @eq:discrete-elbo-1, in the continuous-time limit $T \to \infty$, each discrete transition $q(x_s | x_t)$ is replaced by an instantaneous rate $\dot{R}_t(x_t)$. This motivates introducing the conditional reverse rate $\dot{R}_t(x_t | x_0)$, defined by the first-order Taylor expansion:

$$
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:reverse-kernel}
q(y | x_t, x_0) = \mathbb{I}(y = x_t) + \Delta t\, [\dot{R}_t(x_t | x_0)]_y + o(\Delta t), \quad y \in \mathcal{V}
\end{equation}
$$

For Masked Diffusion, this conditional reverse rate takes the explicit form:

$$
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:reverse-kernel-mask}
\dot{R}_t(x_t | x_0) =
\begin{pmatrix}
 & & & 0 \\
 & \mathbf{0}_{|\mathcal{V}|} & & \vdots \\
 & & & 0 \\
0 & \dots \, r_t \, \dots & 0 & -r_t
\end{pmatrix}
= r_t\, \mathbf{e}_m (\mathbf{e}_{x_0} - \mathbf{e}_m)^\top
\end{equation}
$$

where $r_t = -\dot{\alpha}_t / (1 - \alpha_t)$ is the instantaneous unmasking rate, and the entry $r_t$ in the last row appears at the column index corresponding to $x_0$. Intuitively, this rate captures the instantaneous tendency of a masked token to revert to the clean token $x_0$.

<details>
<summary>Construction of $\dot{R}_t(x_t | x_0)$</summary>
<div>

To derive $\dot{R}_t(x_t | x_0)$, we write the reverse transition probabilities $q(x_s | x_t, x_0)$ for arbitrary $s < t$ using a matrix $R_{s|t}^{x_0}$, and then write the first-order Taylor expansion as $\Delta t = t - s \to 0$ of this matrix.

Using @eq:bridge-mask, the reverse transition $q(x_s | x_t, x_0)$ can be written as a categorical distribution governed by a reverse matrix $R_{s|t}^{x_0}$:
$$
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:bridge-cat}
q(x_s | x_t, x_0) = \mathrm{Cat}\!\left(x_s;\ (R_{s|t}^{x_0})^\top x_t\right)
\end{equation}
$$
and:
$$
\begin{align}
R_{s|t}^{x_0} =
\left(
\begin{array}{ccc|c}
 & & & 0   \\
 & I_{|\mathcal{V}|} & & \vdots  \\
 & & & 0  \\
\hline
0 & \dots \quad \frac{\alpha_s - \alpha_t}{1 - \alpha_t} \quad \dots & 0 & \frac{1 - \alpha_s}{1 - \alpha_t}
\end{array}
\right) \in \mathbb{R}^{K \times K} \label{eq:bridge-matrix}
\end{align}
$$

where the entry $\frac{\alpha_s - \alpha_t}{1 - \alpha_t}$ in the last row appears at the column corresponding to $x_0$, and can be compactly written as $R_{s|t}^{x_0} = I + \frac{\alpha_s - \alpha_t}{1 - \alpha_t} \mathbf{e}_m(\mathbf{e}_{x_0} - \mathbf{e}_m)^\top$.

Taking the first-order Taylor expansion (noting that $\alpha_{t - \Delta t} - \alpha_t \approx -\dot{\alpha}_t \Delta t$):

$$
\begin{align}
R_{t - \Delta t | t}^{x_0} &= I + \frac{-\dot{\alpha}_t \Delta t}{1 - \alpha_t} \mathbf{e}_m(\mathbf{e}_{x_0} - \mathbf{e}_m)^\top + o(\Delta t)  \label{eq:bridge-rate-1} \\
&= I + \Delta t\, \dot{R}_t(x_t | x_0) + o(\Delta t) \label{eq:bridge-rate-2}
\end{align}
$$

which identifies the conditional reverse rate as $\dot{R}_t(x_t | x_0) = r_t\, \mathbf{e}_m(\mathbf{e}_{x_0} - \mathbf{e}_m)^\top$ with $r_t = -\dot{\alpha}_t / (1 - \alpha_t)$.

</div>
</details>

<Statement kind="Proposition" label="continuous-time-elbo" title="Variational and Score-based Diffusion loss equivalence">
In the continuous-time limit, optimizing the variational ELBO @eq:discrete-elbo-1 is equivalent to minimizing the loss

$$
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:ratio-loss}
L_t(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{\substack{x_0 \sim p_{\text{data}} \\ x_t \sim q( \cdot |x_0)}} \Bigg[ \sum_{y \neq x_t} \bigg( [\dot{R}_{\theta,t}(x_t)]_y - [\dot{R}_t(x_t | x_0)]_y \log [\dot{R}_{\theta,t}(x_t)]_y \bigg) \Bigg].
\end{equation}
$$

<a href="#dfm-note-5" class="dfm-note-ref">5</a>
</Statement>

<details>
<summary>Continuous-time limit proof</summary>
<div>

We start from the full ELBO derived in @eq:discrete-elbo-1:

The first term is independent of $\theta$, so we focus on the KL terms inside the sum. Let $\Delta t = 1/T$, $t = j/T$, and $s = (j-1)/T$. Using @eq:reverse-kernel and the learned analogue of @eq:reverse-rate, the $j$-th KL $\left[ \mathrm{KL}\!\left(q\!\left(x_{(j-1)/T} | x_{j/T}, x_0\right),\, p_\theta\!\left(x_{(j-1)/T} | x_{j/T}\right)\right) \right]$ expands over the vocabulary as:

$$
\begin{align}
\mathrm{KL}\left(q( \cdot | x_t, x_0) , p_\theta( \cdot | x_t)\right)
&= \sum_{y \in \mathcal{V}}
\underbrace{q(y | x_t, x_0)}_{\mathbb{I}(y=x_t)+\Delta t[\dot{R}_t(x_t|x_0)]_y+o(\Delta t)}
\log \frac{
  q(y | x_t, x_0)
}{
  \underbrace{p_\theta(y | x_t)}_{\mathclap{\mathbb{I}(y=x_t)+\Delta t[\dot{R}_{\theta,t}(x_t)]_y+o(\Delta t)}}
}
\label{eq:ratio-kl-1}
\end{align}
$$

Separating the sum into the case where the token changes ($y \neq x_t$) and stays the same ($y = x_t$):

1. For $y \neq x_t$:

$$
\begin{align}
    & (\Delta t\, [\dot{R}_t(x_t | x_0)]_y + o(\Delta t)) \log \frac{\Delta t\, [\dot{R}_t(x_t | x_0)]_y + o(\Delta t)}{\Delta t\, [\dot{R}_{\theta,t}(x_t)]_y + o(\Delta t)}  \label{eq:ratio-kl-term-1} \\
    & \quad = \Delta t\, [\dot{R}_t(x_t | x_0)]_y \log \frac{[\dot{R}_t(x_t | x_0)]_y}{[\dot{R}_{\theta,t}(x_t)]_y} + o(\Delta t) \notag
\end{align}
$$

2. For $y = x_t$, using the expansion $\log \frac{1+a}{1+b} = a - b + o(|a|+|b|)$:

$$
\begin{align}
    & (1 + \Delta t\, [\dot{R}_t(x_t | x_0)]_{x_t} + o(\Delta t)) \log \frac{1 + \Delta t\, [\dot{R}_t(x_t | x_0)]_{x_t} + o(\Delta t)}{1 + \Delta t\, [\dot{R}_{\theta,t}(x_t)]_{x_t} + o(\Delta t)}  \label{eq:ratio-kl-term-2-1} \notag \\
    & \quad = \Delta t\, [\dot{R}_t(x_t | x_0)]_{x_t} - \Delta t\, [\dot{R}_{\theta,t}(x_t)]_{x_t} + o(\Delta t)  \label{eq:ratio-kl-term-2-2} \\
    & \quad = \Delta t \left( - \sum_{y \neq x_t} [\dot{R}_t(x_t | x_0)]_y + \sum_{y \neq x_t} [\dot{R}_{\theta,t}(x_t)]_y \right) + o(\Delta t) \label{eq:ratio-kl-term-2-3}
\end{align}
$$

Combining these terms, the $j$-th KL term becomes:

$$
\begin{align}
\mathrm{KL}
&= \Delta t \sum_{y \neq x_t} \Bigg(
[\dot{R}_{\theta,t}(x_t)]_y
- [\dot{R}_t(x_t | x_0)]_y \log [\dot{R}_{\theta,t}(x_t)]_y \label{eq:ratio-kl-sum-1} \\
&\quad
+ \underbrace{
[\dot{R}_t(x_t | x_0)]_y \log [\dot{R}_t(x_t | x_0)]_y
- [\dot{R}_t(x_t | x_0)]_y
}_{\text{ independent of }\theta}
\Bigg) + o(\Delta t). \notag
\end{align}
$$

Writing $t_j=j/T$ and $\Delta t=1/T$, the ELBO @eq:discrete-elbo-1 is therefore (grouping the $\theta$-independent above and $\mathrm{KL}(q(x_1 | x_0) , p_{\text{source}})$ into a constant $C$):

$$
\begin{align}
&\sum_{j=1}^{T}
\mathbb{E}_{x_{t_j}\sim q(\cdot|x_0)}
\left[
\mathrm{KL}\left(q(x_{t_j-\Delta t}|x_{t_j},x_0),p_\theta(x_{t_j-\Delta t}|x_{t_j})\right)
\right] \label{eq:final-celbo} \\
&= \sum_{j=1}^{T}\Delta t\,
\mathbb{E}_{x_{t_j}\sim q(\cdot|x_0)}
\Bigg[
\sum_{y\neq x_{t_j}}
\bigg(
[\dot{R}_{\theta,t_j}(x_{t_j})]_y
-[\dot{R}_{t_j}(x_{t_j}|x_0)]_y
\log [\dot{R}_{\theta,t_j}(x_{t_j})]_y \label{eq:ratio-riemann-1}
\bigg)
\Bigg]
+ C + o(1), \notag
\end{align}
$$

where $C$ collects the terms independent of $\theta$. Taking $T\to\infty$ turns the sum into a Riemann integral over $t$, whose integrand is exactly the loss @eq:ratio-loss.

</div>
</details>

It remains to re-express @eq:ratio-loss in terms of the concrete score. Writing the reverse rates as $[\dot{R}_{t}(x_t)]_y = [\dot{Q}_t(x_t)]_y\,s_{t}(x_t)_y$ we recover, up to a $\theta$-independent constant, the concrete score matching loss @eq:score-loss-prop established in the [score-based perspective](#score-based-discrete-diffusion). The two viewpoints therefore minimize the same objective.

<details>
<summary>Equivalence derivation</summary>
<div>

Applying Bayes' theorem to $x_t \to x_s$ conditioned on $x_0$ gives:
$$
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:reverse-bayes}
\dot{R}_t(x_t | x_0)_y = [\dot{Q}_t(x_t)]_y \frac{p_t(y|x_0)}{p_t(x_t|x_0)}
\end{equation}
$$

Substituting $[\dot{R}_{\theta,t}(x_t)]_y = [\dot{Q}_t(x_t)]_y\,s_{\theta,t}(x_t)_y$ and the expression above into @eq:ratio-loss:
$$
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:score-loss}
\begin{aligned}
L_t(\theta) &= \mathbb{E}_{\substack{x_0 \sim p_0 \\ x_t \sim q( \cdot |x_0)}} \Bigg[ \sum_{y \neq x_t} \Bigg( [\dot{Q}_t(x_t)]_y s_{\theta,t}(x_t)_y \\
&\quad - \left( [\dot{Q}_t(x_t)]_y \frac{p_t(y|x_0)}{p_t(x_t|x_0)} \right) \log \left( [\dot{Q}_t(x_t)]_y s_{\theta,t}(x_t)_y \right) \Bigg) \Bigg]
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
$$
Expanding the logarithm gives:

$$
\begin{align}
L_t(\theta)
&= \mathbb{E}_{\substack{x_0 \sim p_0 \\ x_t \sim q( \cdot |x_0)}} \Bigg[
\sum_{y \neq x_t}
\Bigg(
[\dot{Q}_t(x_t)]_y s_{\theta,t}(x_t)_y \label{eq:score-loss-expand-1} \\
&\qquad
- [\dot{Q}_t(x_t)]_y \frac{p_t(y|x_0)}{p_t(x_t|x_0)}
\log [\dot{Q}_t(x_t)]_y \notag \\
&\qquad
- [\dot{Q}_t(x_t)]_y \frac{p_t(y|x_0)}{p_t(x_t|x_0)}
\log s_{\theta,t}(x_t)_y
\Bigg)
\Bigg]. \notag \\
&= \mathbb{E}_{\substack{x_0 \sim p_0 \\ x_t \sim q( \cdot |x_0)}} \Bigg[
\sum_{y \neq x_t}
[\dot{Q}_t(x_t)]_y
\bigg(
s_{\theta,t}(x_t)_y
- \frac{p_t(y|x_0)}{p_t(x_t|x_0)}
\log s_{\theta,t}(x_t)_y
\bigg)
\Bigg] \label{eq:score-loss-expand-group-1} \\
&\quad + \underbrace{
\mathbb{E}_{\substack{x_0 \sim p_0 \\ x_t \sim q( \cdot |x_0)}} \Bigg[
\sum_{y \neq x_t}
- [\dot{Q}_t(x_t)]_y \frac{p_t(y|x_0)}{p_t(x_t|x_0)}
\log [\dot{Q}_t(x_t)]_y
\Bigg]
}_{C\ \text{independent of }\theta}. \notag
\end{align}
$$

This is exactly the loss @eq:score-loss-prop of the concrete score matching proposition.

{/*
#### Complement: Equivalence Between Masked Diffusion Models and Any-Order Autoregressive Models

An any-order autoregressive model (AO-ARM) is an autoregressive model trained to factorize the joint distribution under *every* possible token ordering $\pi$, not just the left-to-right one. Its training objective minimizes the expected negative log-likelihood over the uniform distribution $\mathcal{U}_\pi$ of all $S!$ orderings:
$$
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:ao-arm}
\mathcal{L}_{\text{AO-ARM}}(\theta) = -\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}_0 \sim p_{\text{data}},\, \pi \sim \mathcal{U}_\pi} \left[ \sum_{i=1}^{S} \log p_\theta(x_0^{\pi(i)} | x_0^{\pi(<i)}; \pi) \right]
\end{equation}
$$
where $x_0^{\pi(<i)} = (x_0^{\pi(1)}, \ldots, x_0^{\pi(i-1)})$ denotes the tokens revealed before position $\pi(i)$ in the ordering $\pi$. In practice, AO-ARMs use a shared neural network with mask tokens to represent absent variables: given an ordering $\pi$ and a step $i$, the positions $\pi(i), \pi(i+1), \ldots, \pi(S)$ are replaced by the mask token $m$, and the network predicts the token at position $\pi(i)$ from this partially masked input.

The following proposition shows that the MDM training objective is equivalent to the AO-ARM objective.

<Statement kind="Proposition" label="mdm-ao-arm-equivalence" title="MDM and AO-ARM Equivalence">
Let $\mathcal{L}_{\text{MDM}}(\theta)$ denote the Masked Diffusion training loss with linear schedule $\alpha_t = 1 - t$ and uniform time sampling $t \sim \mathcal{U}(0,1)$:
$$
\begin{align}
\mathcal{L}_{\text{MDM}}(\theta) ={} & -\mathbb{E}_{t \sim \mathcal{U}(0,1),\, \mathbf{x}_0 \sim p_{\text{data}},\, \mathbf{x}_t \sim q(\cdot | \mathbf{x}_0)}  \label{eq:loss-mdm-exp-1} \\
& \left[ \frac{1}{t} \sum_{i=1}^{S} \mathbb{I}(x_t^i = m) \log p_\theta(x_0^i | \mathbf{x}_t) \right] \label{eq:loss-mdm-exp-2}
\end{align}
$$
Then $\mathcal{L}_{\text{MDM}}(\theta) = \mathcal{L}_{\text{AO-ARM}}(\theta)$.
</Statement>

This equivalence has a practical consequence: since the MDM is implicitly trained on all possible token orderings, it does not privilege left-to-right dependencies.

<details>
<summary>Proof</summary>
<div>
**Proof:** We proceed in two steps. First, we rewrite the MDM loss as a sum over the number of masked tokens using a change of variable. Second, we rewrite the AO-ARM loss in the same form.

**Step 1: Rewriting the MDM loss.**

Under the linear schedule $\alpha_t = 1 - t$, the forward process masks each token independently with probability $t$. The MDM loss can be written as:
$$
\begin{align}
\mathcal{L}_{\text{MDM}}(\theta) = -\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}_0} \Bigg[ & \int_0^1 \frac{1}{t} \mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}_t \sim q(\cdot | \mathbf{x}_0)}  \label{eq:loss-mdm-exp-2-1} \\
& \left[ \sum_{i=1}^{S} \mathbb{I}(x_t^i = m) \log p_\theta(x_0^i | \mathbf{x}_t) \right] dt \Bigg] \label{eq:loss-mdm-exp-2-2}
\end{align}
$$
We now condition on the number of masked tokens. The forward process $q(\cdot | \mathbf{x}_0)$ masks each token independently: $x_t^i = m$ with probability $t$ and $x_t^i = x_0^i$ with probability $1-t$. Equivalently, drawing $\mathbf{x}_t \sim q(\cdot | \mathbf{x}_0)$ amounts to drawing $\xi^i \sim \text{Bernoulli}(t)$ independently for $i = 1, \ldots, S$ and setting:
$$
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:mask-bernoulli}
x_t^i = \begin{cases} m & \text{if } \xi^i = 1 \\ x_0^i & \text{if } \xi^i = 0 \end{cases}
\end{equation}
$$
Let $\mathcal{M} = \{i : \xi^i = 1\}$ denote the set of masked positions. For fixed $\mathbf{x}_0$, $\mathbf{x}_t$ and $\mathcal{M}$ are in bijection ($\mathcal{M} = \{i : x_t^i = m\}$), so we may write $\mathbf{x}_{\mathcal{M}} := \mathbf{x}_t$ and $p_\theta(x_0^i | \mathbf{x}_t) = p_\theta(x_0^i | \mathbf{x}_{\mathcal{M}})$. The inner sum becomes $\sum_{i \in \mathcal{M}} \log p_\theta(x_0^i | \mathbf{x}_{\mathcal{M}})$. For any function $g$:
$$
\begin{align}
\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}_t \sim q(\cdot | \mathbf{x}_0)}[g(\mathbf{x}_t)] & = \mathbb{E}_{(\xi^1, \ldots, \xi^S) \overset{\text{i.i.d.}}{\sim} \text{Bernoulli}(t)}[g(\mathbf{x}_t)]  \label{eq:exp-t-q-1} \\
& = \mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{M}}[g(\mathbf{x}_{\mathcal{M}})] \label{eq:exp-t-q-2}
\end{align}
$$
Applying the law of total expectation by conditioning on $|\mathcal{M}|$:
$$
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:mask-count}
\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{M}}[g(\mathbf{x}_{\mathcal{M}})] = \sum_{k=0}^{S} \mathbb{P}(|\mathcal{M}| = k) \;\mathbb{E}[g(\mathbf{x}_{\mathcal{M}}) | |\mathcal{M}| = k]
\end{equation}
$$
It remains to identify the conditional distribution of $\mathcal{M}$ given $|\mathcal{M}| = k$. For any subset $A \subseteq [S]$ with $|A| = k$:
$$
\begin{align}
\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{M} = A | |\mathcal{M}| = k) = \frac{\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{M} = A)}{\mathbb{P}(|\mathcal{M}| = k)} = \frac{t^k(1-t)^{S-k}}{\binom{S}{k}\, t^k(1-t)^{S-k}} = \frac{1}{\binom{S}{k}} \label{eq:prob-a-k-1}
\end{align}
$$
so $\mathcal{M} | |\mathcal{M}| = k$ is uniformly distributed over all size-$k$ subsets of $[S]$, i.e., $\mathbb{E}[\,\cdot | |\mathcal{M}| = k] = \mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{M} \sim \text{Unif}(\binom{[S]}{k})}[\,\cdot\,]$.
Define:
$$
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:f-k}
f(k, \mathbf{x}_0) := \mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{M} \sim \text{Unif}(\binom{[S]}{k})} \left[ \sum_{i \in \mathcal{M}} \log p_\theta(x_0^i | \mathbf{x}_{\mathcal{M}}) \right]
\end{equation}
$$
Combining these results:
$$
\begin{align}
& \mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}_t \sim q(\cdot | \mathbf{x}_0)}\left[ \frac{1}{t} \sum_{i=1}^{S} \mathbb{I}(x_t^i = m) \log p_\theta(x_0^i | \mathbf{x}_t) \right]  \label{eq:exp-t-q-2-1} \\
& \quad = \frac{1}{t} \sum_{k=0}^{S} \underbrace{\binom{S}{k} t^k (1-t)^{S-k}}_{\mathbb{P}(|\mathcal{M}| = k)} \; f(k, \mathbf{x}_0) \label{eq:exp-t-q-2-2}
\end{align}
$$
The $k = 0$ term vanishes (no masked tokens), so substituting back into $\mathcal{L}_{\text{MDM}}$:
$$
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:mdm-int}
\mathcal{L}_{\text{MDM}}(\theta) = -\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}_0} \left[ \int_0^1 \frac{1}{t} \sum_{k=1}^{S} \binom{S}{k} t^k (1-t)^{S-k}\, f(k, \mathbf{x}_0)\, dt \right]
\end{equation}
$$
Exchanging the sum and integral:
$$
\begin{align}
\mathcal{L}_{\text{MDM}}(\theta) = -\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}_0} \left[ \sum_{k=1}^{S} \binom{S}{k} \left( \int_0^1 t^{k-1} (1-t)^{S-k}\, dt \right) f(k, \mathbf{x}_0) \right] \label{eq:loss-mdm-exp-3-1}
\end{align}
$$
The integral is a Beta function:
$$
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:beta-int}
\int_0^1 t^{k-1}(1-t)^{S-k}\, dt = B(k, S-k+1) = \frac{(k-1)!(S-k)!}{S!}
\end{equation}
$$
Substituting and simplifying $\binom{S}{k} \cdot \frac{(k-1)!(S-k)!}{S!} = \frac{S!}{k!(S-k)!} \cdot \frac{(k-1)!(S-k)!}{S!} = \frac{1}{k}$:
$$
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:mdm-sum}
\mathcal{L}_{\text{MDM}}(\theta) = -\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}_0} \left[ \sum_{k=1}^{S} \frac{1}{k}\, f(k, \mathbf{x}_0) \right]
\end{equation}
$$

**Step 2: Rewriting the AO-ARM loss.**

Fix an ordering $\pi$ and a step $i \in \{1, \ldots, S\}$. The conditional $p_\theta(x_0^{\pi(i)} | x_0^{\pi(<i)}; \pi)$ asks the model to predict the token at position $\pi(i)$ given that positions $\{\pi(1), \ldots, \pi(i-1)\}$ are unmasked and all others are masked. Since the model uses mask tokens to represent absent variables, the prediction depends only on *which* positions are revealed, not on the ordering in which they were revealed. Defining $\mathcal{M}_i = \{\pi(i), \pi(i+1), \ldots, \pi(S)\}$ as the set of $k = S - i + 1$ masked positions at step $i$:
$$
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:ao-arm-mask}
p_\theta(x_0^{\pi(i)} | x_0^{\pi(<i)}; \pi) = p_\theta(x_0^{\pi(i)} | \mathbf{x}_{\mathcal{M}_i})
\end{equation}
$$
We now show that, for a uniformly random $\pi$ and fixed $k$, the pair $(\mathcal{M}_i,\, \pi(i))$ is uniformly distributed over all (size-$k$ subset, element of that subset). Fix any $A \subseteq [S]$ with $|A| = k$ and any $j \in A$. A permutation $\pi$ satisfies $\mathcal{M}_i = A$ and $\pi(i) = j$ if and only if: its first $S - k$ entries are a permutation of $[S] \setminus A$, its $i$-th entry is $j$, and its last $k - 1$ entries are a permutation of $A \setminus \{j\}$. The number of such permutations is $(S-k)!\,(k-1)!$, so:
$$
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:ao-arm-pair}
\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{M}_i = A,\; \pi(i) = j) = \frac{(S-k)!\,(k-1)!}{S!} = \frac{1}{\binom{S}{k} \cdot k}
\end{equation}
$$
Summing over $j \in A$ gives $\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{M}_i = A) = 1/\binom{S}{k}$, and conditionally $\mathbb{P}(\pi(i) = j | \mathcal{M}_i = A) = 1/k$. We can now replace the expectation over $\pi$ with expectations over $\mathcal{M}$ and $j$. For any function $h$:
$$
\begin{align}
\mathbb{E}_{\pi \sim \mathcal{U}_\pi}\left[h(\mathcal{M}_i, \pi(i))\right] &= \frac{1}{S!}\sum_{\pi} h(\mathcal{M}_i, \pi(i))  \label{eq:exp-h-i-1} \\
&= \sum_{\substack{A \subseteq [S] \\ |A| = k}} \sum_{j \in A} \mathbb{P}(\mathcal{M}_i = A,\; \pi(i) = j)\; h(A, j)  \label{eq:exp-h-i-2} \\
&= \sum_{\substack{A \subseteq [S] \\ |A| = k}} \sum_{j \in A} \frac{1}{\binom{S}{k}} \cdot \frac{1}{k}\; h(A, j)  \label{eq:exp-h-i-3} \\
&= \mathbb{E}_{\substack{\mathcal{M} \sim \text{Unif}(\binom{[S]}{k}) \\ j \sim \text{Unif}(\mathcal{M})}} \left[h(\mathcal{M}, j)\right] \label{eq:exp-h-i-4}
\end{align}
$$
Applying this with $h(\mathcal{M}, j) = \log p_\theta(x_0^j | \mathbf{x}_{\mathcal{M}})$ and summing over $k$:
$$
\begin{align}
\mathcal{L}_{\text{AO-ARM}}(\theta) = -\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}_0} \Bigg[ & \sum_{k=1}^{S} \mathbb{E}_{\substack{\mathcal{M} \sim \text{Unif}(\binom{[S]}{k}) \\ j \sim \text{Unif}(\mathcal{M})}}  \label{eq:loss-ao-arm-1} \\
& \left[ \log p_\theta(x_0^j | \mathbf{x}_{\mathcal{M}}) \right] \Bigg] \label{eq:loss-ao-arm-2}
\end{align}
$$
Since $j$ is uniform over $\mathcal{M}$, we have $\mathbb{E}_{j \sim \text{Unif}(\mathcal{M})}[\log p_\theta(x_0^j | \mathbf{x}_{\mathcal{M}})] = \frac{1}{k} \sum_{j \in \mathcal{M}} \log p_\theta(x_0^j | \mathbf{x}_{\mathcal{M}})$, giving:
$$
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:ao-arm-sum}
\mathcal{L}_{\text{AO-ARM}}(\theta) = -\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}_0} \left[ \sum_{k=1}^{S} \frac{1}{k}\, f(k, \mathbf{x}_0) \right]
\end{equation}
$$
with the same $f(k, \mathbf{x}_0)$ defined in Step 1.
</div>
</details>
*/}

#### Equivalence with Discrete Flow Matching

We now connect Discrete Diffusion to Discrete Flow Matching. First, we show that sampling in Discrete Flow Matching (@eq:euler-sampling) is equivalent to sampling in Discrete Diffusion (@eq:reverse-rate).


__Sampling__: To align these two sampling equations directly, we must first account for the main discrepancy between the frameworks: their time conventions. In Discrete Diffusion, $t = 0$ corresponds to clean data, and $t = 1$ corresponds to the fully masked state. Discrete Flow Matching uses the exact opposite convention.

We can therefore perfectly translate between the two frameworks using a simple time reversal: $t_{\text{FM}} = 1 - t_{\text{DD}}$. Concretely, if $(x_t)_{t \in [0,1]}$ denotes the Discrete Diffusion reverse process, then the time-reversed process $\tilde{x}_{t'} := x_{1-t'}$ is a valid Discrete Flow Matching process: it has the same law as the flow matching process with velocity field:

$$
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:time-reversal}
u_{t'}^{\text{FM}} = \dot{R}_{1-t'}
\end{equation}
$$

In particular, sampling from Discrete Diffusion (running the reverse CTMC from $t=1$ to $t=0$) is equivalent to sampling from this flow matching process (running a CTMC from $t'=0$ to $t'=1$): the two procedures produce trajectories with identical distributions.

<details>
<summary>Proof</summary>
<div>

We start from the infinitesimal reverse-time transition of the Discrete Diffusion process @eq:reverse-rate.

We first perform the variable change $t' = 1 - t$ in @eq:reverse-rate. We have $t = 1 - t'$, so $x_t = x_{1-t'}$ and $x_{t - \Delta t} = x_{1 - t' - \Delta t}$. The rate evaluates at $\dot{R}_{1-t'}$, giving:

$$
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:time-change}
p(x_{1-t'-\Delta t} = y | x_{1-t'}) = \mathbb{I}(y = x_{1-t'}) + \Delta t \, [\dot{R}_{1-t'}(x_{1-t'})]_y + o(\Delta t)
\end{equation}
$$

Since $t_{\text{FM}} = 1 - t_{\text{DD}}$, we identify $x^{\text{FM}}_{t'} = x_{1-t'}$. We deduce $x^{\text{FM}}_{t' + \Delta t} = x_{1 - t' - \Delta t}$. Substituting these identities into @eq:time-change:

$$
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:fm-step}
p(x^{\text{FM}}_{t' + \Delta t} = y | x^{\text{FM}}_{t'}) = \mathbb{I}(y = x^{\text{FM}}_{t'}) + \Delta t \, [\dot{R}_{1-t'}(x^{\text{FM}}_{t'})]_y + o(\Delta t)
\end{equation}
$$

Recall the definition of a probability velocity @eq:prob-velocity: a velocity field $u_{t'}$ generates a process $x^{\text{FM}}_{t'}$ if and only if $ p(x^{\text{FM}}_{t' + \Delta t} = y | x^{\text{FM}}_{t'} = x) = \mathbb{I}(y = x^{\text{FM}}_{t'}) + \Delta t \, [u_{t'}(x^{\text{FM}}_{t'})]_y + o(\Delta t) $.

Identifying the first-order terms in @eq:fm-step with those in @eq:prob-velocity directly gives:

$$
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:time-reversal-velocity}
[u_{t'}^{\text{FM}}(x)]_y = [\dot{R}_{1-t'}(x)]_y
\end{equation}
$$

and therefore $u_{t'}^{\text{FM}} = \dot{R}_{1-t'}$.

</div>
</details>

__Training Objectives__: Now we show how their training objectives line up in the masked case. The [variational diffusion perspective](#variational-discrete-diffusion) gives the Masked Diffusion objective @eq:mdm-loss, while the [denoiser parameterization in Discrete Flow Matching](#learning-with-denoiser-parameterization) gives the CDM objective @eq:cdm-loss. We now compare these objectives.

To directly compare the two training losses, we first note that the two denoisers introduced in flow matching in @eq:cdm-loss and in variational diffusion in @eq:mdm-loss are exactly the same object. Applying the time reversal $t_{\text{FM}} = 1 - t_{\text{DD}}$ and identifying the clean data $x_1=x_0$, we get:

$$
\begin{align}
D_{\theta,t_{\text{DD}}}^i(\mathbf{x})_{x_0^i}
&= P_{\theta,t_{\text{DD}}}(X_0^i=x_0^i | X_{t_{\text{DD}}}=\mathbf{x}) \notag \\
&= P_{\theta,t_{\text{FM}}}(X_1^i=x_1^i | X_{t_{\text{FM}}}=\mathbf{x}). \label{eq:denoiser-time-identification}
\end{align}
$$

<Statement kind="Proposition" label="masked-mdm-cdm-loss-equivalence" title="Masked Diffusion/CDM loss equivalence">
Under the time reversal $t_{\text{FM}}=1-t_{\text{DD}}$ and the denoiser identification @eq:denoiser-time-identification, the Masked Diffusion training loss $L_{\text{MDM}}(t)$ from @eq:mdm-loss is directly comparable to the denoising loss $L_{\text{CDM}}(t)$ from @eq:cdm-loss. At fixed flow-matching time $t$:

$$
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:mdm-cdm-scale}
L_{\text{MDM}}(t) \approx \frac{1}{1-t} \, L_{\text{CDM}}(t).
\end{equation}
$$
</Statement>

<details>
<summary>Derivation</summary>
<div>

Under the time reversal above, the weight in @eq:mdm-loss satisfies $-\dot{\alpha}_{t_{\text{DD}}}/(1-\alpha_{t_{\text{DD}}})=1/t_{\text{DD}}=1/(1-t_{\text{FM}})$. Therefore the Masked Diffusion loss @eq:mdm-loss becomes the weighted masked cross-entropy:

$$
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:mdm-mask-loss-fm-time}
L_{\text{MDM}}(t) = \mathbb{E}\!\left[\,\sum_{i=1}^S \mathbb{I}(x_t^i = m) \cdot \frac{1}{1-t} \cdot \left(-\log P_{\theta,t}(X_1^i = x_1^i | \mathbf{x}_t)\right)\right].
\end{equation}
$$

Since $P(x_t^i = m) = 1-t$, applying the law of total expectation,
$\mathbb{E}[Z]=P(x_t^i=m)\mathbb{E}[Z|x_t^i=m]+P(x_t^i\neq m)\mathbb{E}[Z|x_t^i\neq m]$:

$$
\begin{align}
L_{\text{MDM}}(t) \label{eq:mdm-t-1}
  &= (1-t) \cdot \frac{1}{1-t} \cdot \mathbb{E}\!\left[ \label{eq:mdm-t-2}
      -\log P_{\theta,t}(X_1^i = x_1^i | \mathbf{x}_t) \label{eq:mdm-t-3}
      | x_t^i = m \label{eq:mdm-t-4}
    \right]  \label{eq:mdm-t-5} \\
  &\quad + t \cdot
    \underbrace{\mathbb{E}\!\left[
      \mathbb{I}(x_t^i=m)\frac{1}{1-t}
      \left(-\log P_{\theta,t}(X_1^i = x_1^i | \mathbf{x}_t)\right)
      | x_t^i \neq m
    \right]}_{0\ \text{since }x_t^i\neq m} \notag \\
  &= \mathbb{E}\!\left[ \label{eq:mdm-t-6}
      -\log P_{\theta,t}(X_1^i = x_1^i | \mathbf{x}_t) \label{eq:mdm-t-7}
      | x_t^i = m \label{eq:mdm-t-8}
    \right]. \label{eq:mdm-t-9}
\end{align}
$$

We decompose $L_{\text{CDM}}$ using the same law of total expectation, splitting over masked and unmasked positions:

$$
\begin{align}
L_{\text{CDM}}(t) \label{eq:cm-t-1}
  &= (1-t) \cdot \mathbb{E}\!\left[ \label{eq:cm-t-2}
      -\log P_{\theta,t}(X_1^i = x_1^i | \mathbf{x}_t) \label{eq:cm-t-3}
      | x_t^i = m \label{eq:cm-t-4}
    \right]  \label{eq:cm-t-5} \\
  &\quad + t \cdot \mathbb{E}\!\left[ \label{eq:cm-t-6}
      -\log P_{\theta,t}(X_1^i = x_1^i | \mathbf{x}_t) \label{eq:cm-t-7}
      | x_t^i \neq m \label{eq:cm-t-8}
    \right]. \notag
\end{align}
$$

For unmasked positions $i$, a well trained denoiser should just copy the current token $x_t^i=x_1^i$ it sees, therefore $P_{\theta,t}(X_1^i=x_1^i|\mathbf{x}_t)=1$, and the second term vanishes, giving:

$$
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:cm-mask-loss}
L_{\text{CDM}}(t) \approx (1-t) \cdot \mathbb{E}\!\left[
  -\log P_{\theta,t}(X_1^i = x_1^i | \mathbf{x}_t)
  | x_t^i = m
\right].
\end{equation}
$$

Both expressions share the same conditional expectation. Eliminating it yields @eq:mdm-cdm-scale.

</div>
</details>

**Disclaimer** In general, the objectives @eq:cdfm-2 (or equivalently @eq:ratio-loss recalling @eq:time-reversal) and @eq:cdm-loss are not equivalent for arbitrary source distributions or paths.

### To go further: Planners in masked flow-matching sampling

#### Sampling with a planner

In this section, we introduce planners as a way to make sampling more efficient in masked diffusion, and explain why using a planner also requires adapting the training loss. We follow the [Discrete Flow Matching](#discrete-flow-matching) convention: $p_0$ is the source distribution, corresponding here to the fully masked sequence $mm...m$, and $p_1$ is the target distribution, corresponding to the clean data distribution. For the complete theoretical treatment, see [<a class="cite-ref" href="#ref-7">7</a>].

Vanilla sampling in Masked Diffusion is computationally expensive. It requires $(1/h) \times S$ sequential steps, where $h$ is the time step size and $S$ is the length of the sequence. At every single micro-step, we must compute and draw from probability vectors of size $|\mathcal{V}|$ for all tokens. For example, with a step size of $h = 0.01$ and a sequence length of $S = 3000$, we would need $300,000$ steps to generate a single sample.
We can parallelize the sampling of all tokens at each step, so the number of forward passes through the model is $1/h = 100$ steps rather than $S/h$, but it requires more memory.

To make generation tractable, the standard approach used for large Masked Diffusion models is **planner-guided sampling**. Instead of relying on infinitesimal continuous-time steps, we fix the total generation steps $N$ and directly use the denoiser to reveal tokens at each step.

<Statement kind="Definition" label="planner" title="Planner $G_\phi$">
A planner $G_\phi$ maps a candidate sequence $\mathbf{z}$ sampled from the denoiser and the current partially masked sequence $\mathbf{x}_k$ to a distribution over positions to reveal. Note $\Delta(A)$ the probability simplex over a finite set $A$.<a href="#dfm-note-6" class="dfm-note-ref">6</a> At step $k$, with $\mathcal{M}_k = \{i : x_k^i = m\}$, we write:
$$
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:planner-definition}
G_\phi(\mathbf{z}, \mathbf{x}_k) \in \Delta(\{1,\ldots,S\}), \qquad
G_\phi(\mathbf{z}, \mathbf{x}_k)_i = 0 \text{ for } i \notin \mathcal{M}_k.
\end{equation}
$$
In other words, given a denoised candidate $\mathbf{z}$ produced by the denoiser from a partially masked sequence $\mathbf{x}_k$, the planner indicates, for each position $i$ in the sequence, the probability of denoising that token. If the token is already denoised, this probability is zero. For example, if $\mathbf{x}_k=(m,A,m)$ and $\mathbf{z}=(B,A,A)$, then a possible value of $G_\phi(\mathbf{z}, \mathbf{x}_k)$ is $(0.7,0,0.3)$, which means revealing position $1$ with probability $70\%$ and position $3$ with probability $30\%$.
</Statement>

**Algorithm: Planner-Guided Sampling**

**Input:** Planner $G_\phi$, trained denoiser $D_\theta$, total number of sampling steps $N$, schedule $f$ mapping step index to number of tokens to reveal at that step, e.g., $f(k) = S/N$ for all $k$ when $N$ divides $S$.
1. **Initialize:** $\mathbf{x}_0 \leftarrow (m, \dots, m)$ (a sequence of $S$ mask tokens).
2. **For** $k = 0, 1, \dots, N - 1$:
    * **Plan:** Sample candidate tokens $\mathbf{z} \sim D_{\theta,k}(\mathbf{x}_k)$.
    * **Select:**
        1. Initialize $\mathcal{I}\leftarrow \varnothing$.
        2. **For** $\ell=1,\ldots,f(k)$:
            * Sample one position $i_\ell$ from $G_\phi(\mathbf{z}, \mathbf{x}_k)$, restricted to positions not already in $\mathcal{I}$.
            * Add it to the set: $\mathcal{I}\leftarrow \mathcal{I}\cup\{i_\ell\}$.
    * **Denoise:** Create $\mathbf{x}_{k+1}$ by setting $x_{k+1}^i = z^i$ for all $i \in \mathcal{I}$, and leaving all other positions unchanged.
3. **Return** fully generated sequence $\mathbf{x}_N$.

In the rest of this section, we suppose that the denoiser does not need to know the current time in its predictions, i.e. for any step $k$ and any given sequence $\mathbf{x}$, $D_{\theta,k}(\mathbf{x}) = D_{\theta}(\mathbf{x})$.

<Statement kind="Proposition" label="sampling-scheme-equivalence" title="Sampling-scheme equivalence with $G$">
If we choose the uniform random planner that selects positions uniformly at random among the masked tokens, i.e. $G(\mathbf{z}, \mathbf{x}_k)_i = \frac{1}{|\mathcal{M}_k|}$ for each $i \in \mathcal{M}_k$, then planner-guided sampling with $N = S$ steps and $f(k) = 1$ token per step is exactly equivalent to the sampling procedure given @eq:reverse-rate as $\Delta t \to 0$.
</Statement>

However, we rarely decide to unmask tokens by picking a random position at each step. Instead, we can use heuristic or learned planners that rank positions according to how reliable or informative they seem to reveal.

A simple and widely used strategy in this direction is the soft greedy planner. Instead of unmasking tokens at random, it selects the positions where the denoiser is most confident in its predictions. We then relax this strict selection into a soft probability distribution using a softmax function with a temperature parameter $\tau$:
$$
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:planner}
G_\tau(\mathbf{z}, \mathbf{x}_k)_j \propto \exp\left(\frac{1}{\tau} \log D_\theta^j(\mathbf{x}_k)_{z^j}\right)
\end{equation}
$$
<details>
<summary>Other planning strategies</summary>
<div>

**Entropy planner.** Another heuristic is to score each position by the negative entropy of the denoiser distribution. Low entropy means the denoiser is concentrated on a few tokens, so the position is treated as more reliable:
$$
\begin{equation}
s^i(\mathbf{x}_k) = \sum_{v \in \mathcal{V}} D_\theta^i(\mathbf{x}_k)_v \log D_\theta^i(\mathbf{x}_k)_v.
\end{equation}
$$
The planner then applies a softmax over the still-masked positions:
$$
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:entropy-planner}
G_\tau^{\mathrm{ent}}(\mathbf{z}, \mathbf{x}_k)_i \propto \exp\left(\frac{s^i(\mathbf{x}_k)}{\tau}\right)
\end{equation}
$$
Unlike soft greedy, this planner does not use the sampled token $z^i$ directly; it only uses the shape of the denoiser distribution at position $i$.

**Learned planner.** With a pretrained denoiser, one can also train a separate planner offline. Starting from a clean target sequence $\mathbf{x}_1$, we first sample a partially masked sequence $\mathbf{x}_k$ along the flow-matching path, then sample a candidate denoised sequence $\mathbf{z} \sim D_\theta(\mathbf{x}_k)$ with the frozen denoiser. The planner is trained to put more mass on masked positions where the sampled token $z^i$ matches the clean target token $x_1^i$. This can be done with a cross-entropy objective of the form:
$$
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{G}(\phi) =
\mathbb{E}\!\left[
\sum_{i=1}^{S}
\mathbb{I}(x_k^i=m)
\frac{1}{1-t}
\mathrm{CE}\!\left(G_\phi^i(\mathbf{x}_k,\mathbf{z}),\, \mathbb{I}[z^i=x_1^i]\right)
\right].
\end{equation}
$$
The learned planner can then be interpreted as a consistency scorer: positions whose sampled token looks reliable under the current partially denoised context receive more planner mass.

</div>
</details>

The soft-greedy formulation assigns higher selection probabilities to the tokens the model is sure about. As $\tau \to \infty$, this reduces to uniform random sampling. As $\tau \to 0$, it converges to a strict, hard selection of only the most confident token in the sampled sequence $\mathbf{z}$.

<details>
<summary>Example: one sampling step with a soft greedy planner</summary>
<div>

To illustrate one sampling step, take the vocabulary $\mathcal{V}=\{A,B,m\}$ and sequences of length $S=3$.
Suppose that after one planner step ($k=1$), the current sequence is
$$

so positions $2$ and $3$ are still masked.

$$
For this sampled candidate, the planner only looks at the denoiser confidence assigned to the proposed token at each still-masked position, and assigns zero mass to positions that are already unmasked.

If the planner samples position $3$, then the next sequence is obtained by copying only that proposed token:
$$
\begin{equation}

\end{equation}
$$

#### Exposure Bias

Introducing a planner-aware sampling process creates a mismatch between training and inference. During training, the standard objective masks tokens uniformly at random across all positions and orderings. During inference, the planner reveals tokens along a specific, confidence-guided trajectory.

This difference matters because uniform masking can put training weight on mask patterns that the planner would almost never visit in practice. For example, in a structured sequence where the last token is only predictable after the first two have been revealed, uniform masking may still train the model to predict that last token from $(m,m,m)$ or from $(m,m,B)$, even though a confidence-based planner would first reveal easier positions and avoid asking for that token with so little context. These hard denoising tasks can inject extra error into the learned denoiser, and those errors can then be amplified during sampling when early wrong predictions become part of the context.

A fix is to reweight the training loss toward the positions the planner would select. This gives the Planner-Aware Path Learning (PAPL) objective:
$$
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:papl-loss}
\mathcal{L}_{\text{PAPL}}(\theta) = - \mathbb{E}_{\substack{t \sim \mathcal{U}(0,1) \\ \mathbf{x}_1 \sim p_{\text{data}} \\ \mathbf{x}_t \sim p_t(\cdot | \mathbf{x}_1)}} \left[ \sum_{i=1}^S \mathbb{I}(x_t^i = m) \frac{1}{1-t}(1 + \alpha \operatorname{sg}[w^i]) \log D_{\theta,t}^i(\mathbf{x}_t)_{x_1^i} \right]
\end{equation}
$$
Here, $w^i = G_\phi(\mathbf{z}, \mathbf{x}_t)_i$ is the normalized planner weight for position $i$, and $\alpha$ controls the strength of this guidance. The stop-gradient $\operatorname{sg}[\cdot]$ detaches these weights: for example, soft greedy weights depend on $\theta$ through $\operatorname{softmax}_i\left(\frac{1}{\tau}\log D_\theta^i(\mathbf{x}_t)_{z^i}\right)$, but gradients are not propagated through this dependence.
