- **$m$** mask token $m \notin \mathcal{V}$, represented as the one-hot vector $(0, \dots, 0, 1) \in \mathbb{R}^{|\mathcal{V}|+1}$
- **$\mathbf{x}^{\neq i}$** sub-sequence of $\mathbf{x}$ at all positions except $i$;
- **$p_0$** source distribution (mask or uniform)
- **$p_{\text{mask}}$** mask source distribution: $p_{\text{mask}}(v) = \mathbb{I}(v = m)$
- **$p_{\text{unif}}$** uniform source distribution: $p_{\text{unif}}(v) = \frac{1}{|\mathcal{V}|}$
- **$p_1$** target distribution
- **$p_t$** marginal distribution at time $t \in [0,1]$; the probability path representing the trajectory that transports $p_0$ to $p_1$
- **$p_t(\mathbf{x} | \mathbf{z}) = \prod_{j=1}^S q_t(x^j | z^j)$** factorized conditional probability path given a conditioning variable $z$
- **$q_t(x | z)$** token-wise conditional distribution given $z$.
- **$q_t(x | x_0, x_1)$** source-target token-wise conditional distribution: $q_t(x | x_0, x_1) = \kappa_t \mathbb{I}(x = x_1) + (1 - \kappa_t) \mathbb{I}(x = x_0)$
- **$\kappa_t$** interpolation schedule; boundary conditions are $\kappa_0 = 0$ and $\kappa_1 = 1$, with a natural choice being $\kappa_t = t$
- **$u_t(\mathbf{x})_{\mathbf{y}}$** probability velocity: the instantaneous rate at which the sequence transitions from $\mathbf{x}$ to $\mathbf{y}$ at time $t$
- **$u_t^i(\mathbf{x})_v$** token-wise velocity at position $i$: the instantaneous rate at which the $i$-th token of $\mathbf{x}$ transitions to $v$ at time $t$
- **$u_t(\mathbf{x} | \mathbf{z})_{\mathbf{y}}$** conditional velocity field given $z$; it relates to the marginal velocity via expectation, formulated as $u_t(\mathbf{x})_{\mathbf{y}} = \mathbb{E}_{Z | X_t = \mathbf{x}}[u_t(\mathbf{x} | Z)_{\mathbf{y}}] $
- **$u_t(x | x_0, x_1)$** source-target token-wise conditional velocity: $u_t(x | x_0, x_1)_v = \frac{\dot{\kappa}_t}{1 - \kappa_t} \left( \mathbb{I}(v = x_1) - \mathbb{I}(v = x) \right)$
- **$u_{\theta,t}^i(\mathbf{x})_v$** learned velocity field parameterized by $\theta$
- **$P_{\theta,t}(X_1^i | \mathbf{x}_t)$** learned denoiser: at coordinate j, conditional probability that the $i$-th token at time 1 is $\mathbf{e}_j$, given current sequence $\mathbf{x}_t$

Discrete diffusion models were introduced independently by Hoogeboom et al. and Austin et al. (both NeurIPS 2021), later extended to continuous time by Campbell et al. (NeurIPS 2022), and made competitive with autoregressive models by Lou et al. SEDD (ICML 2024). Discrete flow matching was then introduced by Campbell et al. (ICML 2024) and further generalized by Gat et al. (NeurIPS 2024), mirroring the shift from diffusion to flow matching seen in continuous spaces.
The main goal of this post is to explain the theory behind discrete flow matching and to provide a clear parallel with continuous flow matching.

### Discrete Flow Matching

#### Intuition of Discrete Flow Matching

Discrete flow matching was initially introduced as an alternative to autoregressive (AR) models for sequence generation. A fundamental limitation of current AR models is their reliance on the next-token prediction paradigm. This inherently restricts them to generating sequences one token at a time in a strict, left-to-right order.

While AR models excel at purely sequential generative task, such as answering a conversational prompt or writing a block of code from scratch, this architecture feels unnatural and inefficient for tasks that require bidirectional or global context:
- **Code infilling:** When missing tokens are scattered throughout a file, the model must condition heavily on both the few preceding and succeeding lines of code to fill the gaps accurately. To achieve this task with an AR model, we should give the full noisy code to the model before it starts filling, which is a counter-intuitive approach.
- **Solving Sudoku:** This is an inherently 2D spatial constraint problem. A number in a cell is constrained by its entire row, column, and subgrid, not just by the cells evaluated "before" it in a 1D sequence.
- **DNA sequence design:** Biological sequences rely on complex secondary structures and long-range dependencies. Parts of the sequence that are far apart often need to connect together like perfectly matched puzzle pieces so the molecule can fold into the right shape, making a purely left-to-right generation prone to structural errors.

By contrast, the paradigm of diffusion and flow matching is bidirectional. Throughout the generation process, every token is updated while simultaneously "seeing" both the past and the future of the sequence. This global context makes solving infilling, constraint-satisfaction games, and complex spatial tasks much more natural.

The primary objective of discrete flow matching is to define a process that smoothly transports a simple source distribution, denoted as $p_0$, into a complex target distribution (the actual data we want to generate), denoted as $p_1$. To achieve this, we construct a continuous transformation, the *flow* $f(\cdot, t): \mathcal{S} \times [0,1] \to \mathcal{S}$ such that the mapping
$$
\mathbf{x} \mapsto f(\mathbf{x}, t)
$$
satisfies $p_t = f(\cdot, t)_\sharp p_0$.

This means that for a sequence $\mathbf{x} \sim p_0$, we have $f(\mathbf{x}, t) \sim p_t$. Here, the probability path $p_t$ represents the intermediate states as it transports the source distribution $p_0$ to the target distribution $p_1$ over time $t \in [0,1]$.

In practice, we assume we have a dataset of $N$ samples $\mathcal{A} = \{a_1, \dots, a_N\}$ that follow the target distribution $p_1$.

The source distribution $p_0$ acts as our starting point for generation and is typically chosen to be either:
- **Mask distribution ($p_{\text{mask}}$):** All sequences start entirely filled with the mask token $m$.
- **Uniform distribution ($p_{\text{unif}}$):** All tokens in the starting sequence are sampled uniformly across the vocabulary $\mathcal{V}$.

For instance, if our goal is solving Sudoku, $p_0$ represents the theoretical distribution of initial states, like partially filled grids with masked unknowns ($p_{\text{mask}}$), while $\hat{p}_0$ denotes the $N$ empirical puzzle samples we actually draw. Conversely, the true target distribution $p_1$ defines the ideal space of all valid, completed Sudoku boards. Its empirical counterpart, $\hat{p}_1$, is formed by our training dataset $\mathcal{A}$, which consists of the exact $N$ solved grids corresponding to the initial puzzles in $\hat{p}_0$.

#### Generation of discrete data

To generate discrete data, we rely on a time-dependent vector field $u$ that guides the sampling process.

**Definition (Probability Velocity)**:
Let $u : [0,1] \times \mathcal{S} \to \mathbb{R}^{|\mathcal{S}|}$ be a time-dependent vector field. We say that $u$ *generates* the probability path $p_t$ if, for a random variable $X_t$ constructed via the standard sampling algorithm, it holds that $X_t \sim p_t$.

Intuitively, $u_t(\mathbf{x})_{\mathbf{y}}$ is the instantaneous rate at which the sequence transitions from $\mathbf{x}$ to $\mathbf{y}$ at time $t$. Therefore, for any $\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{y} \in \mathcal{S}$:
$$
\begin{equation}
\mathbb{P}(X_{t+h} = \mathbf{y} | X_t = \mathbf{x}) = \mathbb{I}(\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{y}) + h u_{t}(\mathbf{x})_{\mathbf{y}} + o(h)
\end{equation}
$$

In practice, this means that at each sampling step, the next state is drawn according to the following categorical distribution update:
$$
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{x}_{\tau + h} \sim \text{Cat}\left(\mathbf{e}_{\mathbf{x}_\tau} + h u_\tau(\mathbf{x}_\tau)\right)
\end{equation}
$$

The relationship between this velocity field and the marginal probability path is formalized by the Kolmogorov Equation.

**Theorem (Kolmogorov Equation)**:
The vector field $u$ generates $p_t$ if and only if for all $\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{y} \in \mathcal{S}$:
$$
\begin{equation}
\frac{d}{dt} p_t(\mathbf{y}) = \sum_{\mathbf{x} \in \mathcal{S}} u_{t}(\mathbf{x})_{\mathbf{y}} \, p_t(\mathbf{x})
\end{equation}
$$

<summary>Proof</summary>
<div>
We can derive this directly by expanding the marginal probability at $t+h$ using the law of total probability and our velocity definition:

$$
\begin{aligned}
p_{t+h}(\mathbf{y}) &= \mathbb{P}(X_{t+h} = \mathbf{y}) \\
&= \sum_{\mathbf{x} \in \mathcal{S}} \mathbb{P}(X_{t+h} = \mathbf{y} | X_t = \mathbf{x})\, \mathbb{P}(X_t = \mathbf{x}) \\
&= \sum_{\mathbf{x} \in \mathcal{S}} \left(\mathbb{I}(\mathbf{y} = \mathbf{x}) + h\,u_t(\mathbf{x})_{\mathbf{y}} + o(h)\right) p_t(\mathbf{x}) \\
&= p_t(\mathbf{y}) + h \sum_{\mathbf{x} \in \mathcal{S}} u_t(\mathbf{x})_{\mathbf{y}}\, p_t(\mathbf{x}) + o(h)
\end{aligned}
$$

Rearranging the terms and taking the limit as $h \to 0$ gives:

$$
\lim_{h \to 0} \frac{p_{t+h}(\mathbf{y}) - p_t(\mathbf{y})}{h} = \sum_{\mathbf{x} \in \mathcal{S}} u_t(\mathbf{x})_{\mathbf{y}}\, p_t(\mathbf{x})
$$
</div>
</details>

Because modeling the joint transition for an entire sequence over a large state space is computationally intractable, we typically make a **factorization assumption**. We assume that the transition probabilities factorize over the tokens, meaning during generation transitions are applied independently to each position of the sequence. Concretely, for $\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{y} \in \mathcal{S}$:
$$
\begin{align}
\mathbb{P}(X_{t+h} = \mathbf{y} | X_t = \mathbf{x}) &= \mathbb{P}(X_{t+h}^1 = y_1, X_{t+h}^2 = y_2, \dots, X_{t+h}^S = y_S | X_t = \mathbf{x}) \\
&= \prod_{i=1}^S \mathbb{P}(X_{t+h}^i = y^i | X_t = \mathbf{x})
\end{align}
$$

This factorization leads us to write the overall velocity field as a sum of individual token-wise velocities:
$$
\begin{equation}
u_t(\mathbf{x})_{\mathbf{y}} = \sum_{i=1}^S \mathbb{I}(\mathbf{y}^{\neq i} = \mathbf{x}^{\neq i}) u_t^i(\mathbf{x})_{y^i}
\end{equation}
$$

<details>
<summary>Derivation of the factorized velocity</summary>
<div>
By definition, the transition probability for a single token at position $i$ is:
$$
\mathbb{P}(X_{t+h}^i = y^i | X_t = \mathbf{x}) = \mathbb{I}(y^i = x^i) + h u_t^i(\mathbf{x})_{y^i} + o(h)
$$

Substituting this into our factorized joint probability gives:
$$
\mathbb{P}(X_{t+h} = \mathbf{y} | X_t = \mathbf{x}) = \prod_{i=1}^S \left[ \mathbb{I}(y^i = x^i) + h u_t^i(\mathbf{x})_{y^i} + o(h) \right]
$$

Expanding this product and keeping only the first-order terms in $h$, we get:
$$
\mathbb{P}(X_{t+h} = \mathbf{y} | X_t = \mathbf{x}) = \prod_{i=1}^S \mathbb{I}(y^i = x^i) + h \sum_{i=1}^S \left[ \mathbb{I}(\mathbf{y}^{\neq i} = \mathbf{x}^{\neq i}) u_t^i(\mathbf{x})_{y^i} \right] + o(h)
$$
where $\mathbb{I}(\mathbf{y}^{\neq i} = \mathbf{x}^{\neq i})$ equals 1 if $\mathbf{y}$ and $\mathbf{x}$ differ at most at position $i$.

Recognizing that $\prod_{i=1}^S \mathbb{I}(y^i = x^i) = \mathbb{I}(\mathbf{y} = \mathbf{x})$, we can rewrite the expansion as:
$$
\mathbb{P}(X_{t+h} = \mathbf{y} | X_t = \mathbf{x}) = \mathbb{I}(\mathbf{y} = \mathbf{x}) + h \sum_{i=1}^S \mathbb{I}(\mathbf{y}^{\neq i} = \mathbf{x}^{\neq i}) u_t^i(\mathbf{x})_{y^i} + o(h)
$$

By comparing this result with the fundamental definition of the probability velocity $\mathbb{P}(X_{t+h} = \mathbf{y} | X_t = \mathbf{x}) = \mathbb{I}(\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{y}) + h u_t(\mathbf{x})_{\mathbf{y}} + o(h)$, we directly identify:
$$
u_t(\mathbf{x})_{\mathbf{y}} = \sum_{i=1}^S \mathbb{I}(\mathbf{y}^{\neq i} = \mathbf{x}^{\neq i}) u_t^i(\mathbf{x})_{y^i}
$$
</div>
</details>

This implies that for infinitesimally small time steps, each sequence $\mathbf{x}$ is assumed to transition only to states where at most one token differs. By decoupling the updates, we reduce the computational complexity from exponential in the sequence length to linear.

Therefore, at each sampling step, we draw each token independently following:
$$
\begin{equation}
x_{\tau+h}^i \sim \text{Cat}\left(\mathbf{e}_{x_\tau^i} + h u_\tau^i(\mathbf{x}_\tau)\right)
\end{equation}
$$

#### Modelling Choices

Similarly to continuous flow matching, there are infinitely many possible choices for the probability path $\{p_t\}$. Because the true target data distribution $p_1$ is highly complex and unknown, it is extremely difficult to choose a global probability path or its associated velocity field directly.

To solve this, we borrow the core idea from Conditional Flow Matching (CFM). Instead of defining the global path directly, we choose a conditioning variable $\mathbf{z}$ and define a *conditional* probability path $p_t(\cdot | \mathbf{z})$. If designed correctly, this approach guarantees two things:
1. The induced marginal probability path $p_t$ successfully transforms $p_0$ into $p_1$.
2. The associated conditional velocity field $u_t(\cdot | \mathbf{z})$ has a simple, analytic form that we can easily calculate and use for training.

We define the conditioning variable $\mathbf{z} \in \mathcal{Z}^S$ to be either:
* **A target sample:** $\mathbf{z} = \mathbf{x}_1$ (useful when the source $p_0$ is a known, simple distribution like uniform or mask).
* **A source-target pair:** $\mathbf{z} = (\mathbf{x}_0, \mathbf{x}_1)$ (useful when transforming between two arbitrary datasets).

For all conditioning variables $\mathbf{z}$ considered below, we assume a fully factorized conditional probability path:
$$
\begin{equation}
p_t(\mathbf{x} | \mathbf{z}) = \prod_{j=1}^S q_t(x^j | z^j)
\end{equation}
$$
Concretely, this means that to sample a sequence $\mathbf{x} \sim p_t( \cdot | \mathbf{z})$, we can just sample each token $x^j$ independently from its token-wise distribution $q_t( \cdot | z^j)$. This factorization is what allows us to define the conditional probability $q_t$ and the conditional velocity $u_t$ on a purely per-token basis.

Below are the explicit formulations for the masked source, the others are developed in the appendix.

At time $t$, the token is the target $x_1^i$ with probability $t$, or it remains the mask token $m$ with probability $1 - t$:
$$
\begin{equation}
q_t(x^i | x_1^i) = t \mathbb{I}(x^i = x_1^i) + (1 - t) \mathbb{I}(x^i = m)
\end{equation}
$$

The conditional velocity field driving this transition is:
$$
\begin{equation}
u_t(x^i | x_1^i)_v = \begin{cases}
\dfrac{1}{1-t} & \text{if } v = x_1^i \text{ and } x^i = m, \\[0.3cm]
-\dfrac{1}{1-t} & \text{if } v = x^i = m, \\[0.3cm]
0 & \text{otherwise}.
\end{cases}
\end{equation}
$$

Concrete example with a vocabulary of 3 tokens $\{1, 2, 3\}$ and a mask token $m$, where the target is $x_1^i = 2$:

$$
u_t(x^i | x_1^i = 2)_v = \begin{array}{c|cccc}
x^i \backslash v & 1 & 2 & 3 & m \\
\hline
1 & 0 & 0 & 0 & 0 \\[0.3cm]
2 & 0 & 0 & 0 & 0 \\[0.3cm]
3 & 0 & 0 & 0 & 0 \\[0.3cm]
m & 0 & \dfrac{1}{1-t} & 0 & -\dfrac{1}{1-t}
\end{array}
$$

**Useful note:** For all three cases detailed above, the conditional velocity can actually be unified and written in a more compact form:
$$
\begin{equation}
u_t(x^i | z^i) = \frac{1}{1-t} \left( \mathbf{e}_{z^i} - \mathbf{e}_{x^i} \right)
\end{equation}
$$

#### From Conditional to Unconditional Velocity

Now that we have defined our conditional probability paths and velocities, we need to connect them back to the global, unconditional process.

The overall marginal distribution $p_t$ is obtained simply by marginalizing over all possible conditional paths:
$$
\begin{equation}
p_t(\mathbf{x}) = \sum_{\mathbf{z}} p_t(\mathbf{x} | \mathbf{z}) \pi(\mathbf{z})
\end{equation}
$$
where $\pi(\mathbf{z})$ is the distribution over the conditioning variable $\mathbf{z}$ used during training.
{/* Maybe =choice to be consistent*/}.

Just as the marginal probability is an average of conditional probabilities, the marginal velocity field $u_t(\mathbf{x})_{\mathbf{y}}$ can be expressed as the expectation of the conditional velocity over the conditioning variable $\mathbf{z}$:
$$
\begin{equation}
u_t(\mathbf{x})_{\mathbf{y}} = \mathbb{E}_{Z | X_t = \mathbf{x}}[u_t(\mathbf{x} | Z)_{\mathbf{y}}] = \sum_{\mathbf{z}} u_t(\mathbf{x} | \mathbf{z})_{\mathbf{y}} \mathbb{P}(Z = \mathbf{z} | X_t = \mathbf{x})
\end{equation}
$$

**Learning with Conditional Velocity Fields**

To train our model, we use the KL divergence to measure the difference between velocity fields. This choice is natural: it arises from the KL divergence between the probability paths generated by the true and learned velocity fields. The ideal discrete flow matching loss minimizes the KL between the true marginal velocity $u_t$ and our learned velocity $u_{\theta,t}$:

$$
\begin{equation}
L_{\text{DFM}}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{\substack{t \sim \mathcal{U}(0,1) \\ \mathbf{x} \sim p_t}}\left[ D_{\text{KL}}\left(u_t(\mathbf{x}) \,\|\, u_{\theta,t}(\mathbf{x})\right) \right]
\end{equation}
$$

However, $u_t(\mathbf{x})$ requires marginalizing over the entire dataset, making it computationally intractable. Following the approach of continuous flow matching, we instead use the **conditional discrete flow matching loss**:

$$
\begin{equation}
L_{\text{CDFM}}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{\substack{t \sim \mathcal{U}(0,1) \\ \mathbf{z} \sim \pi \\ \mathbf{x} \sim p_t( \cdot | \mathbf{z})}}\left[ D_{\text{KL}}\left(u_t(\mathbf{x} | \mathbf{z}) \,\|\, u_{\theta,t}(\mathbf{x})\right) \right]
\end{equation}
$$

**Theorem:** Regressing the neural network $u_\theta$ against the known conditional velocity field $u_t(\mathbf{x} | \mathbf{z})$ using the loss $L_{\text{CDFM}}(\theta)$ is mathematically equivalent to directly regressing $u_\theta$ against the intractable, unknown marginal velocity field $u_t(\mathbf{x})$ using the loss $L_{\text{DFM}}(\theta)$.

In practice, thanks to our factorization assumption, the loss decomposes as a sum over individual token positions:

$$
\begin{equation}
L_{\text{CDFM}}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{\substack{t \sim \mathcal{U}(0,1) \\ \mathbf{z} \sim \pi \\ \mathbf{x} \sim p_t( \cdot | \mathbf{z})}}\left[ \sum_{i=1}^S D_{\text{KL}}\left( u_t(x^i | z^i) \,\|\, u_{\theta,t}^i(\mathbf{x}) \right) \right]
\end{equation}
$$

#### Denoiser

In discrete spaces, directly predicting a continuous vector field or instantaneous transition rates can feel unintuitive. Instead, a much more natural object to learn is a **denoiser**.

A denoiser simply observes the current noisy, corrupted, or partially masked sequence $\mathbf{x}_t$ at time $t$ and attempts to predict the true, clean target token $X_1^i$ for each position $i$. We denote this learned neural network distribution as $P_{\theta,t}(X_1^i | \mathbf{x})$.

We can directly connect the denoiser and velocity through this simple formula:

$$
\begin{equation}
u_t^i(\mathbf{x})_v = \frac{1}{1 - t} \left( \mathbb{P}(X_1^i = v | X_t = \mathbf{x}) - \mathbb{I}(v = x^i) \right)
\end{equation}
$$

Because the term $\mathbb{I}(v = x^i)$ is a known constant given the current state, learning the velocity field $u$ is mathematically exactly equivalent to learning the denoiser probability $\mathbb{P}(X_1^i = v | X_t = \mathbf{x})$.

Consequently, we can train the denoiser directly. Using the KL divergence between the true clean token distribution and the learned one, the loss simplifies to the standard cross-entropy:

$$
\begin{equation}
L_{\text{CDM}}(\theta) = -\mathbb{E}_{\substack{t \sim \mathcal{U}(0,1) \\ \mathbf{z} \sim \pi \\ \mathbf{x} \sim p_t( \cdot | \mathbf{z})}} \left[ \sum_{i=1}^S \log P_{\theta,t}(X_1^i = x_1^i | \mathbf{x}) \right]
\end{equation}
$$

**Note:** In practice, particularly when working with masked diffusion models, the adopted approach is to compute this loss exclusively on the positions that are currently masked. The model learns only how to denoise the corrupted tokens, and it is not penalized or trained to explicitly "keep" the unmasked tokens that are already visible.

#### Other conditioning choices

In the main text we detailed the mask source conditioning. Here we present the two other common setups.

##### Source-Target Conditioning

Here, we condition on both the start and end points, so $z^i = (x_0^i, x_1^i)$. The token $x^i$ at time $t$ is simply $x_1^i$ with probability $t$, and $x_0^i$ with probability $1 - t$:
$$
\begin{equation}
q_t(x^i | x_0^i, x_1^i) = t \mathbb{I}(x^i = x_1^i) + (1 - t) \mathbb{I}(x^i = x_0^i)
\end{equation}
$$

The conditional velocity field driving this transition is:
$$
\begin{equation}
u_t(x^i | x_0^i, x_1^i)_v = \begin{cases}
\dfrac{1}{1 - t} & \text{if } v = x_1^i \text{ and } x^i = x_0^i, \\[0.3cm]
-\dfrac{1}{1 - t} & \text{if } v = x^i = x_0^i, \\[0.3cm]
0 & \text{otherwise}.
\end{cases}
\end{equation}
$$

##### Uniform Source

When $p_0$ is the uniform distribution, we can only condition on the target $z^i = x_1^i$. At time $t$, the token is the target $x_1^i$ with probability $t + (1 - t) \frac{1}{|\mathcal{V}|}$, or is drawn uniformly from other tokens in the vocabulary $\mathcal{V}$ with probability $(1 - t) \frac{1}{|\mathcal{V}|}$:
$$
\begin{equation}
q_t(x^i | x_1^i) = t \mathbb{I}(x^i = x_1^i) + (1 - t) \frac{1}{|\mathcal{V}|}
\end{equation}
$$

The conditional velocity field driving this transition is:
$$
\begin{equation}
u_t(x^i | x_1^i)_v = \begin{cases}
\dfrac{1}{1-t} & \text{if } v = x_1^i \text{ and } x^i \neq x_1^i \\[0.3cm]
-\dfrac{1}{1-t} & \text{if } v = x^i \neq x_1^i \\[0.3cm]
0 & \text{otherwise}
\end{cases}
\end{equation}
$$

#### Planner aware path learning

Vanilla sampling in masked discrete diffusion and flow matching is computationally expensive. It requires $(1/h) \times S$ sequential steps, where $h$ is the time step size and $S$ is the length of the sequence. At every single micro-step, we must compute and draw from probability vectors of size $|\mathcal{V}|$ for all tokens. For example, with a step size of $h = 0.01$ and a sequence length of $S = 3000$, we would need an impractical $300,000$ steps to generate a single sample.

To make generation tractable, the standard approach used in the industry for large masked diffusion models is **planner-guided sampling**. Instead of relying on infinitesimal continuous-time steps, we fix a manageable number of total generation steps, denoted as $N$. At each step, the algorithm aggressively decodes and fixes exactly $S/N$ tokens.

**Algorithm: Planner-Guided Sampling**

**Input:** Planner strategy, trained denoiser $D_\theta$, total number of steps $N$
1. **Initialize:** $\mathbf{x}_0 \leftarrow (m, \dots, m)$ (a sequence of $S$ mask tokens)
2. **For** $k = 0, 1, \dots, N - 1$:
    * **Plan:** Sample candidate tokens $\mathbf{z} \sim D_\theta(\mathbf{x}_k)$
    * **Select:** A "planner" evaluates these candidate predictions based on a specific criterion. It chooses a set of positions $\mathcal{I}$ where $|\mathcal{I}| = S/N$ to reveal.
    * **Denoise:** Create $\mathbf{x}_{k+1}$ by setting $x_{k+1}^i = z^i$ for all $i \in \mathcal{I}$, keeping the rest as masks
3. **Return** fully generated sequence $\mathbf{x}_N$

Why learning a new loss with a planner
Loss for a greedy planner (just a weight 1 + alpha w_i).
