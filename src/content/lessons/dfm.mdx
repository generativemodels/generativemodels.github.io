- **$\mathcal{V}$** vocabulary set; $|\mathcal{V}|$ is the vocabulary size

- **$m$** mask token $m \notin \mathcal{V}$, represented as the one-hot vector $(0, \dots, 0, 1) \in \mathbb{R}^{|\mathcal{V}|+1}$
- **$\mathbf{x}^{\neq i}$** sub-sequence of $\mathbf{x}$ at all positions except $i$;
- **$p_0$** source distribution (mask or uniform)
- **$p_{\text{mask}}$** mask source distribution: $p_{\text{mask}}(v) = \mathbb{I}(v = m)$
- **$p_{\text{unif}}$** uniform source distribution: $p_{\text{unif}}(v) = \frac{1}{|\mathcal{V}|}$
- **$p_1$** target distribution
- **$p_t$** marginal distribution at time $t \in [0,1]$; the probability path representing the trajectory that transports $p_0$ to $p_1$
- **$p_t(\mathbf{x} | \mathbf{z}) = \prod_{j=1}^S q_t(x^j | z^j)$** factorized conditional probability path given a conditioning variable $z$
- **$q_t(x | z)$** token-wise conditional distribution given $z$.
- **$q_t(x | x_0, x_1)$** source-target token-wise conditional distribution: $q_t(x | x_0, x_1) = \kappa_t \mathbb{I}(x = x_1) + (1 - \kappa_t) \mathbb{I}(x = x_0)$
- **$\kappa_t$** interpolation schedule; boundary conditions are $\kappa_0 = 0$ and $\kappa_1 = 1$, with a natural choice being $\kappa_t = t$
- **$u_t(\mathbf{x})_{\mathbf{y}}$** probability velocity: the instantaneous rate at which the sequence transitions from $\mathbf{x}$ to $\mathbf{y}$ at time $t$
- **$u_t^i(\mathbf{x})_v$** token-wise velocity at position $i$: the instantaneous rate at which the $i$-th token of $\mathbf{x}$ transitions to $v$ at time $t$
- **$u_t(\mathbf{x} | \mathbf{z})_{\mathbf{y}}$** conditional velocity field given $z$; it relates to the marginal velocity via expectation, formulated as $u_t(\mathbf{x})_{\mathbf{y}} = \mathbb{E}_{Z | X_t = \mathbf{x}}[u_t(\mathbf{x} | Z)_{\mathbf{y}}] $
- **$u_t(x | x_0, x_1)$** source-target token-wise conditional velocity: $u_t(x | x_0, x_1)_v = \frac{\dot{\kappa}_t}{1 - \kappa_t} \left( \mathbb{I}(v = x_1) - \mathbb{I}(v = x) \right)$
- **$u_{\theta,t}^i(\mathbf{x})_v$** learned velocity field parameterized by $\theta$
- **$P_{\theta,t}(X_1^i | \mathbf{x}_t)$** learned denoiser: at coordinate j, conditional probability that the $i$-th token at time 1 is $\mathbf{e}_j$, given current sequence $\mathbf{x}_t$

Discrete diffusion models were introduced independently by Hoogeboom et al. and Austin et al. (both NeurIPS 2021), later extended to continuous time by Campbell et al. (NeurIPS 2022), and made competitive with autoregressive models by Lou et al. SEDD (ICML 2024). Discrete flow matching was then introduced by Campbell et al. (ICML 2024) and further generalized by Gat et al. (NeurIPS 2024), mirroring the shift from diffusion to flow matching seen in continuous spaces.
The main goal of this post is to explain the theory behind discrete flow matching and to provide a clear parallel with continuous flow matching.

### Discrete Flow Matching

#### Intuition of Discrete Flow Matching

Discrete flow matching was initially introduced as an alternative to autoregressive (AR) models for sequence generation. A fundamental limitation of current AR models is their reliance on the next-token prediction paradigm. This inherently restricts them to generating sequences one token at a time in a strict, left-to-right order.

While AR models excel at purely sequential generative task, such as answering a conversational prompt or writing a block of code from scratch, this architecture feels unnatural and inefficient for tasks that require bidirectional or global context:
- **Code infilling:** When missing tokens are scattered throughout a file, the model must condition heavily on both the few preceding and succeeding lines of code to fill the gaps accurately. To achieve this task with an AR model, we should give the full noisy code to the model before it starts filling, which is a counter-intuitive approach.
- **Solving Sudoku:** This is an inherently 2D spatial constraint problem. A number in a cell is constrained by its entire row, column, and subgrid, not just by the cells evaluated "before" it in a 1D sequence.
- **DNA sequence design:** Biological sequences rely on complex secondary structures and long-range dependencies. Parts of the sequence that are far apart often need to connect together like perfectly matched puzzle pieces so the molecule can fold into the right shape, making a purely left-to-right generation prone to structural errors.

By contrast, the paradigm of diffusion and flow matching is bidirectional. Throughout the generation process, every token is updated while simultaneously "seeing" both the past and the future of the sequence. This global context makes solving infilling, constraint-satisfaction games, and complex spatial tasks much more natural.

The primary objective of discrete flow matching is to define a process that smoothly transports a simple source distribution, denoted as $p_0$, into a complex target distribution (the actual data we want to generate), denoted as $p_1$. To achieve this, we construct a continuous transformation, the *flow* $f(\cdot, t): \mathcal{S} \times [0,1] \to \mathcal{S}$ such that the mapping
$$
\mathbf{x} \mapsto f(\mathbf{x}, t)
$$
satisfies $p_t = f(\cdot, t)_\sharp p_0$.

This means that for a sequence $\mathbf{x} \sim p_0$, we have $f(\mathbf{x}, t) \sim p_t$. Here, the probability path $p_t$ represents the intermediate states as it transports the source distribution $p_0$ to the target distribution $p_1$ over time $t \in [0,1]$.

In practice, we assume we have a dataset of $N$ samples $\mathcal{A} = \{a_1, \dots, a_N\}$ that follow the target distribution $p_1$.

The source distribution $p_0$ acts as our starting point for generation and is typically chosen to be either:
- **Mask distribution ($p_{\text{mask}}$):** All sequences start entirely filled with the mask token $m$.
- **Uniform distribution ($p_{\text{unif}}$):** All tokens in the starting sequence are sampled uniformly across the vocabulary $\mathcal{V}$.

For instance, if our goal is solving Sudoku, $p_0$ represents the theoretical distribution of initial states, like partially filled grids with masked unknowns ($p_{\text{mask}}$), while $\hat{p}_0$ denotes the $N$ empirical puzzle samples we actually draw. Conversely, the true target distribution $p_1$ defines the ideal space of all valid, completed Sudoku boards. Its empirical counterpart, $\hat{p}_1$, is formed by our training dataset $\mathcal{A}$, which consists of the exact $N$ solved grids corresponding to the initial puzzles in $\hat{p}_0$.

#### Generation of discrete data

To generate discrete data, we rely on a time-dependent vector field $u$ that guides the sampling process.

**Definition (Probability Velocity)**:
Let $u : [0,1] \times \mathcal{S} \to \mathbb{R}^{|\mathcal{S}|}$ be a time-dependent vector field. We say that $u$ *generates* the probability path $p_t$ if, for a random variable $X_t$ constructed via the standard sampling algorithm, it holds that $X_t \sim p_t$.

Intuitively, $u_t(\mathbf{x})_{\mathbf{y}}$ is the instantaneous rate at which the sequence transitions from $\mathbf{x}$ to $\mathbf{y}$ at time $t$. Therefore, for any $\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{y} \in \mathcal{S}$:
$$
\begin{equation}
\mathbb{P}(X_{t+h} = \mathbf{y} | X_t = \mathbf{x}) = \mathbb{I}(\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{y}) + h u_{t}(\mathbf{x})_{\mathbf{y}} + o(h)
\end{equation}
$$

In practice, this means that at each sampling step, the next state is drawn according to the following categorical distribution update:
$$
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{x}_{\tau + h} \sim \text{Cat}\left(\mathbf{e}_{\mathbf{x}_\tau} + h u_\tau(\mathbf{x}_\tau)\right)
\end{equation}
$$

The relationship between this velocity field and the marginal probability path is formalized by the Kolmogorov Equation.

**Theorem (Kolmogorov Equation)**:
The vector field $u$ generates $p_t$ if and only if for all $\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{y} \in \mathcal{S}$:
$$
\begin{equation}
\frac{d}{dt} p_t(\mathbf{y}) = \sum_{\mathbf{x} \in \mathcal{S}} u_{t}(\mathbf{x})_{\mathbf{y}} \, p_t(\mathbf{x})
\end{equation}
$$

<details>
<summary>Proof</summary>
<div>
We can derive this directly by expanding the marginal probability at $t+h$ using the law of total probability and our velocity definition:

$$
\begin{align}
p_{t+h}(\mathbf{y}) &= \mathbb{P}(X_{t+h} = \mathbf{y}) \\
&= \sum_{\mathbf{x} \in \mathcal{S}} \mathbb{P}(X_{t+h} = \mathbf{y} | X_t = \mathbf{x})\, \mathbb{P}(X_t = \mathbf{x}) \\
&= \sum_{\mathbf{x} \in \mathcal{S}} \left(\mathbb{I}(\mathbf{y} = \mathbf{x}) + h\,u_t(\mathbf{x})_{\mathbf{y}} + o(h)\right) p_t(\mathbf{x}) \\
&= p_t(\mathbf{y}) + h \sum_{\mathbf{x} \in \mathcal{S}} u_t(\mathbf{x})_{\mathbf{y}}\, p_t(\mathbf{x}) + o(h)
\end{align}
$$

Rearranging the terms and taking the limit as $h \to 0$ gives:

$$
\begin{equation}
\lim_{h \to 0} \frac{p_{t+h}(\mathbf{y}) - p_t(\mathbf{y})}{h} = \sum_{\mathbf{x} \in \mathcal{S}} u_t(\mathbf{x})_{\mathbf{y}}\, p_t(\mathbf{x})
\end{equation}
$$

For the reverse implication (if the equation holds, then $u$ generates $p_t$), let $\tilde{p}_t$ denote the true marginal probability path generated by simulating the continuous-time Markov chain with the velocity field $u_t$, starting from the initial distribution $\tilde{p}_0 = p_0$. By the forward implication proven above, $\tilde{p}_t$ strictly satisfies the Kolmogorov forward equation:

$$
\begin{align}
\frac{d}{dt} \tilde{p}_t(\mathbf{y}) &= \sum_{\mathbf{x} \in \mathcal{S}} u_t(\mathbf{x})_{\mathbf{y}}\, \tilde{p}_t(\mathbf{x})
\end{align}
$$

By hypothesis, the given probability path $p_t$ also satisfies this exact same linear ordinary differential equation (ODE) system. Because both $p_t$ and $\tilde{p}_t$ are solutions to the same system of first-order linear ODEs and share the identical initial condition ($p_0 = \tilde{p}_0$), the Picard-Lindelöf uniqueness theorem for ordinary differential equations guarantees that $p_t(\mathbf{y}) = \tilde{p}_t(\mathbf{y})$ for all times $t$ and all states $\mathbf{y} \in \mathcal{S}$. Therefore, the vector field $u_t$ exactly generates the probability path $p_t$.

Because modeling the joint transition for an entire sequence over a large state space is computationally intractable, we typically make a **factorization assumption**. We assume that the transition probabilities factorize over the tokens, meaning during generation transitions are applied independently to each position of the sequence. Concretely, for $\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{y} \in \mathcal{S}$:
$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathbb{P}(X_{t+h} = \mathbf{y} | X_t = \mathbf{x}) &= \mathbb{P}(X_{t+h}^1 = y_1, X_{t+h}^2 = y_2, \dots, X_{t+h}^S = y_S | X_t = \mathbf{x}) \\
&= \prod_{i=1}^S \mathbb{P}(X_{t+h}^i = y^i | X_t = \mathbf{x})
\end{aligned}
\begin{equation} \end{equation}
$$

This factorization leads us to write the overall velocity field as a sum of individual token-wise velocities:
$$
\begin{equation}
u_t(\mathbf{x})_{\mathbf{y}} = \sum_{i=1}^S \mathbb{I}(\mathbf{y}^{\neq i} = \mathbf{x}^{\neq i}) u_t^i(\mathbf{x})_{y^i}
\end{equation}
$$

<div>
By definition, the transition probability for a single token at position $i$ is:
$$
\mathbb{P}(X_{t+h}^i = y^i | X_t = \mathbf{x}) = \mathbb{I}(y^i = x^i) + h u_t^i(\mathbf{x})_{y^i} + o(h)
$$

Substituting this into our factorized joint probability gives:
$$
\mathbb{P}(X_{t+h} = \mathbf{y} | X_t = \mathbf{x}) = \prod_{i=1}^S \left[ \mathbb{I}(y^i = x^i) + h u_t^i(\mathbf{x})_{y^i} + o(h) \right]
$$

Expanding this product and keeping only the first-order terms in $h$, we get:
$$
\mathbb{P}(X_{t+h} = \mathbf{y} | X_t = \mathbf{x}) = \prod_{i=1}^S \mathbb{I}(y^i = x^i) + h \sum_{i=1}^S \left[ \mathbb{I}(\mathbf{y}^{\neq i} = \mathbf{x}^{\neq i}) u_t^i(\mathbf{x})_{y^i} \right] + o(h)
$$
where $\mathbb{I}(\mathbf{y}^{\neq i} = \mathbf{x}^{\neq i})$ equals 1 if $\mathbf{y}$ and $\mathbf{x}$ differ at most at position $i$.

Recognizing that $\prod_{i=1}^S \mathbb{I}(y^i = x^i) = \mathbb{I}(\mathbf{y} = \mathbf{x})$, we can rewrite the expansion as:
$$
\mathbb{P}(X_{t+h} = \mathbf{y} | X_t = \mathbf{x}) = \mathbb{I}(\mathbf{y} = \mathbf{x}) + h \sum_{i=1}^S \mathbb{I}(\mathbf{y}^{\neq i} = \mathbf{x}^{\neq i}) u_t^i(\mathbf{x})_{y^i} + o(h)
$$

By comparing this result with the fundamental definition of the probability velocity $\mathbb{P}(X_{t+h} = \mathbf{y} | X_t = \mathbf{x}) = \mathbb{I}(\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{y}) + h u_t(\mathbf{x})_{\mathbf{y}} + o(h)$, we directly identify:
$$
u_t(\mathbf{x})_{\mathbf{y}} = \sum_{i=1}^S \mathbb{I}(\mathbf{y}^{\neq i} = \mathbf{x}^{\neq i}) u_t^i(\mathbf{x})_{y^i}
$$

This implies that for infinitesimally small time steps, each sequence $\mathbf{x}$ is assumed to transition only to states where at most one token differs. By decoupling the updates, we reduce the computational complexity from exponential in the sequence length to linear.

Therefore, at each sampling step, we draw each token independently following:
$$
\begin{equation}
x_{\tau+h}^i \sim \text{Cat}\left(\mathbf{e}_{x_\tau^i} + h u_\tau^i(\mathbf{x}_\tau)\right)
\end{equation}
$$

#### Modelling Choices

Similarly to continuous flow matching, there are infinitely many possible choices for the probability path $\{p_t\}$. Because the true target data distribution $p_1$ is highly complex and unknown, it is extremely difficult to choose a global probability path or its associated velocity field directly.

To solve this, we borrow the core idea from Conditional Flow Matching (CFM). Instead of defining the global path directly, we choose a conditioning variable $\mathbf{z}$ and define a *conditional* probability path $p_t(\cdot | \mathbf{z})$. If designed correctly, this approach guarantees two things:
1. The induced marginal probability path $p_t$ successfully transforms $p_0$ into $p_1$.
2. The associated conditional velocity field $u_t(\cdot | \mathbf{z})$ has a simple, analytic form that we can easily calculate and use for training.

We define the conditioning variable $\mathbf{z} \in \mathcal{Z}^S$ to be either:
* **A target sample:** $\mathbf{z} = \mathbf{x}_1$ (useful when the source $p_0$ is a known, simple distribution like uniform or mask).
* **A source-target pair:** $\mathbf{z} = (\mathbf{x}_0, \mathbf{x}_1)$ (useful when transforming between two arbitrary datasets).

For all conditioning variables $\mathbf{z}$ considered below, we assume a fully factorized conditional probability path:
$$
\begin{equation}
p_t(\mathbf{x} | \mathbf{z}) = \prod_{j=1}^S q_t(x^j | z^j)
\end{equation}
$$
Concretely, this means that to sample a sequence $\mathbf{x} \sim p_t( \cdot | \mathbf{z})$, we can just sample each token $x^j$ independently from its token-wise distribution $q_t( \cdot | z^j)$. This factorization is what allows us to define the conditional probability $q_t$ and the conditional velocity $u_t$ on a purely per-token basis.

Below are the explicit formulations for three common setups:

**Masked Source**

At time $t$, the token is the target $x_1^i$ with probability $t$, or it remains the mask token $m$ with probability $1 - t$:
$$
\begin{equation}
q_t(x^i | x_1^i) = t \mathbb{I}(x^i = x_1^i) + (1 - t) \mathbb{I}(x^i = m)
\end{equation}
$$

The conditional velocity field driving this transition is:
$$
\begin{equation}
u_t(x^i | x_1^i)_v = \begin{cases}
\dfrac{1}{1-t} & \text{if } v = x_1^i \text{ and } x^i = m, \\[0.3cm]
-\dfrac{1}{1-t} & \text{if } v = x^i = m, \\[0.3cm]
0 & \text{otherwise}.
\end{cases}
\end{equation}
$$

Concrete example with a vocabulary of 3 tokens $\{1, 2, 3\}$ and a mask token $m$, where the target is $x_1^i = 2$:

$$
u_t(x^i | x_1^i = 2)_v = \begin{array}{c|cccc}
x^i \backslash v & 1 & 2 & 3 & m \\
\hline
1 & 0 & 0 & 0 & 0 \\[0.3cm]
2 & 0 & 0 & 0 & 0 \\[0.3cm]
3 & 0 & 0 & 0 & 0 \\[0.3cm]
m & 0 & \dfrac{1}{1-t} & 0 & -\dfrac{1}{1-t}
\end{array}
$$

**Source-Target Conditioning**

Here, we condition on both the start and end points, so $z^i = (x_0^i, x_1^i)$. The token $x^i$ at time $t$ is simply $x_1^i$ with probability $t$, and $x_0^i$ with probability $1 - t$:
$$
\begin{equation}
q_t(x^i | x_0^i, x_1^i) = t \mathbb{I}(x^i = x_1^i) + (1 - t) \mathbb{I}(x^i = x_0^i)
\end{equation}
$$

The conditional velocity field driving this transition is:
$$
\begin{equation}
u_t(x^i | x_0^i, x_1^i)_v = \begin{cases}
\dfrac{1}{1 - t} & \text{if } v = x_1^i \text{ and } x^i = x_0^i, \\[0.3cm]
-\dfrac{1}{1 - t} & \text{if } v = x^i = x_0^i, \\[0.3cm]
0 & \text{otherwise}.
\end{cases}
\end{equation}
$$

**Uniform Source**

When $p_0$ is the uniform distribution, we can only condition on the target $z^i = x_1^i$. At time $t$, the token is the target $x_1^i$ with probability $t + (1 - t) \frac{1}{|\mathcal{V}|}$, or is drawn uniformly from other tokens in the vocabulary $\mathcal{V}$ with probability $(1 - t) \frac{1}{|\mathcal{V}|}$:
$$
\begin{equation}
q_t(x^i | x_1^i) = t \mathbb{I}(x^i = x_1^i) + (1 - t) \frac{1}{|\mathcal{V}|}
\end{equation}
$$

The conditional velocity field driving this transition is:
$$
\begin{equation}
u_t(x^i | x_1^i)_v = \begin{cases}
\dfrac{1}{1-t} & \text{if } v = x_1^i \text{ and } x^i \neq x_1^i \\[0.3cm]
-\dfrac{1}{1-t} & \text{if } v = x^i \neq x_1^i \\[0.3cm]
0 & \text{otherwise}
\end{cases}
\end{equation}
$$

**Useful note:** For all three cases detailed above, the conditional velocity can actually be unified and written in a more compact form:
$$
\begin{equation}
u_t(x^i | z^i) = \frac{1}{1-t} \left( \mathbf{e}_{z^i} - \mathbf{e}_{x^i} \right)
\end{equation}
$$

#### From Conditional to Unconditional Velocity

Now that we have defined our conditional probability paths and velocities, we need to connect them back to the global, unconditional process.

The overall marginal distribution $p_t$ is obtained simply by marginalizing over all possible conditional paths:
$$
\begin{equation}
p_t(\mathbf{x}) = \sum_{\mathbf{z}} p_t(\mathbf{x} | \mathbf{z}) \pi(\mathbf{z})
\end{equation}
$$
where $\pi(\mathbf{z})$ is the distribution over the conditioning variable $\mathbf{z}$ used during training.

Just as the marginal probability is an average of conditional probabilities, the marginal velocity field $u_t(\mathbf{x})_{\mathbf{y}}$ can be expressed as the expectation of the conditional velocity over the conditioning variable $\mathbf{z}$:
$$
\begin{equation}
u_t(\mathbf{x})_{\mathbf{y}} = \mathbb{E}_{Z | X_t = \mathbf{x}}[u_t(\mathbf{x} | Z)_{\mathbf{y}}] = \sum_{\mathbf{z}} u_t(\mathbf{x} | \mathbf{z})_{\mathbf{y}} \mathbb{P}(Z = \mathbf{z} | X_t = \mathbf{x})
\end{equation}
$$

**Learning with Conditional Velocity Fields**

To train our model, we use the KL divergence to measure the difference between velocity fields. This choice is natural: it arises from the KL divergence between the probability paths generated by the true and learned velocity fields. The ideal discrete flow matching loss minimizes the KL between the true marginal velocity $u_t$ and our learned velocity $u_{\theta,t}$:

$$
\begin{equation}
L_{\text{DFM}}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{\substack{t \sim \mathcal{U}(0,1) \\ \mathbf{x} \sim p_t}}\left[ D_{\text{KL}}\left(u_t(\mathbf{x}) \,\|\, u_{\theta,t}(\mathbf{x})\right) \right]
\end{equation}
$$

However, $u_t(\mathbf{x})$ requires marginalizing over the entire dataset, making it computationally intractable. Following the approach of continuous flow matching, we instead use the **conditional discrete flow matching loss**:

$$
\begin{equation}
L_{\text{CDFM}}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{\substack{t \sim \mathcal{U}(0,1) \\ \mathbf{z} \sim \pi \\ \mathbf{x} \sim p_t( \cdot | \mathbf{z})}}\left[ D_{\text{KL}}\left(u_t(\mathbf{x} | \mathbf{z}) \,\|\, u_{\theta,t}(\mathbf{x})\right) \right]
\end{equation}
$$

**Theorem:** Regressing the neural network $u_\theta$ against the known conditional velocity field $u_t(\mathbf{x} | \mathbf{z})$ using the loss $L_{\text{CDFM}}(\theta)$ is mathematically equivalent to directly regressing $u_\theta$ against the intractable, unknown marginal velocity field $u_t(\mathbf{x})$ using the loss $L_{\text{DFM}}(\theta)$.

In practice, thanks to our factorization assumption, the loss decomposes as a sum over individual token positions:

$$
\begin{equation}
L_{\text{CDFM}}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{\substack{t \sim \mathcal{U}(0,1) \\ \mathbf{z} \sim \pi \\ \mathbf{x} \sim p_t( \cdot | \mathbf{z})}}\left[ \sum_{i=1}^S D_{\text{KL}}\left( u_t(x^i | z^i) \,\|\, u_{\theta,t}^i(\mathbf{x}) \right) \right]
\end{equation}
$$

#### Denoiser

In discrete spaces, directly predicting a continuous vector field or instantaneous transition rates can feel unintuitive. Instead, a much more natural object to learn is a **denoiser**.

A denoiser simply observes the current noisy, corrupted, or partially masked sequence $\mathbf{x}_t$ at time $t$ and attempts to predict the true, clean target token $X_1^i$ for each position $i$. We denote this learned neural network distribution as $P_{\theta,t}(X_1^i | \mathbf{x})$.

We can directly connect the denoiser and velocity through this simple formula:

$$
\begin{equation}
u_t^i(\mathbf{x})_v = \frac{1}{1 - t} \left( \mathbb{P}(X_1^i = v | X_t = \mathbf{x}) - \mathbb{I}(v = x^i) \right)
\end{equation}
$$

Because the term $\mathbb{I}(v = x^i)$ is a known constant given the current state, learning the velocity field $u$ is mathematically exactly equivalent to learning the denoiser probability $\mathbb{P}(X_1^i = v | X_t = \mathbf{x})$.

Consequently, we can train the denoiser directly. Using the KL divergence between the true clean token distribution and the learned one, the loss simplifies to the standard cross-entropy:

$$
\begin{equation}
L_{\text{CDM}}(\theta) = -\mathbb{E}_{\substack{t \sim \mathcal{U}(0,1) \\ \mathbf{x}_1 \sim p_{\text{data}} \\ \mathbf{x} \sim p_t( \cdot | \mathbf{x}_1)}} \left[ \sum_{i=1}^S \log P_{\theta,t}(X_1^i = x_1^i | \mathbf{x}) \right]
\end{equation}
$$

In the specific context of masked diffusion, we usually restrict the learning process to predict only the tokens that are currently masked. Consequently, the loss is evaluated exclusively on these masked positions and scaled by the appropriate transition rate, becoming:
$$
\begin{equation}

##### Forward and Reverse Diffusion Processes

We first consider a sentence where we progressively replace each word with a special mask token, transforming the sequence $\mathbf{x}$ into a sequence of masks over time. This forward process is applied independently to each token. Thus, for $s < t$, we can compute the transition probability from time $s$ to $t$ and define the conditional transition matrix $\bar{Q}_{t|s}$ satisfying $q(x_t | x_s) = \text{Cat}(x_t; \ \bar{Q}_{t|s}^\top x_s)$. In matrix form, this is explicitly:
$$
\begin{equation}
\bar{Q}_{t|s} =
\begin{pmatrix}
\frac{\alpha_t}{\alpha_s} & 0 & \dots & 0 & 1 - \frac{\alpha_t}{\alpha_s} \\
0 & \frac{\alpha_t}{\alpha_s} & \dots & 0 & 1 - \frac{\alpha_t}{\alpha_s} \\
\vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots & \vdots \\
0 & 0 & \dots & \frac{\alpha_t}{\alpha_s} & 1 - \frac{\alpha_t}{\alpha_s} \\
0 & 0 & \dots & 0 & 1
\end{pmatrix}
= \frac{\alpha_t}{\alpha_s} I + \left(1 - \frac{\alpha_t}{\alpha_s}\right)\mathbf{1}\mathbf{e}_m^\top
\end{equation}
$$
where $\alpha_t$ is the probability of a token remaining unmasked at time $t$.
Interpretation: if the token at time $s$ is $x_s$, the probability it transitions to $x_t$ is $\frac{\alpha_t}{\alpha_s}$ if it remains unchanged, and $1 - \frac{\alpha_t}{\alpha_s}$ if it becomes the mask token $m$.

Let $j \in \{1, 2, \dots, T\}$ be an integer, and let $t \in \{0, \frac{1}{T}, \frac{2}{T}, \dots, 1\}$ denote the discrete time steps of the diffusion process.

We can define the single-step transition matrix $Q_{t}$ (where $t=j/T$) acting on the state space. Since the transition logic is identical for all non-mask tokens, the matrix has a specific structure. Let $\beta_j \in [0, 1]$ be the probability of transitioning to the mask token at time step $j/T$. The transition $x_{(j-1)/T} \to x_{j/T}$ is defined by:
$$
\begin{align}
x_{j/T} &= \begin{cases}
x_{(j-1)/T} & \text{with probability } 1 - \beta_j \\
m & \text{with probability } \beta_j
\end{cases}
\end{align}
$$

We can represent this transition distribution using a Categorical distribution parameterized by a transition matrix $Q_j \in \mathbb{R}^{K \times K}$:
$$
\begin{equation}
q(x_{j/T} | x_{(j-1)/T}) = \text{Cat}(x_{j/T}; \ Q_j^\top x_{(j-1)/T})
\end{equation}
$$
where $x$ vectors are treated as column vectors. The matrix $Q_j$ is given by:
$$
\begin{equation}
Q_j =
\begin{pmatrix}
1-\beta_j & 0 & \dots & 0 & \beta_j \\
0 & 1-\beta_j & \dots & 0 & \beta_j \\
\vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots & \vdots \\
0 & 0 & \dots & 1-\beta_j & \beta_j \\
0 & 0 & \dots & 0 & 1
\end{pmatrix} \in \mathbb{R}^{K \times K}
\end{equation}
$$
Here, the last row corresponds to the mask token $m$, which is an absorbing state (it stays $m$ with probability 1). The first $|\mathcal{V}|$ rows correspond to valid tokens.

We consider the continuous-time limit of this process, where the time steps become infinitesimally small and the transition probabilities are defined by a rate function $\beta(t)$, in order to avoid having a model that only learns to noise and denoise at specific discrete time steps.

The probability that a token remains equal to its initial value $x_0$ at time $t$ can be computed as:
$$
\begin{align}
\mathbb{P}(x_t = x_0) &= \mathbb{P}\left(x_t = x_0 | x_{t-\frac{1}{T}} = x_0\right) \dots \mathbb{P}\left(x_{\frac{1}{T}} = x_0 | x_0 = x_0\right) \\
&= \prod_{i=1}^{tT} (1 - \beta_i) \\
&= \exp\left(\sum_{i=1}^{tT} \log(1 - \beta_i)\right) \\
&\approx \exp\left(-\sum_{i=1}^{tT} \beta_i\right) \quad \text{(for small $\beta_i$)} \\
&\xrightarrow[T \to \infty]{} \exp\left(-\int_0^t \beta(s) \, ds\right)
\end{align}
$$
We note $\alpha_t = \exp\left(-\int_0^t \beta(s) \, ds\right)$ as the probability of a token remaining unmasked at time $t$.

We can define the cumulative transition matrix (or forward marginal) $\bar{Q}_t$ such that:
$$
\begin{equation}
q(x_t | x_0) = \text{Cat}(x_t; \ \bar{Q}_t^\top x_0)
\end{equation}
$$
Since the mask state is absorbing, this matrix essentially summarizes the cumulative probability of not being masked yet. We can write it compactly using the product of the all-ones vector $\mathbf{1}$ and the one-hot mask vector $\mathbf{e}_m$:
$$
\begin{equation}
\bar{Q}_t = \alpha_t I + (1-\alpha_t)\mathbf{1}\mathbf{e}_m^\top \in \mathbb{R}^{K \times K}
\end{equation}
$$
where $I$ is the identity matrix and $\mathbf{1}\mathbf{e}_m^\top$ is a matrix with ones in the $m$-th column and zeros elsewhere.

Now, we want to calculate the transition probability from an intermediate time $s$ to a later time $t$ (where $s < t$). We rely on the Markov property of the diffusion process. This property dictates that the forward marginal transition matrix $\bar{Q}_t$ can be decomposed as the product of the transitions from $0$ to $s$ and from $s$ to $t$:
$$
\begin{equation}
\bar{Q}_t = \bar{Q}_s \bar{Q}_{t|s}
\end{equation}
$$

We want to find the conditional transition matrix $\bar{Q}_{t|s}$ that satisfies this equation. We hypothesize that $\bar{Q}_{t|s}$ has the same structural form as the marginals, parameterized by some survival probability $\gamma \in [0, 1]$:
$$
\begin{equation}
\bar{Q}_{t|s} = \gamma I + (1 - \gamma)\mathbf{1}\mathbf{e}_m^\top
\end{equation}
$$

Substituting the known forms of $\bar{Q}_t$ and $\bar{Q}_s$ into the Markov equation gives:
$$
\begin{equation}
\alpha_t I + (1-\alpha_t)\mathbf{1}\mathbf{e}_m^\top = \left(\alpha_s I + (1-\alpha_s)\mathbf{1}\mathbf{e}_m^\top\right) \left(\gamma I + (1-\gamma)\mathbf{1}\mathbf{e}_m^\top\right)
\end{equation}
$$

Expanding the right side, and using the fact that $\mathbf{e}_m^\top \mathbf{1} = 1$ (since the dot product of a one-hot vector and an all-ones vector is the scalar 1), we obtain:
$$
\begin{aligned}
\bar{Q}_s \bar{Q}_{t|s} &= \alpha_s \gamma I + \alpha_s (1-\gamma)\mathbf{1}\mathbf{e}_m^\top + \gamma (1-\alpha_s)\mathbf{1}\mathbf{e}_m^\top + (1-\alpha_s)(1-\gamma)\mathbf{1}(\mathbf{e}_m^\top \mathbf{1})\mathbf{e}_m^\top
\end{aligned}
\begin{equation} \end{equation}
$$
$$
\begin{align}
&= \alpha_s \gamma I + \left[ \alpha_s(1-\gamma) + \gamma(1-\alpha_s) + (1-\alpha_s)(1-\gamma) \right]\mathbf{1}\mathbf{e}_m^\top \\
&= \alpha_s \gamma I + (1 - \alpha_s \gamma)\mathbf{1}\mathbf{e}_m^\top
\end{align}
$$

By matching the coefficients with our target $\bar{Q}_t = \alpha_t I + (1-\alpha_t)\mathbf{1}\mathbf{e}_m^\top$, we find the required value for $\gamma$:
$$
\begin{equation}
\alpha_s \gamma = \alpha_t \implies \gamma = \frac{\alpha_t}{\alpha_s}
\end{equation}
$$

This derivation mathematically proves that the probability of a token surviving unmasked from time $s$ to $t$, given that it was unmasked at time $s$, is exactly $\frac{\alpha_t}{\alpha_s}$. Plugging $\gamma$ back into our matrix formulation yields:
$$
\begin{equation}
\bar{Q}_{t|s} = \frac{\alpha_t}{\alpha_s} I + \left(1 - \frac{\alpha_t}{\alpha_s}\right)\mathbf{1}\mathbf{e}_m^\top
\end{equation}
$$

Explicitly, the matrix is written as:
$$
\begin{equation}
\bar{Q}_{t|s} =
\begin{pmatrix}
\frac{\alpha_t}{\alpha_s} & 0 & \dots & 0 & 1 - \frac{\alpha_t}{\alpha_s} \\
0 & \frac{\alpha_t}{\alpha_s} & \dots & 0 & 1 - \frac{\alpha_t}{\alpha_s} \\
\vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots & \vdots \\
0 & 0 & \dots & \frac{\alpha_t}{\alpha_s} & 1 - \frac{\alpha_t}{\alpha_s} \\
0 & 0 & \dots & 0 & 1
\end{pmatrix}
\end{equation}
$$

where $\sigma(t) = -\frac{\dot{\alpha}_t}{\alpha_t} > 0$ represents the instantaneous masking rate.

To generate data, we define a reverse process that progressively unmasks tokens. The probability of reverting to the clean token $x_0$ yields a reverse matrix $R_{s|t}^{x_0}$:
$$
\begin{equation}
R_{s|t}^{x_0} =
\begin{pmatrix}

\end{pmatrix}

\end{equation}

Interpretation: if the token at time $t$ is $m$, the probability it transitions to $x_0$ at time s is $\frac{\alpha_s - \alpha_t}{1 - \alpha_t}$.

Using Bayes' theorem:

##### Equivalence with Discrete Flow Matching

While discrete diffusion and discrete flow matching share similar mathematical foundations, their primary difference lies in the time convention. In discrete diffusion, $t = 0$ corresponds to clean data, and $t = 1$ corresponds to the fully masked state. Discrete flow matching uses the exact opposite convention.

We can perfectly translate between the two frameworks using a simple time reversal: $t_{\text{FM}} = 1 - t_{\text{DD}}$. Under this time change, the velocity field in flow matching directly corresponds to the entries of the reverse rate matrix in discrete diffusion:
$$
\begin{equation}
u_{1-t}^{\text{FM}} = \dot{R}_t
\end{equation}
$$

##### Training

For a discrete-time masked diffusion process, we define a path-wise generative model by approximately reversing the forward transitions step by step along the entire noising trajectory. We want to align the forward and reverse trajectories so that $\log p_\theta(x_0) \geq \text{ELBO}(x_0)$, where:
$$
\begin{equation}
\text{ELBO}(x_0) = \mathbb{E}_{\substack{x_{1:T} \sim q( \cdot | x_0)}} \left[ \log p_\theta(x_{0:T}) - \log q(x_{1:T}|x_0) \right]
\end{equation}
$$
Intuitively, this ELBO inequality means we can lower-bound the log-likelihood of the data $x_0$ under our model by measuring how well the model's reverse process $p_\theta(x_{0:T})$ agrees with the forward noising process $q(x_{1:T}|x_0)$. The closer the two are, the tighter the bound.

The ELBO for a general discrete diffusion process is given by:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\text{ELBO}(x_0) = - D_{KL}(q( \cdot | x_0) \parallel p_{\text{source}}) - \int_0^1 \mathbb{E}_{x_t \sim q( \cdot | x_0)} \left[ D_{KL}(\dot{R}_t(x_t | x_0) \parallel \dot{R}_{\theta,t}(x_t)) \right] dt
\end{aligned}
\begin{equation}\end{equation}
$$

<summary>Training Loss Proof</summary>
<div>

We start with the known inequality for variational inference:
$$
\begin{equation}
    \log p_\theta(x_0) \geq \text{ELBO}(x_0),
\end{equation}
$$
where the evidence lower bound is defined as:
$$
\begin{equation}
    \text{ELBO}(x_0) = \mathbb{E}_{\substack{x_{1:T} \sim q( \cdot | x_0)}} \left[ \log p_\theta(x_{0:T}) - \log q(x_{1:T}|x_0) \right],
\end{equation}
$$
and $x_{j:T} = (x_{j/T}, x_{(j+1)/T}, \dots, x_1)$ denotes the sequence of states from time $j/T$ to $t=1$.

The forward process follows a Markovian structure:
$$
\begin{aligned}
    q(x_{1:T}|x_0) = \prod_{j=1}^{T} q(x_{j/T} | x_{(j-1)/T}, x_{(j-2)/T}, \dots, x_0) = \prod_{j=1}^{T} q(x_{j/T} | x_{(j-1)/T}, x_0)
\end{aligned}
\begin{equation} \end{equation}
$$
where the last equality holds because the forward process is memoryless given the previous states.

Similarly, the reverse model decomposes as:
$$
\begin{equation}
    p_\theta(x_{0:T}) = p_\theta(x_1) \prod_{j=1}^{T} p_\theta(x_{(j-1)/T} | x_{j/T}).
\end{equation}
$$

Substituting the Markovian decompositions into the ELBO:
$$
\begin{aligned}
    \text{ELBO}(x_0) &= \mathbb{E}_{\substack{x_{1:T} \sim q( \cdot | x_0)}} \left[ \log p_\theta(x_1) \right] \\
    &\quad + \sum_{j=1}^{T} \mathbb{E}_{\substack{x_{1:T} \sim q( \cdot | x_0)}} \left[ \log p_\theta(x_{(j-1)/T} | x_{j/T}) - \log q(x_{j/T} | x_{(j-1)/T}, x_0) \right]
\end{aligned}
\begin{equation} \end{equation}
$$

Using Bayes' theorem, we can rewrite the forward process conditional:
$$
\begin{aligned}
    \log q(x_{j/T} | x_{(j-1)/T}, x_0) = \log q(x_{(j-1)/T} | x_{j/T}, x_0) + \log q(x_{j/T} | x_0) - \log q(x_{(j-1)/T} | x_0).
\end{aligned}
\begin{equation} \end{equation}
$$

Substituting Bayes' rule into the ELBO and observing that consecutive terms telescope:
$$
\begin{aligned}
    \text{ELBO}(x_0) &= \mathbb{E}_{\substack{x_{1:T} \sim q( \cdot | x_0)}} \left[ \log q(x_1 | x_0) - \log q(x_0 | x_0) \right] \\
    &\quad + \mathbb{E}_{\substack{x_{1:T} \sim q( \cdot | x_0)}} \left[ \log p_\theta(x_1) \right] + \sum_{j=1}^{T} \mathbb{E}_{\substack{x_{1:T} \sim q( \cdot | x_0)}} \left[ \log p_\theta(x_{(j-1)/T} | x_{j/T}) - \log q(x_{(j-1)/T} | x_{j/T}, x_0) \right]
\end{aligned}
\begin{equation} \end{equation}
$$

Note that $\log q(x_0 | x_0) = \log(1) = 0$, so:
$$
\begin{aligned}
    \text{ELBO}(x_0) &= - \mathbb{E}_{\substack{x_{1:T} \sim q( \cdot | x_0)}} \left[ \log q(x_1 | x_0) - \log p_\theta(x_1) \right] \\
    &\quad - \sum_{j=1}^{T} \mathbb{E}_{\substack{x_{1:T} \sim q( \cdot | x_0)}} \left[ \log q(x_{(j-1)/T} | x_{j/T}, x_0) - \log p_\theta(x_{(j-1)/T} | x_{j/T}) \right]
\end{aligned}
\begin{equation} \end{equation}
$$

We can simplify the expectations over the joint distribution $q(x_{1:T}|x_0)$ by marginalizing:

For the first term:
$$
\begin{aligned}
    \mathbb{E}_{\substack{x_{1:T} \sim q( \cdot | x_0)}} \left[ \log q(x_1 | x_0) - \log p_\theta(x_1) \right] = \mathbb{E}_{\substack{x_1 \sim q( \cdot | x_0)}} \left[ \log q(x_1 | x_0) - \log p_\theta(x_1) \right]
\end{aligned}
\begin{equation} \end{equation}
$$

For the sum, we marginalize over all intermediate variables:
$$
\begin{aligned}
    &\mathbb{E}_{\substack{x_{1:T} \sim q( \cdot | x_0)}} \left[ \log q(x_{(j-1)/T} | x_{j/T}, x_0) - \log p_\theta(x_{(j-1)/T} | x_{j/T}) \right] \\
    &= \mathbb{E}_{\substack{x_{j/T} \sim q( \cdot | x_0)}} \left[ \mathbb{E}_{\substack{x_{(j-1)/T} \sim q( \cdot | x_{j/T}, x_0)}} \left[ \log q(x_{(j-1)/T} | x_{j/T}, x_0) - \log p_\theta(x_{(j-1)/T} | x_{j/T}) \right] \right]
\end{aligned}
\begin{equation} \end{equation}
$$

Therefore, the ELBO becomes:
$$
\begin{aligned}
    \text{ELBO}(x_0) &= - D_{KL}\left(q(x_1|x_0) \parallel p_\theta(x_1)\right) \\
    &\quad - \sum_{j=1}^{T} \mathbb{E}_{\substack{x_{j/T} \sim q( \cdot | x_0)}} \left[ D_{KL}\left(q(x_{(j-1)/T} | x_{j/T}, x_0) \parallel p_\theta(x_{(j-1)/T} | x_{j/T})\right) \right]
\end{aligned}
\begin{equation} \end{equation}
$$

**Conclusion**

For a general discrete diffusion process acting on tokens, we start from the full ELBO derived earlier:
$$
\begin{align}
    \text{ELBO}(x_0) &= - D_{KL}\left(q(x_1 | x_0) \parallel p_\theta(x_1)\right) \\
    &\quad - \sum_{j=1}^{T} \mathbb{E}_{\substack{x_{j/T} \sim q( \cdot | x_0)}} \left[ D_{KL}\left(q(x_{(j-1)/T} | x_{j/T}, x_0) \parallel p_\theta(x_{(j-1)/T} | x_{j/T})\right) \right]
\end{align}
$$

Let $\Delta t = 1/T$, $t = j/T$, and $s = (j-1)/T$. We denote the token transition rate vectors of the denoiser forward process and the reverse model as $\dot{R}_t(x_t | x_0)$ and $\dot{R}_{\theta,t}(x_t)$ respectively. For a small time step $\Delta t$, the probability of transitioning to a token $y \in \mathcal{V} \cup \{m\}$ is approximated by:
$$
\begin{align}
    q(y | x_t, x_0) &\approx \mathbb{I}(y = x_t) + \Delta t [\dot{R}_t(x_t | x_0)]_y \\
    p_\theta(y | x_t) &\approx \mathbb{I}(y = x_t) + \Delta t [\dot{R}_{\theta,t}(x_t)]_y
\end{align}
$$
where $[\dot{R}(x)]_y \geq 0$ for $y \neq x$, and the diagonal entries ensure normalization: $[\dot{R}(x)]_x = - \sum_{y \neq x} [\dot{R}(x)]_y$.

We expand the KL divergence term inside the sum by summing over the vocabulary:
$$
\begin{aligned}
D_{KL}\left(q( \cdot | x_t, x_0) \parallel p_\theta( \cdot | x_t)\right) = \sum_{y \in \mathcal{V} \cup \{m\}} q(y | x_t, x_0) \log \frac{q(y | x_t, x_0)}{p_\theta(y | x_t)}
\end{aligned}
\begin{equation} \end{equation}
$$

Separating the sum into the case where the token changes ($y \neq x_t$) and stays the same ($y = x_t$):

1. For $y \neq x_t$:
$$
\begin{aligned}
    \Delta t [\dot{R}_t(x_t | x_0)]_y \log \frac{\Delta t [\dot{R}_t(x_t | x_0)]_y}{\Delta t [\dot{R}_{\theta,t}(x_t)]_y} = \Delta t [\dot{R}_t(x_t | x_0)]_y \log \frac{[\dot{R}_t(x_t | x_0)]_y}{[\dot{R}_{\theta,t}(x_t)]_y}
\end{aligned}
\begin{equation} \end{equation}
$$

2. For $y = x_t$:
Using the approximation $\log \frac{1+a}{1+b} \approx a-b$:
$$
\begin{aligned}
    (1 + \Delta t [\dot{R}_t(x_t | x_0)]_{x_t}) \log \frac{1 + \Delta t [\dot{R}_t(x_t | x_0)]_{x_t}}{1 + \Delta t [\dot{R}_{\theta,t}(x_t)]_{x_t}}
    &\approx 1 \cdot (\Delta t [\dot{R}_t(x_t | x_0)]_{x_t} - \Delta t [\dot{R}_{\theta,t}(x_t)]_{x_t}) \\
    &= \Delta t \left( - \sum_{y \neq x_t} [\dot{R}_t(x_t | x_0)]_y + \sum_{y \neq x_t} [\dot{R}_{\theta,t}(x_t)]_y \right)
\end{aligned}
\begin{equation} \end{equation}
$$

Combining these terms, the KL divergence becomes:
$$
\begin{aligned}
    D_{KL} \approx \Delta t \sum_{y \neq x_t} \left( [\dot{R}_t(x_t | x_0)]_y \log \frac{[\dot{R}_t(x_t | x_0)]_y}{[\dot{R}_{\theta,t}(x_t)]_y} - [\dot{R}_t(x_t | x_0)]_y + [\dot{R}_{\theta,t}(x_t)]_y \right)
\end{aligned}
\begin{equation} \end{equation}
$$
This expression corresponds to $\Delta t \cdot D_{KL}(\dot{R}_t(x_t | x_0) \parallel \dot{R}_{\theta,t}(x_t))$, the generalized KL divergence between the instantaneous transition rate vectors for the token $x_t$.

Substituting this back into the ELBO, the sum over $j$ becomes a Riemann sum. Taking the limit as $T \to \infty$:
$$
\begin{aligned}
    \text{ELBO}(x_0) = - D_{KL}\left(q(x_1 | x_0) \parallel p_\theta(x_1)\right) - \int_0^1 \mathbb{E}_{\substack{x_t \sim q( \cdot | x_0)}} \left[ D_{KL}\left(\dot{R}_t(x_t | x_0) \parallel \dot{R}_{\theta,t}(x_t)\right) \right] dt
\end{aligned}
\begin{equation} \end{equation}
$$
Note that $p_\theta(x_1)$ corresponds to the known base distribution $p_{\text{source}}$ (typically the fully masked state or uniform distribution).

##### Concrete Score

In continuous diffusion, the score function $\nabla \log p_t(x)$ dictates the deterministic reverse ODE:
$$
\begin{equation}
dx = \left[h(x, t) - \tfrac{1}{2}g(t)^2 \,\nabla \log p_t(x)\right]dt
\end{equation}
$$
which has the same marginal densities as the time-reversed stochastic process. In a discrete state space, there is no notion of a continuous gradient. However, one can write the sampling equation for the reverse process using Bayes' theorem:
$$
\begin{align}
    p_\theta(x_s = y | x_t = x) &= q(x_t = x | x_s = y)  \frac{p_s(y)}{p_t(x)} \\
    &\approx q(x_t = x | x_s = y)  s_t(x)_y
\end{align}
$$
where the approximation holds under the hypothesis that $s \to t$, meaning $p_s(y) \approx p_t(y)$.

This naturally introduces the definition of the concrete score:
$$
\begin{equation}
s_t(x)_y = \frac{p_t(y)}{p_t(x)}
\end{equation}
$$
where $p_t(v) = \mathbb{P}(x_t = v)$.

Specifically, the concrete score relates the true forward rate matrix to the reverse rate matrix:
$$
\begin{equation}
s_t(x)_y = \frac{[\dot{R}_t(x)]_y}{[\dot{Q}_t(x)]_y}
\end{equation}
$$
This relationship directly unlocks our sampling mechanism used in practice with a learned concrete score:
$$
\begin{equation}
\mathbb{P}\!\left(X_{t-h} = y | X_t = x\right) = \mathbb{I}(x=y) + h\,[\dot{Q}_t(x)]_y\,s_{\theta,t}(x)_y + o(h)
\end{equation}
$$

Then, we can express the KL loss between the rates in terms of concrete score:
$$
\begin{aligned}
L_t(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{\substack{x_0 \sim p_0 \\ x_t \sim q( \cdot |x_0)}} \left[ \sum_{y \neq x_t} [\dot{Q}_t(x_t)]_y \left( s_{\theta,t}(x_t)_y - \frac{p_t(y|x_0)}{p_t(x_t|x_0)} \log s_{\theta,t}(x_t)_y \right) \right] + C
\end{aligned}
\begin{equation} \end{equation}
$$

which is exactly the statement in the proposition above.

##### Complement: Equivalence Between Masked Diffusion Models and Any-Order Autoregressive Models

An any-order autoregressive model (AO-ARM) is an autoregressive model trained to factorize the joint distribution under *every* possible token ordering $\pi$, not just the left-to-right one. Its training objective minimizes the expected negative log-likelihood over the uniform distribution $\mathcal{U}_\pi$ of all $S!$ orderings:
$$
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{\text{AO-ARM}}(\theta) = -\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}_0 \sim p_{\text{data}},\, \pi \sim \mathcal{U}_\pi} \left[ \sum_{i=1}^{S} \log p_\theta(x_0^{\pi(i)} | x_0^{\pi(<i)}; \pi) \right]
\end{equation}
$$
where $x_0^{\pi(<i)} = (x_0^{\pi(1)}, \ldots, x_0^{\pi(i-1)})$ denotes the tokens revealed before position $\pi(i)$ in the ordering $\pi$. In practice, AO-ARMs use a shared neural network with mask tokens to represent absent variables: given an ordering $\pi$ and a step $i$, the positions $\pi(i), \pi(i+1), \ldots, \pi(S)$ are replaced by the mask token $m$, and the network predicts the token at position $\pi(i)$ from this partially masked input.

The following proposition shows that the MDM training objective is equivalent to the AO-ARM objective.

**Proposition (MDM and AO-ARM Equivalence):** Let $\mathcal{L}_{\text{MDM}}(\theta)$ denote the masked diffusion training loss with linear schedule $\alpha_t = 1 - t$ and uniform time sampling $t \sim \mathcal{U}(0,1)$:
$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathcal{L}_{\text{MDM}}(\theta) = -\mathbb{E}_{t \sim \mathcal{U}(0,1),\, \mathbf{x}_0 \sim p_{\text{data}},\, \mathbf{x}_t \sim q(\cdot | \mathbf{x}_0)} \left[ \frac{1}{t} \sum_{i=1}^{S} \mathbb{I}(x_t^i = m) \log p_\theta(x_0^i | \mathbf{x}_t) \right]
\end{aligned}
\begin{equation} \end{equation}
$$
Then $\mathcal{L}_{\text{MDM}}(\theta) = \mathcal{L}_{\text{AO-ARM}}(\theta)$.

This equivalence has a practical consequence: since the MDM is implicitly trained on all possible token orderings, it does not privilege left-to-right dependencies.

<details>
<summary>Proof</summary>
<div>
**Proof:** We proceed in two steps. First, we rewrite the MDM loss as a sum over the number of masked tokens using a change of variable. Second, we rewrite the AO-ARM loss in the same form.

**Step 1: Rewriting the MDM loss.**

Under the linear schedule $\alpha_t = 1 - t$, the forward process masks each token independently with probability $t$. The MDM loss can be written as:
$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathcal{L}_{\text{MDM}}(\theta) = -\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}_0} \left[ \int_0^1 \frac{1}{t} \mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}_t \sim q(\cdot | \mathbf{x}_0)} \left[ \sum_{i=1}^{S} \mathbb{I}(x_t^i = m) \log p_\theta(x_0^i | \mathbf{x}_t) \right] dt \right]
\end{aligned}
\begin{equation} \end{equation}
$$
We now condition on the number of masked tokens. The forward process $q(\cdot | \mathbf{x}_0)$ masks each token independently: $x_t^i = m$ with probability $t$ and $x_t^i = x_0^i$ with probability $1-t$. Equivalently, drawing $\mathbf{x}_t \sim q(\cdot | \mathbf{x}_0)$ amounts to drawing $\xi^i \sim \text{Bernoulli}(t)$ independently for $i = 1, \ldots, S$ and setting:
$$
\begin{equation}
x_t^i = \begin{cases} m & \text{if } \xi^i = 1 \\ x_0^i & \text{if } \xi^i = 0 \end{cases}
\end{equation}
$$
Let $\mathcal{M} = \{i : \xi^i = 1\}$ denote the set of masked positions. For fixed $\mathbf{x}_0$, $\mathbf{x}_t$ and $\mathcal{M}$ are in bijection ($\mathcal{M} = \{i : x_t^i = m\}$), so we may write $\mathbf{x}_{\mathcal{M}} := \mathbf{x}_t$ and $p_\theta(x_0^i | \mathbf{x}_t) = p_\theta(x_0^i | \mathbf{x}_{\mathcal{M}})$. The inner sum becomes $\sum_{i \in \mathcal{M}} \log p_\theta(x_0^i | \mathbf{x}_{\mathcal{M}})$. For any function $g$:
$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}_t \sim q(\cdot | \mathbf{x}_0)}[g(\mathbf{x}_t)] = \mathbb{E}_{(\xi^1, \ldots, \xi^S) \overset{\text{i.i.d.}}{\sim} \text{Bernoulli}(t)}[g(\mathbf{x}_t)] = \mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{M}}[g(\mathbf{x}_{\mathcal{M}})]
\end{aligned}
\begin{equation} \end{equation}
$$
Applying the law of total expectation by conditioning on $|\mathcal{M}|$:
$$
\begin{equation}
\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{M}}[g(\mathbf{x}_{\mathcal{M}})] = \sum_{k=0}^{S} \mathbb{P}(|\mathcal{M}| = k) \;\mathbb{E}[g(\mathbf{x}_{\mathcal{M}}) \mid |\mathcal{M}| = k]
\end{equation}
$$
It remains to identify the conditional distribution of $\mathcal{M}$ given $|\mathcal{M}| = k$. For any subset $A \subseteq [S]$ with $|A| = k$:
$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{M} = A \mid |\mathcal{M}| = k) = \frac{\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{M} = A)}{\mathbb{P}(|\mathcal{M}| = k)} = \frac{t^k(1-t)^{S-k}}{\binom{S}{k}\, t^k(1-t)^{S-k}} = \frac{1}{\binom{S}{k}}
\end{aligned}
\begin{equation} \end{equation}
$$
so $\mathcal{M} \mid |\mathcal{M}| = k$ is uniformly distributed over all size-$k$ subsets of $[S]$, i.e., $\mathbb{E}[\,\cdot \mid |\mathcal{M}| = k] = \mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{M} \sim \text{Unif}(\binom{[S]}{k})}[\,\cdot\,]$.
Define:
$$
\begin{equation}
f(k, \mathbf{x}_0) := \mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{M} \sim \text{Unif}(\binom{[S]}{k})} \left[ \sum_{i \in \mathcal{M}} \log p_\theta(x_0^i | \mathbf{x}_{\mathcal{M}}) \right]
\end{equation}
$$
Combining these results:
$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}_t \sim q(\cdot | \mathbf{x}_0)}\left[ \frac{1}{t} \sum_{i=1}^{S} \mathbb{I}(x_t^i = m) \log p_\theta(x_0^i | \mathbf{x}_t) \right] = \frac{1}{t} \sum_{k=0}^{S} \underbrace{\binom{S}{k} t^k (1-t)^{S-k}}_{\mathbb{P}(|\mathcal{M}| = k)} \; f(k, \mathbf{x}_0)
\end{aligned}
\begin{equation} \end{equation}
$$
The $k = 0$ term vanishes (no masked tokens), so substituting back into $\mathcal{L}_{\text{MDM}}$:
$$
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{\text{MDM}}(\theta) = -\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}_0} \left[ \int_0^1 \frac{1}{t} \sum_{k=1}^{S} \binom{S}{k} t^k (1-t)^{S-k}\, f(k, \mathbf{x}_0)\, dt \right]
\end{equation}
$$
Exchanging the sum and integral:
$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathcal{L}_{\text{MDM}}(\theta) = -\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}_0} \left[ \sum_{k=1}^{S} \binom{S}{k} \left( \int_0^1 t^{k-1} (1-t)^{S-k}\, dt \right) f(k, \mathbf{x}_0) \right]
\end{aligned}
\begin{equation} \end{equation}
$$
The integral is a Beta function:
$$
\begin{equation}
\int_0^1 t^{k-1}(1-t)^{S-k}\, dt = B(k, S-k+1) = \frac{(k-1)!(S-k)!}{S!}
\end{equation}
$$
Substituting and simplifying $\binom{S}{k} \cdot \frac{(k-1)!(S-k)!}{S!} = \frac{S!}{k!(S-k)!} \cdot \frac{(k-1)!(S-k)!}{S!} = \frac{1}{k}$:
$$
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{\text{MDM}}(\theta) = -\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}_0} \left[ \sum_{k=1}^{S} \frac{1}{k}\, f(k, \mathbf{x}_0) \right]
\end{equation}
$$

**Step 2: Rewriting the AO-ARM loss.**

Fix an ordering $\pi$ and a step $i \in \{1, \ldots, S\}$. The conditional $p_\theta(x_0^{\pi(i)} | x_0^{\pi(<i)}; \pi)$ asks the model to predict the token at position $\pi(i)$ given that positions $\{\pi(1), \ldots, \pi(i-1)\}$ are unmasked and all others are masked. Since the model uses mask tokens to represent absent variables, the prediction depends only on *which* positions are revealed, not on the ordering in which they were revealed. Defining $\mathcal{M}_i = \{\pi(i), \pi(i+1), \ldots, \pi(S)\}$ as the set of $k = S - i + 1$ masked positions at step $i$:
$$
\begin{equation}
p_\theta(x_0^{\pi(i)} | x_0^{\pi(<i)}; \pi) = p_\theta(x_0^{\pi(i)} | \mathbf{x}_{\mathcal{M}_i})
\end{equation}
$$
We now show that, for a uniformly random $\pi$ and fixed $k$, the pair $(\mathcal{M}_i,\, \pi(i))$ is uniformly distributed over all (size-$k$ subset, element of that subset). Fix any $A \subseteq [S]$ with $|A| = k$ and any $j \in A$. A permutation $\pi$ satisfies $\mathcal{M}_i = A$ and $\pi(i) = j$ if and only if: its first $S - k$ entries are a permutation of $[S] \setminus A$, its $i$-th entry is $j$, and its last $k - 1$ entries are a permutation of $A \setminus \{j\}$. The number of such permutations is $(S-k)!\,(k-1)!$, so:
$$
\begin{equation}
\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{M}_i = A,\; \pi(i) = j) = \frac{(S-k)!\,(k-1)!}{S!} = \frac{1}{\binom{S}{k} \cdot k}
\end{equation}
$$
Summing over $j \in A$ gives $\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{M}_i = A) = 1/\binom{S}{k}$, and conditionally $\mathbb{P}(\pi(i) = j \mid \mathcal{M}_i = A) = 1/k$. We can now replace the expectation over $\pi$ with expectations over $\mathcal{M}$ and $j$. For any function $h$:
$$
\begin{align}
\mathbb{E}_{\pi \sim \mathcal{U}_\pi}\left[h(\mathcal{M}_i, \pi(i))\right] &= \frac{1}{S!}\sum_{\pi} h(\mathcal{M}_i, \pi(i)) \\
&= \sum_{\substack{A \subseteq [S] \\ |A| = k}} \sum_{j \in A} \mathbb{P}(\mathcal{M}_i = A,\; \pi(i) = j)\; h(A, j) \\
&= \sum_{\substack{A \subseteq [S] \\ |A| = k}} \sum_{j \in A} \frac{1}{\binom{S}{k}} \cdot \frac{1}{k}\; h(A, j) \\
&= \mathbb{E}_{\substack{\mathcal{M} \sim \text{Unif}(\binom{[S]}{k}) \\ j \sim \text{Unif}(\mathcal{M})}} \left[h(\mathcal{M}, j)\right]
\end{align}
$$
Applying this with $h(\mathcal{M}, j) = \log p_\theta(x_0^j | \mathbf{x}_{\mathcal{M}})$ and summing over $k$:
$$
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{\text{AO-ARM}}(\theta) = -\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}_0} \left[ \sum_{k=1}^{S} \mathbb{E}_{\substack{\mathcal{M} \sim \text{Unif}(\binom{[S]}{k}) \\ j \sim \text{Unif}(\mathcal{M})}} \left[ \log p_\theta(x_0^j | \mathbf{x}_{\mathcal{M}}) \right] \right]
\end{equation}
$$
Since $j$ is uniform over $\mathcal{M}$, we have $\mathbb{E}_{j \sim \text{Unif}(\mathcal{M})}[\log p_\theta(x_0^j | \mathbf{x}_{\mathcal{M}})] = \frac{1}{k} \sum_{j \in \mathcal{M}} \log p_\theta(x_0^j | \mathbf{x}_{\mathcal{M}})$, giving:
$$
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{\text{AO-ARM}}(\theta) = -\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}_0} \left[ \sum_{k=1}^{S} \frac{1}{k}\, f(k, \mathbf{x}_0) \right]
\end{equation}
$$
with the same $f(k, \mathbf{x}_0)$ defined in Step 1.

#### Planner aware path learning

Vanilla sampling in masked discrete diffusion and flow matching is computationally expensive. It requires $(1/h) \times S$ sequential steps, where $h$ is the time step size and $S$ is the length of the sequence. At every single micro-step, we must compute and draw from probability vectors of size $|\mathcal{V}|$ for all tokens. For example, with a step size of $h = 0.01$ and a sequence length of $S = 3000$, we would need an impractical $300,000$ steps to generate a single sample.

To make generation tractable, the standard approach used in the industry for large masked diffusion models is **planner-guided sampling**. Instead of relying on infinitesimal continuous-time steps, we fix a manageable number of total generation steps, denoted as $N$. At each step, the algorithm aggressively decodes and fixes exactly $S/N$ tokens.

**Algorithm: Planner-Guided Sampling**

**Input:** Planner strategy, trained denoiser $D_\theta$, total number of steps $N$
1. **Initialize:** $\mathbf{x}_0 \leftarrow (m, \dots, m)$ (a sequence of $S$ mask tokens)
2. **For** $k = 0, 1, \dots, N - 1$:
    * **Plan:** Sample candidate tokens $\mathbf{z} \sim D_\theta(\mathbf{x}_k)$
    * **Select:** A "planner" evaluates these candidate predictions based on a specific criterion. It chooses a set of positions $\mathcal{I}$ where $|\mathcal{I}| = S/N$ to reveal.
    * **Denoise:** Create $\mathbf{x}_{k+1}$ by setting $x_{k+1}^i = z^i$ for all $i \in \mathcal{I}$, keeping the rest as masks
3. **Return** fully generated sequence $\mathbf{x}_N$

To make this concrete, let's look at how we actually choose these tokens and why that choice forces us to rethink how we train the model. A simple and widely used strategy is the soft greedy planner. Instead of unmasking tokens at random, it selects the positions where the denoiser is most confident in its predictions. To prevent the generation process from becoming rigidly deterministic, we relax this strict selection into a soft probability distribution using a softmax function with a temperature parameter $\tau$:
$$
\begin{equation}
G_\phi^\tau(\mathbf{z}, \mathbf{x}_t)_j \propto \exp\left(\frac{1}{\tau} \log D_\theta^j(\mathbf{x}_t)_{z^j}\right)
\end{equation}
$$
This formulation assigns higher selection probabilities to the tokens the model is sure about. As $\tau \to \infty$, this reduces to uniform random sampling. As $\tau \to 0$, it converges to a strict, hard selection of only the most confident token.

For a concrete example, suppose we have two masked positions where the denoiser predicts the target tokens with confidences of $0.8$ and $0.2$. Setting the temperature to $\tau = 1/2$, the planner mathematically scales these probabilities by a power of $1/\tau = 2$ and normalizes them:
$$
D_\theta(\mathbf{x}_t)_{\mathbf{z}} = \begin{pmatrix} 0.8 \\ 0.2 \end{pmatrix} \quad \implies \quad G_\phi^{1/2}(\mathbf{z}, \mathbf{x}_t) = \frac{1}{0.8^2 + 0.2^2} \begin{pmatrix} 0.8^2 \\ 0.2^2 \end{pmatrix} \approx \begin{pmatrix} 0.941 \\ 0.059 \end{pmatrix}
$$
The initial confidence gap is heavily amplified, strongly favoring to denoise next the more certain prediction while still preserving a small element of stochasticity.

However, introducing a planner-aware sampling process creates a mismatch between training and inference. During training, the standard objective masks tokens uniformly at random across all positions and orderings, so the denoiser learns to predict any token from any arbitrary subset of revealed context. At inference, however, the planner selectively reveals tokens along an optimized path, meaning the denoiser operates on structured, informative contexts it was never explicitly trained on. Paradoxically, this makes training strictly harder than inference: the model must learn to denoise under the worst-case random maskings, whereas the planner later guides it through the most favorable ones.

To fix this discrepancy, we need a loss function that makes the model aware of the planner during the training phase. By safely detaching gradients through the planner to maintain numerical stability, we can achieve this by simply reweighting our standard objective. We assign higher loss weights to the positions the planner is most likely to select. The resulting Planner-Aware Path Learning (PAPL) loss is the standard continuous masked diffusion cross-entropy, mathematically augmented with these planner-derived weights:
$$
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{\text{PAPL}}(\theta) = - \mathbb{E}_{t, \mathbf{x}_0, \mathbf{x}_t} \left[ \sum_{ix_t^i = m} \frac{1}{t}(1 + \alpha w^i) \log D_\theta^i(\mathbf{x}_t)_{x_0^i} \right]
\end{equation}
$$
Here, $w^i$ represents the normalized weight of position $i$ assigned by the planner, and $\alpha$ is a hyperparameter that controls the strength of this planner guidance.
