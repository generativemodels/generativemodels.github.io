- **$\mathcal{V}$** vocabulary set; $|\mathcal{V}|$ is the vocabulary size
- **$\mathcal{S} = \mathcal{V}^S$** space of all possible sequences of length $S$ over the vocabulary
- **$\mathbf{x} = x^1 \dots x^S$** sequence of $S$ tokens, where each token $x^i$ takes values in $\mathcal{V}$
- **$\mathbf{e}_v$** one-hot vector in $\mathbb{R}^{|\mathcal{V}|}$ for token $v \in \mathcal{V}$
- **$m$** mask token
- **$\mathbf{x}^{\neq i}$** sub-sequence of $\mathbf{x}$ at all positions except $i$
- **$\Delta_k$** for $k \in \mathbb{N}$, $\Delta_k=\{\pi\in\mathbb{R}_+^k:\sum_{\ell=1}^{k}\pi_\ell=1\}$ is the probability simplex
- **$\text{Cat}$** categorical distribution: $x \sim \text{Cat}(\mathbf{p})$ means $\mathbb{P}(x = v) = \mathbf{p}_v$, for a probability vector $\mathbf{p} \in \Delta_{|\mathcal{V}|}$
- *Convention: $t=0$: clean data; $t=1$: noisy data.*
- **$p_0$** clean data (target) distribution; the forward process corrupts it toward the mask distribution $\delta_{m\ldots m}$ at the final time
- **$p_t$** marginal distribution at time $t$; the probability path between the clean data distribution $p_0$ and the mask distribution
- **$\beta_t$** noise schedule: probability that a token is corrupted (masked) at step $t$
- **$\alpha_t = \prod_{i=1}^t (1 - \beta_i)$** cumulative schedule, equal to the probability that a token is still clean (has survived) at step $t$
- **$D_{\theta,t}(\mathbf{x}_t) \in \Delta_{|\mathcal{V}|}$** learned denoiser: predicts the distribution over the clean token $\mathbf{x}_0$ given the noisy sequence $\mathbf{x}_t$
- **$\dot{Q}_t$** forward rate matrix (generator of the continuous-time forward process)
- **$\dot{R}_{\theta,t}$** learned reverse rate matrix (generator of the continuous-time reverse process)

This blog post is a __pedagogical introduction__ to the adaptation of diffusion models for discrete data.

In this blog post, we deal with discrete data: the space on which the probability distributions are defined is $\mathcal{S} := \mathcal{V}^S$, namely the space of sequences of length $S \in \mathbb{N}$ over a given vocabulary $\mathcal{V}$.
A sequence $\mathbf{x}$ in $\mathcal{S}$ is denoted as $\mathbf{x} = x^1 \dots x^S$, the $x^i$'s being called *tokens*.
In the vocabulary, we add a special token: the mask token, denoted as $m$.
The reader interested to have a more complete introduction on discrete data modelling is referred to the [blog post on discrete flow matching](https://dl.heeere.com/dfm).

### Variational discrete diffusion

Continuous-space diffusion models learn to generate data, e.g. images, by reversing a forward process that gradually corrupts data into pure noise through the addition of Gaussian noise.
In discrete diffusion, an analogous approach generates sequences by reversing a masking process that progressively replaces tokens with a mask token.

#### Forward process

We first consider the forward process: it starts from a clean sequence $\mathbf{x}_0$, and progressively replaces each token by the mask token, transforming the sequence into $\mathbf{x}_T = mm...m$.
Because each token is masked independently, the forward process factorizes over tokens, and we restrict to the case of a single token transitioning from $x_0^i$ to $m$ in the following.

In continuous Denoising Diffusion Probabilistic Models (DDPM), we start by defining a noising process over $T$ steps, where each step slightly corrupts the signal by adding Gaussian noise:
$$
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:ddpm-step}
x_t = \sqrt{1 - \beta_t}\, x_{t-1} + \sqrt{\beta_t}\,\varepsilon, \qquad \varepsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I)
\end{equation}
$$
where $\beta_t \in [0,1]$ controls the amount of noise injected at step $t \in \{1,\ldots,T\}$.
In discrete space diffusion, at each step, a token either survives unchanged with probability $1 - \beta_t$, or is irreversibly replaced by the mask token $m$ with probability $\beta_t$:

$$
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:mdm-step}
x_t = \begin{cases}
x_{t-1}, & \text{with probability } 1 - \beta_t \\
m, & \text{with probability } \beta_t
\end{cases}
\end{equation}
$$
{/*
The above equation illustrates the discrete masking process from $x_0$ to $x_t$.
However, we want to be able to sample $x_t$ directly from $x_0$, without simulating all the intermediate steps.
In continuous-space diffusion models, one can use the _cumulative noise schedule_ $\alpha_t$ to sample $x_t$ directly from $x_0$ as follows: $x_t = \sqrt{\alpha_t}\, x_0 + \sqrt{1 - \alpha_t}\, \varepsilon$, where $\varepsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I)$. */}

{/*


We derive its analogous counterpart in discrete space, where the same product gives the *survival probability* of a token.
Fix a step $t \in \{1,\ldots,T\}$. */}
From @eq:mdm-step, we can infer the law of $x_t$ given its starting value $x_0$, without simulating the full chain:
$$
q(x_t = x_0 | x_0) = \prod_{i=1}^{t} (1-\beta_i) =: \alpha_t
$$
and we can interpret $\alpha_t$ as the probability that a token has survived up to step $t$, i.e. $x_t$ is still $x_0$ and was not replaced by $m$.


<details>
<summary>Proof</summary>
<div>

$$
\begin{align}
q(x_t = x_0 | x_0)
&= \underbrace{q(x_t = x_0 | x_{t-1} = x_0, x_0)}_{
   \substack{=\,q(x_t = x_0 | x_{t-1} = x_0)\text{ by the Markov property}}}\,
   q(x_{t-1} = x_0 | x_0) \\
&= (1-\beta_t)\,
   q(x_{t-1} = x_0 | x_0) \\
&= \prod_{i=1}^{t} (1-\beta_i) \qquad \text{by recursion}
\end{align}
$$
</div>
</details>

As expected, the schedule $\alpha_t$ decreases in $t$: as the step index $t$ increases, the probability of a token remaining unmasked decreases.
You can observe this trend in Figure <Ref label="fig:masked-forward-discrete" /> when selecting "Show N trajectories".
It turns out that we can then compute the transition probabilities between any two steps $s < t$ as follows:
$$
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:forward-transition}
x_t \mid x_s = \begin{cases}
x_s, & \text{with probability } \frac{\alpha_t}{\alpha_s} \\
m, & \text{with probability } 1 - \frac{\alpha_t}{\alpha_s}
\end{cases}
\end{equation}
$$

<details>
<summary>Proof</summary>
<div>

By the Markov property $q(x_t | x_s, x_0) = q(x_t | x_s)$, the marginal $q(x_t | x_0)$<a href="#conditional-law" class="dfm-note-ref"></a> factorizes through any intermediate step $s$:

$$
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:marginal-recursion}
q(x_t | x_0) = \sum_{x_s} q(x_t | x_s)\, q(x_s | x_0)
\end{equation}
$$

Using $q(x_s | x_0) = \alpha_s\, \mathbb{I}(x_s = x_0) + (1 - \alpha_s)\, \mathbb{I}(x_s = m)$, the sum simplifies to:

$$
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:marginal-split}
q(x_t | x_0) = \alpha_s\, q(x_t | x_s = x_0) + (1 - \alpha_s)\, q(x_t | x_s = m)
\end{equation}
$$

Since masking is irreversible, $q(x_t | x_s = m) = \mathbb{I}(x_t = m)$.
Writing $q(x_t | x_s = x_0) = a\, \mathbb{I}(x_t = x_0) + (1 - a)\, \mathbb{I}(x_t = m)$, for $x_t = x_0$, @eq:marginal-split becomes:  $\alpha_t = \alpha_s \cdot a$, so $a = \dfrac{\alpha_t}{\alpha_s}$

Therefore, for an unmasked token $x_s \neq m$ (since the probabilities sum to 1):

$$
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:masked-transition}
q(x_t | x_s) = \frac{\alpha_t}{\alpha_s}\, \mathbb{I}(x_t = x_s) + \left(1 - \frac{\alpha_t}{\alpha_s}\right) \mathbb{I}(x_t = m)
\end{equation}
$$

</div>
</details>

<ParagraphAccent>

In summary, the forward process masks each token independently and irreversibly: a token remains unmasked until time $t$ with probability $\alpha_t$, and once it reaches the mask state $m$, it stays masked.
The user-defined schedule $\alpha_t$ therefore controls how quickly clean data is transformed into pure mask noise.
We now turn to reversing this process, in order to generate data from noise.
</ParagraphAccent>

#### Reverse process

Generating data requires a reverse process that progressively unmasks tokens to produce samples from the discrete data distribution, i.e. generating samples $x_s$ from $x_t$ for $s < t$ until $s=0$.
However, the transition kernel $q(x_s|x_t)$ is __intractable__.
To overcome this difficulty, we express $q(x_s|x_t)$ by marginalizing the conditional reverse transition $q(x_s | x_t, x_0)$ <a href="#conditional-law" class="dfm-note-ref"></a> over all possible $x_0$.

<Statement kind="Lemma" label="masked-diffusion-bridge" title="Conditional reverse transitions $q(x_s | x_t,x_0)$">
For $x_t = m$ and $s<t$, we have:
$$
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:bridge-mask}
q(x_s | x_t, x_0) = \left[ \frac{\alpha_s - \alpha_t}{1 - \alpha_t} \mathbb{I}(x_s = x_0) + \frac{1 - \alpha_s}{1 - \alpha_t} \mathbb{I}(x_s = m) \right]
\end{equation}
$$
Equivalently:
$$
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:bridge-mask-sampling}
x_s | x_t=m, x_0 = \begin{cases}
x_0, & \text{with probability } \dfrac{\alpha_s - \alpha_t}{1 - \alpha_t} \\
m, & \text{with probability } \dfrac{1 - \alpha_s}{1 - \alpha_t}
\end{cases}
\end{equation}
$$
</Statement>

<details>
<summary>Proof</summary>
<div>

We use Bayes' theorem to compute the probability that a token which is masked at step $t$ reverts to the clean token $x_0$ by step $s < t$:
$$
\begin{align}
\mathbb{P}(x_s = x_0 | x_0, x_t = m) &= \frac{\mathbb{P}(x_t = m | x_s = x_0)\, \mathbb{P}(x_s = x_0 | x_0)}{\mathbb{P}(x_t = m | x_0)}  \label{eq:bridge-bayes-1} \\
&= \frac{\left(1 - \tfrac{\alpha_t}{\alpha_s}\right) \alpha_s}{1 - \alpha_t}  \label{eq:bridge-bayes-2} \\
&= \frac{\alpha_s - \alpha_t}{1 - \alpha_t} \label{eq:bridge-bayes-3}
\end{align}
$$
We conclude using that $x_s$ is either at state $x_0$ or at state $m$ with probabilities that sum to 1.

</div>
</details>

#### Training

We learn the reverse process by training a neural network whose generated trajectories align with those of the forward process.
In Variational Diffusion, this is done by minimizing the KL divergence between the distribution induced by the (known) forward process $q(x_{0:T})$ and the one induced by the learned backward process $p_\theta(x_{0:T})$:

$$
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:path-kl}
\min_\theta\; \mathrm{KL}\!\Big(q(x_{0:T}),\, p_\theta(x_{0:T})\Big)
\end{equation}
$$

We can show that:

$$
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:path-kl-2}
\min_\theta\; \mathrm{KL}\!\Big(q(x_{0:T}),\, p_\theta(x_{0:T})\Big)
\;\iff\;
\max_\theta\; \mathbb{E}_{q(x_0)}\!\left[\text{ELBO}_{\theta}(x_0)\right]
\end{equation}
$$

where the ELBO (evidence lower bound) is defined as:

$$
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:elbo}
\text{ELBO}_{\theta}(x_0) = \mathbb{E}_{x_{1:T} \sim q(\cdot | x_0)} \left[ \log p_\theta(x_{0:T}) - \log q(x_{1:T}|x_0) \right]
\end{equation}
$$

<details>
<summary>Equivalence of KL minimization and ELBO maximization</summary>
<div>

Expand the KL by factoring $q(x_{0:T}) = q(x_0)\, q(x_{1:T}|x_0)$:

$$
\begin{align}
\mathrm{KL}\!\Big(q(x_{0:T}),\, p_\theta(x_{0:T})\Big) &= \mathbb{E}_{q(x_{0:T})}\!\left[\log \frac{q(x_{0:T})}{p_\theta(x_{0:T})}\right]  \label{eq:kl-elbo-proof-2} \\
&\hspace{-8em}{}= \mathbb{E}_{q(x_0)}\!\left[\mathbb{E}_{q(x_{1:T}|x_0)}\!\left[\log \frac{q(x_0)\,q(x_{1:T}|x_0)}{p_\theta(x_{0:T})}\right]\right]  \label{eq:kl-elbo-proof-3} \\
&\hspace{-8em}{}= \mathbb{E}_{q(x_0)}\!\left[\log q(x_0)\right] + \mathbb{E}_{q(x_0)}\!\left[\mathbb{E}_{q(x_{1:T}|x_0)}\!\left[\log q(x_{1:T}|x_0) - \log p_\theta(x_{0:T})\right]\right]  \label{eq:kl-elbo-proof-4} \\
&\hspace{-8em}{}= -H(q) - \mathbb{E}_{q(x_0)}\!\left[\text{ELBO}_{\theta}(x_0)\right] \label{eq:kl-elbo-proof-6}
\end{align}
$$

Since $H(q) = -\mathbb{E}_{q(x_0)}[\log q(x_0)]$ does not depend on $\theta$, minimizing
the KL is exactly equivalent to maximizing $\mathbb{E}_{q(x_0)}[\text{ELBO}_{\theta}(x_0)]$.

</div>
</details>

As written, @eq:elbo couples all $T$ steps along a whole trajectory $x_{1:T}$. The following theorem shows that we can simplify this global objective into a sum of local objectives, one for each step.

<Statement kind="Theorem" label="discrete-diffusion-elbo" title="Discrete diffusion ELBO">
The ELBO for a discrete diffusion process is given by [<a class="cite-ref" href="#ref-1">1</a>, <a class="cite-ref" href="#ref-2">2</a>, <a class="cite-ref" href="#NonequilibriumThermodynamics">10</a>]:

$$
\begin{align}
\text{ELBO}_{\theta}(x_0) ={}& - \mathrm{KL}(q(x_T | x_0) , p_T)  \label{eq:discrete-elbo-1} \\
& - \sum_{t=1}^{T} \mathbb{E}_{x_t \sim q( \cdot | x_0)} \left[ \mathrm{KL}\!\left(q\!\left(x_{t-1} | x_t, x_0\right),\, p_\theta\!\left(x_{t-1} | x_t\right)\right) \right]  \label{eq:discrete-elbo-2} \notag
\end{align}
$$
where $p_T$ is a the prior distribution, here $p_T = \delta_{m\ldots m}$.
</Statement>

<details>
<summary>Proof</summary>
<div>

The forward process follows a Markovian structure:

$$
\begin{align}
    q(x_{1:T}|x_0) &= \prod_{t=1}^{T} q(x_t | x_{t-1}, x_{t-2}, \dots, x_0)  \label{eq:forward-markov-1} \\
    &= \prod_{t=1}^{T} q(x_t | x_{t-1}, x_0) \label{eq:forward-markov-2}
\end{align}
$$

where the last equality holds because the forward process is memoryless given the previous states. Similarly, the reverse model decomposes as:

$$
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:reverse-factorization}
    p_\theta(x_{0:T}) = p_\theta(x_T) \prod_{t=1}^{T} p_\theta(x_{t-1} | x_t).
\end{equation}
$$

Substituting into the ELBO:

$$
\begin{align}
    \text{ELBO}_{\theta}(x_0) &= \mathbb{E}_{\substack{x_{1:T} \sim q( \cdot | x_0)}} \left[ \log p_\theta(x_T) \right]  \label{eq:elbo-expand-1} \\
    &\quad + \sum_{t=1}^{T} \mathbb{E}_{\substack{x_{1:T} \sim q( \cdot | x_0)}} \left[ \log p_\theta(x_{t-1} | x_t) - \log q(x_t | x_{t-1}, x_0) \right]  \label{eq:elbo-expand-2} \notag
\end{align}
$$

Using Bayes' theorem, we can rewrite the forward process conditional:

$$
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:elbo-sum}
\begin{aligned}
    \log q(x_t | x_{t-1}, x_0) &= \log q(x_{t-1} | x_t, x_0) + \log q(x_t | x_0) \\
    &\quad - \log q(x_{t-1} | x_0)
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
$$

Substituting into the ELBO and observing that consecutive terms telescope:

$$
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:elbo-mask}
\begin{aligned}
    \text{ELBO}_{\theta}(x_0) &= \mathbb{E}_{\substack{x_{1:T} \sim q( \cdot | x_0)}} \left[ \log q(x_T | x_0) - \log q(x_0 | x_0) \right] \\
    &\quad + \mathbb{E}_{\substack{x_{1:T} \sim q( \cdot | x_0)}} \left[ \log p_\theta(x_T) \right] \\
    &\quad + \sum_{t=1}^{T} \mathbb{E}_{\substack{x_{1:T} \sim q( \cdot | x_0)}} \left[ \log p_\theta(x_{t-1} | x_t) - \log q(x_{t-1} | x_t, x_0) \right]
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
$$

Since $\log q(x_0 | x_0) = \log(1) = 0$:

$$
\begin{align}
    \text{ELBO}_{\theta}(x_0) &= - \mathbb{E}_{\substack{x_{1:T} \sim q( \cdot | x_0)}} \left[ \log q(x_T | x_0) - \log p_\theta(x_T) \right] \notag \\
    &\quad - \sum_{t=1}^{T} \mathbb{E}_{\substack{x_{1:T} \sim q( \cdot | x_0)}} \big[ \log q(x_{t-1} | x_t, x_0) - \log p_\theta(x_{t-1} | x_t) \big] \label{eq:elbo-rewrite}
\end{align}
$$

We simplify by marginalizing over all intermediate variables. For the first term:

$$
\begin{align}
    &\mathbb{E}_{\substack{x_{1:T} \sim q( \cdot | x_0)}} \left[ \log q(x_T | x_0) - \log p_\theta(x_T) \right] \label{eq:elbo-continuous} \\
    &= \mathbb{E}_{\substack{x_T \sim q( \cdot | x_0)}} \left[ \log q(x_T | x_0) - \log p_\theta(x_T) \right] \notag
\end{align}
$$

For each term in the sum:

$$
\begin{align}
    &\mathbb{E}_{\substack{x_{1:T} \sim q( \cdot | x_0)}} \left[ \log q(x_{t-1} | x_t, x_0) - \log p_\theta(x_{t-1} | x_t) \right]  \label{eq:posterior-exp-1} \\
    &= \mathbb{E}_{\substack{x_t \sim q( \cdot | x_0)}} \bigg[ \mathbb{E}_{\substack{x_{t-1} \sim q( \cdot | x_t, x_0)}}  \label{eq:posterior-exp-2} \\
    &\qquad \left[ \log q(x_{t-1} | x_t, x_0) - \log p_\theta(x_{t-1} | x_t) \right] \bigg] \notag
\end{align}
$$

Therefore:

$$
\begin{align}
    \text{ELBO}_{\theta}(x_0) &= - \mathrm{KL}\left(q(x_T|x_0) , p_T\right)  \label{eq:discrete-elbo-proof-1} \\
    &\quad - \sum_{t=1}^{T} \mathbb{E}_{\substack{x_t \sim q( \cdot | x_0)}} \left[ \mathrm{KL}\left(q(x_{t-1} | x_t, x_0) , p_\theta(x_{t-1} | x_t)\right) \right]  \label{eq:discrete-elbo-proof-2} \notag
\end{align}
$$

where the first KL term is a constant independent of $\theta$, since $p_\theta(x_T) = p_T$ is a fixed base distribution.

</div>
</details>

The sum of KL terms corresponds to a forward trajectory where $x_0, x_1, \ldots, x_T$ is sampled from the noising process, and at each step the learned reverse transition $p_\theta(x_{t-1} | x_t)$ is trained to match the reverse transition conditioned on the initial clean token $x_0$: $q(x_{t-1} | x_t, x_0)$.

To sample from the data, we therefore need to learn the transitions $p_\theta(x_{t-1} | x_t)$. {/* They can be parameterized directly with a neural network, but this is inefficient.
Indeed, we know that a token changes at most once along the trajectory.
From @eq:bridge-denoiser-1, the only information not already determined is which clean token to jump to.
Therefore, outputting the full reverse step at every $t$ would relearn this fixed structure repeatedly, which is redundant. */}
However, the discrete diffusion models of [<a class="cite-ref" href="#ref-1">1</a>, <a class="cite-ref" href="#ref-2">2</a>] prefer to follow the key innovation of DDPM [<a class="cite-ref" href="#DDPM">11</a>] instead: predict the clean signal $x_0$ and rebuild the reverse step using $q(x_{t-1} | x_t, x_0)$.
Here, we use @eq:denoiser-bridge-proof-1, which combines this term with the denoiser $\mathbb{P}(X_0 = v | X_t=x_t)$ to weight each possible clean token.
Since this denoiser is itself intractable, we approximate it by a learned denoiser $D_{\theta,t}(x_t) \in \Delta_{|\mathcal{V}|}$.<a href="#denoiser-mask" class="dfm-note-ref"></a>.
This definition naturally extends to each position $i$ if we consider sequences: $[D_{\theta,t}^i(\mathbf{x}_t)]_v \approx \mathbb{P}(X_0^i = v | \mathbf{X}_t = \mathbf{x}_t)$.

{/* Equivalently, the learned reverse transition is obtained by replacing the denoiser $\mathbb{P}(X_0 = v | X_t=x_t)$ in @eq:bridge-denoiser-1 with the learned one $D_{\theta,t}$:
$$

    p_\theta(x_s=v| x_t=m) &= \frac{\alpha_s-\alpha_t}{1-\alpha_t}\,[D_{\theta,t}(x_t)]_v\quad (v\in\mathcal{V}\setminus\{m\}),  \label{eq:bridge-model-1} \\
    p_\theta(x_s=m| x_t=m) &= \frac{1-\alpha_s}{1-\alpha_t}. \label{eq:bridge-model-2}
\end{align}
$$ */}

The following proposition rewrites the ELBO term entirely in terms of this denoiser.

<Statement kind="Proposition" label="masked-diffusion-training-loss" title="Masked diffusion training loss">
In the specific case of Masked Diffusion, we can express the loss in terms of the denoiser output $D_{\theta,t}(\mathbf{x}_t) = (D_{\theta,t}^1(\mathbf{x}_t), \ldots, D_{\theta,t}^S(\mathbf{x}_t)) \in \Delta_{|\mathcal{V}|}^S$, where $t \sim \mathrm{Unif}\{1,\ldots,T\}$:

$$
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:mdm-loss}
L_T(\theta) = T\,\mathbb{E}_{\substack{\mathbf{x}_0 \sim p_0 \\ t \sim \mathrm{Unif}\{1,\ldots,T\} \\ \mathbf{x}_t \sim q( \cdot | \mathbf{x}_0)}} \left[ \sum_{i=1}^{S} \mathbb{I}(x_t^i = m) \left( -\frac{\alpha_{t-1} - \alpha_t}{1 - \alpha_t} \mathbf{x}_0^{i \top} \log D_{\theta,t}^i(\mathbf{x}_t) \right) \right]
\end{equation}
$$
</Statement>

<details>
<summary>Proof</summary>
<div>

We write the proof only for one token, starting from @eq:discrete-elbo-1. Since $x_T = m$ is fully masked, we have $\mathrm{KL}(q(x_T|x_0) , p_\theta(x_T)) = 0$. Therefore:
$$
\begin{align}
&\text{ELBO}_{\theta}(x_0) = {} \label{eq:mdm-elbo-no-source} \\
&- \sum_{t=1}^{T} \mathbb{E}_{\substack{x_t \sim q( \cdot | x_0)}} \left[ \mathrm{KL}\left(q(x_{t-1} | x_t, x_0) , p_\theta(x_{t-1} | x_t)\right) \right] \notag
\end{align}
$$

Let $s = t-1$. The KL divergence can be expanded as a sum over all possible values of $x_s \in \mathcal{V}$:
$$
\begin{align}
& \mathrm{KL}\left(q(x_s | x_t, x_0) , p_\theta(x_s | x_t)\right) \label{eq:bridge-kl} \\
& \quad = \sum_{x_s \in \mathcal{V}} q(x_s | x_t, x_0) \log \frac{q(x_s | x_t, x_0)}{p_\theta(x_s | x_t)} \notag
\end{align}
$$

When $x_t\neq m$, both $q(x_s| x_t,x_0)$ and $p_\theta(x_s| x_t)$ put all their mass on $x_s=x_t=x_0$, so the KL term is zero.

Hence only the case $x_t=m$ contributes. Therefore, using @eq:bridge-mask together with the learned reverse transition @eq:bridge-denoiser-1 and @bridge-denoiser-2,

$$
\begin{align}
    &\mathrm{KL}\!\left(q(x_s| x_t,x_0)\,\|\,p_\theta(x_s| x_t)\right) \notag \\
    &= \mathbb{I}(x_t=m)
    \sum_{x_s \in \mathcal{V}\cup\{m\}}
    q(x_s| x_t,x_0)\,
    \log\frac{q(x_s| x_t,x_0)}{p_\theta(x_s| x_t)} \label{eq:bridge-kl-proof-2} \\
    &= \mathbb{I}(x_t=m)
    \sum_{x_s \in \mathcal{V}\cup\{m\}}
    \left[
        \frac{\alpha_s-\alpha_t}{1-\alpha_t}\mathbb{I}(x_s=x_0)
        + \frac{1-\alpha_s}{1-\alpha_t}\mathbb{I}(x_s=m)
    \right] \label{eq:bridge-kl-proof-3} \\
    &\quad \log
    \frac{
        \frac{\alpha_s-\alpha_t}{1-\alpha_t}\mathbb{I}(x_s=x_0)
        + \frac{1-\alpha_s}{1-\alpha_t}\mathbb{I}(x_s=m)
    }{
        \frac{\alpha_s-\alpha_t}{1-\alpha_t}[D_{\theta,t}(x_t)]_{x_s}\mathbb{I}(x_s\neq m)
        + \frac{1-\alpha_s}{1-\alpha_t}\mathbb{I}(x_s=m)
    } \notag \\
    &= \mathbb{I}(x_t=m)\Bigg[
    \frac{\alpha_s-\alpha_t}{1-\alpha_t}
    \log\frac{\frac{\alpha_s-\alpha_t}{1-\alpha_t}}
    {\frac{\alpha_s-\alpha_t}{1-\alpha_t}[D_{\theta,t}(x_t)]_{x_0}}
    +\frac{1-\alpha_s}{1-\alpha_t}
    \underbrace{\log\frac{\frac{1-\alpha_s}{1-\alpha_t}}
    {\frac{1-\alpha_s}{1-\alpha_t}}}_{=0}
    \Bigg] \label{eq:bridge-kl-proof-4} \\
    &= \mathbb{I}(x_t=m)\,\frac{\alpha_s-\alpha_t}{1-\alpha_t}\,
    \big(-\log [D_{\theta,t}(x_t)]_{x_0}\big). \label{eq:bridge-kl-proof-5}
\end{align}
$$

Plugging this into the ELBO yields:
$$
\begin{equation}
\text{ELBO}_{\theta}(x_0) = \sum_{t=1}^T \mathbb{E}_{x_t \sim q(\cdot | x_0)} \left[\mathbb{I}(x_t=m)\,\frac{\alpha_{t-1}-\alpha_t}{1-\alpha_t}\,x_0^\top \log D_{\theta, t}(x_t)\right]. \label{eq:mdm-elbo-sum-1}
\end{equation}
$$

Equivalently, by sampling a step $t \sim \mathrm{Unif}\{1,\ldots,T\}$, we use $\sum_{t=1}^{T} f(t) = T\,\mathbb{E}_{t \sim \mathrm{Unif}\{1,\ldots,T\}}[f(t)]$:
$$
\begin{align}
\text{ELBO}_{\theta}(x_0)
&= T\,\mathbb{E}_{\substack{t \sim \mathrm{Unif}\{1,\ldots,T\} \\ x_t \sim q( \cdot | x_0)}} \left[
    \mathbb{I}(x_t=m)\,\frac{\alpha_{t-1}-\alpha_t}{1-\alpha_t}
    \,x_0^\top \log D_{\theta, t}(x_t)\right]. \label{eq:mdm-elbo-uniform-t}
\end{align}
$$

<div>

A drawback of the discrete-time construction presented in this section is that the loss @eq:mdm-loss only evaluates the denoiser $D_{\theta,t}$ at the $T$ grid times $t\in\{1,\ldots,T\}$, so it learns to undo the noise at the levels $\alpha_1,\ldots,\alpha_T$ and no others. Refining the schedule $\alpha_t$ at sampling time places new levels between the trained ones, where the denoiser must be evaluated at a noise level it never saw. Avoiding this mismatch ties us to the fixed grid $\alpha_1,\ldots,\alpha_T$. To remove this constraint, [<a class="cite-ref" href="#ref-3">3</a>] introduced the continuous-time limit of these models (later simplified for masked diffusion by [<a class="cite-ref" href="#ShiMaskedDiffusion">13</a>]): training over $t\sim\mathcal{U}(0,1)$ exposes the denoiser to every level, so the number of steps and the schedule become choices made after training rather than before.

In order to provide a continuous-time version of the loss @eq:mdm-loss, we first need define a continuous-time analogue of the survival schedule $\alpha_t = \displaystyle\prod_{i=1}^{t} (1 - \beta_i)$, and give an intuition for its form.

Introduce a continuous masking-rate function $\beta : [0,1] \to \mathbb{R}_+$. If a discrete grid has step size $\Delta t = 1/T$, the corresponding small masking probability at grid point $i/T$ is
$$
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:ct-beta-discretization}
\beta_i := \beta(i/T)\Delta t = \frac{1}{T}\beta\left(\frac{i}{T}\right).
\end{equation}
$$

For a continuous time $t\in[0,1]$, write $n=\lfloor Tt\rfloor$. The discrete survival probability becomes
$$
\begin{align}
q(x_t = x_0 | x_0)
&\approx \prod_{i=1}^{n}
\left(1- \frac{1}{T}\beta\left(\frac{i}{T}\right)\right) \label{eq:survival-prob-ct-1} \\
&= \exp\left(
\sum_{i=1}^{n}
\log\left(1-\frac{1}{T}\beta\left(\frac{i}{T}\right)\right)
\right) \label{eq:survival-prob-ct-2} \\
&\approx \exp\left(
-\sum_{i=1}^{n}
\frac{1}{T}\beta\left(\frac{i}{T}\right)
\right) \label{eq:survival-prob-ct-3} \\
&\xrightarrow[T\to\infty]{}
\exp\left(-\int_0^t \beta(s)\, ds\right), \label{eq:survival-prob-ct-4}
\end{align}
$$
where the last line recognizes a Riemann sum.

This motivates the continuous-time survival schedule
$$
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:alpha-def}
\alpha_t = \exp\!\left(-\int_0^t \beta(s)\, ds\right).
\end{equation}
$$
We deduce the following properties on $\alpha_t$ from the derivation above:
- $\alpha_0 = 1$: at time $t=0$, all tokens are unmasked.
- $\alpha_1 = 0$: at time $t=1$, all tokens are masked.
- $\alpha_t$ is decreasing in $t$: as the step index increases, the probability of a token remaining unmasked decreases.

Now we want to express the continuous-time limit of denoiser loss @eq:mdm-loss. We normalize the timsteps in [0,1] and therefore define the grid $\tau_n=n/T$, $\Delta t=1/T$, with $\alpha_n=\alpha_{\tau_n}$ and $\tau_{n-1}=\tau_n-\Delta t$.

$$
\begin{align}
L_T(\theta)
&= T\,\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}_0\sim p_0}\;
\underbrace{\mathbb{E}_{n\sim\mathrm{Unif}\{1,\ldots,T\}}}_{\textstyle =\,\frac{1}{T}\sum_{n=1}^{T}}\;
\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}_{\tau_n}\sim q(\cdot|\mathbf{x}_0)} \\
&\quad \left[\sum_{i=1}^{S}\mathbb{I}(x_{\tau_n}^i=m)\left(-\frac{\alpha_{n-1}-\alpha_n}{1-\alpha_n}\,\mathbf{x}_0^{i\top}\log D_{\theta,\tau_n}^i(\mathbf{x}_{\tau_n})\right)\right] \notag \\[1.4em]
&= \mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}_0\sim p_0}\;
\frac{1}{T}\sum_{n=1}^{T}\;
\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}_{\tau_n}\sim q(\cdot|\mathbf{x}_0)} \\
&\quad \left[\sum_{i=1}^{S}\mathbb{I}(x_{\tau_n}^i=m)\;
\underbrace{T\,\frac{\alpha_{n-1}-\alpha_n}{1-\alpha_n}}_{\textstyle =\,\frac{-\dot{\alpha}_{\tau_n}}{1-\alpha_{\tau_n}}+o(1)}
\left(-\mathbf{x}_0^{i\top}\log D_{\theta,\tau_n}^i(\mathbf{x}_{\tau_n})\right)\right] \notag
\end{align}
$$

As $T\to\infty$ this average is a Riemann sum, $\tfrac{1}{T}\sum_{n=1}^{T} g(\tau_n)\to\int_0^1 g(t)\,dt=\mathbb{E}_{t\sim\mathcal{U}(0,1)}[g(t)]$, and we obtain the continuous-time masked denoiser loss:

$$
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:mdm-loss-ct}
L(\theta) =
\mathbb{E}_{\substack{
\mathbf{x}_0 \sim p_0 \\
t \sim \mathcal{U}(0,1) \\
\mathbf{x}_t \sim q(\cdot|\mathbf{x}_0)
}}
\left[
\sum_{i=1}^{S}
\mathbb{I}(x_t^i=m)\,
\frac{-\dot{\alpha}_t}{1-\alpha_t}
\left(-\mathbf{x}_0^{i\top}\log D_{\theta,t}^i(\mathbf{x}_t)\right)
\right].
\end{equation}
$$

</div>

### Score-based discrete diffusion

{/* We now turn to the score-based viewpoint: predict a score function, i.e. the direction pointing toward more likely samples at noise level $t$, and follow it to move from noise back to data. In continuous diffusion, a key tool in the analysis is to model the forward process as a discretization of a forward stochastic differential equation; simulating the backward process then amounts to discretizing the associated reverse SDE. Critically, the reverse SDE involves the score, namely $\nabla \log p_t(x)$, where $p_t$ is the distribution of the variables $x_t$ defined by the forward process. */}

In this section, we study the score-based perspective on discrete diffusion.
The main idea is to learn a transport guided by the _score function_ $s_t(x_t)$ to reverse the diffusion process.
This quantity points toward more likely samples at noise level $t$, guiding the generation process back toward the data distribution.
In continuous-space diffusion models, the generation process, i.e. the backward process, is defined by the reverse SDE (see Figure <Ref label="fig:discrete-continuous-score" />, bottom); critically, the reverse SDE involves the score, namely $\nabla_x \log p_t(x_t)$, where $p_t$ is the distribution of the variables $x_t$ defined by the forward process.

We follow the same approach in discrete space.
Since there is no SDE involved here, we work with the first-order expansion of $q(x_{t+\Delta t} | x_t)$ as $\Delta t \to 0$, which plays the role of $dx_t$ in the forward SDE, i.e. describing the evolution of $x_t$ during the corruption process.
Then we reverse the process to obtain $p(x_{t-\Delta t} | x_t)$ and identify the **concrete score** $s_t(x_t)_{y}$, the discrete analogue of $\nabla_x \log p_t(x_t)$ (the score function of the continuous process).
Finally, we learn a neural network to predict the concrete score and use it to generate new discrete data.

#### Forward process

In continuous-space score-based diffusion, we model the forward process as the solution of the SDE $dx_t = f_t(x_t)\,dt + g_t\,dW_t$, where we fix the drift $f_t$ and the diffusion coefficient $g_t$, and $W_t$ is a Wiener process.
We follow a similar approach in discrete space: we model the forward process as a continuous-time Markov chain (CTMC), which can be written as

<PinkEquation>
$$
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:forward-rate}
q(x_{t+\Delta t} = y | x_t) = \mathbb{I}(y = x_t) + [\dot{Q}_t(x_t)]_y \Delta t + o(\Delta t),
\end{equation}
$$
</PinkEquation>

where we choose the forward rate matrix $\dot{Q}_t \in \mathbb{R}^{|\mathcal{V}| \times |\mathcal{V}|}$.
In the above equation and in what follows, $[\dot{Q}_t(x_t)]_y$ denotes the entry of $\dot{Q}_t$ at row $x_t$ and column $y$.

For continuous-space diffusion models, the forward SDE means that over an infinitesimal time $dt$, a particle at $x_t$ moves by a deterministic drift $f_t(x_t)\,dt$ plus an independent Gaussian increment of standard deviation $g_t\sqrt{dt}$. Here, $[\dot{Q}_t(x_t)]_y$ is the instantaneous rate at which a particle at state $x_t$ jumps to state $y$: over a small interval $\Delta t$, the probability of such a jump is $[\dot{Q}_t(x_t)]_y\Delta t$ to first order.

{/* A common instantiation of the continuous SDE is the variance-preserving Gaussian diffusion: the coefficients are chosen as $f_t(x_t) = -\frac{1}{2}\beta(t)x_t$ and $g_t = \sqrt{\beta(t)}$, where $\beta(t)$ is a noise schedule.  */}
Then, we need to specify the forward rate matrix $\dot{Q}_t$ that governs the masking process.
A simple choice is to set the rate of transition from any unmasked token to the mask state:

$$
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:q-dot-def}
\dot{Q}_t
= \sigma(t) (\mathbf{1}\mathbf{e}_m^\top - I)
= \sigma(t)
\begin{pmatrix}
-1 & 0 & \dots & 0 & 1 \\
0 & -1 & \dots & 0 & 1 \\
\vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots & \vdots \\
0 & 0 & \dots & -1 & 1 \\
0 & 0 & \dots & 0 & 0
\end{pmatrix},
\end{equation}
$$

where $\sigma(t) > 0$ is the instantaneous masking rate, $\mathbf{e}_m$ is the one-hot vector for the mask token.
The last column of $\dot{Q}_t$ indicates that the rates for jumping to $m$ are equal to $\sigma(t)$ for any token $x_t\in\mathcal{V}\setminus\{m\}$, and the last row indicates that the rate for jumping from $m$ to any other state is 0 (the mask is an absorbing state).

Continuous-space diffusion models benefits from _simulation-free_ forward processes, which mean we can directly sample $x_t$ given $x_0$ without having to simulate the process step by step, simply using the closed-form expression $q(x_t | x_0) = \mathcal{N}\!\left(x_t;\ \sqrt{\alpha_t}\,x_0,\ (1-\alpha_t)I\right)$, where $\alpha_t = \exp\!\left(-\int_0^t \beta(s)\,ds\right)$ is the cumulative noise schedule.
The same thing is possible in discrete space, we can define the transition matrix from time $0$ to time $t$, denoted $\bar{Q}_{t|0}$, such that $q(x_t | x_0)=\mathrm{Cat}(x_t;\ \bar{Q}_{t|0}^\top x_0)$.
Denoting $\alpha_t = \exp\!\left(-\int_0^t \sigma(s)\,ds\right)$ the *cumulative masking schedule*, we have that $\bar{Q}_{t|0}$ takes the following form:

$$
\begin{align}
\bar{Q}_{t|0}
&= \alpha_t I + (1-\alpha_t)\mathbf{1}\mathbf{e}_m^\top \\
&=
\begin{pmatrix}
\alpha_t & 0 & \dots & 0 & 1-\alpha_t \\
0 & \alpha_t & \dots & 0 & 1-\alpha_t \\
\vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots & \vdots \\
0 & 0 & \dots & \alpha_t & 1-\alpha_t \\
0 & 0 & \dots & 0 & 1
\end{pmatrix},
\label{eq:integrated-forward-transition}
\end{align}
$$

Therefore, for a clean token $x_0$, the forward marginal is simply:

$$
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:ctmc-forward-marginal}
x_t =
\begin{cases}
x_0, & \text{with probability } \alpha_t, \\
m, & \text{with probability } 1-\alpha_t.
\end{cases}
\end{equation}
$$

<summary>Proof</summary>

Consider a small time step $\Delta t$. Using the first-order Taylor expansion $\alpha_{t+\Delta t} \approx \alpha_t + \dot{\alpha}_t \Delta t$, the ratio of survival probabilities becomes:

$$
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:alpha-ratio}
\frac{\alpha_{t+\Delta t}}{\alpha_t} \approx 1 + \frac{\dot{\alpha}_t}{\alpha_t} \Delta t
\end{equation}
$$

Substituting into the compact expression @eq:transition-matrix for $\bar{Q}_{t+\Delta t|t}$:

$$
\begin{align}
\bar{Q}_{t+\Delta t|t} &\approx \left(1 + \frac{\dot{\alpha}_t}{\alpha_t}\Delta t\right) I + \left(1 - \left(1 + \frac{\dot{\alpha}_t}{\alpha_t}\Delta t\right)\right)\mathbf{1}\mathbf{e}_m^\top  \label{eq:q-dot-derivation-1} \\
&= I + \Delta t\, \frac{\dot{\alpha}_t}{\alpha_t} \left(I - \mathbf{1}\mathbf{e}_m^\top\right) \label{eq:q-dot-derivation-2}
\end{align}
$$

We now calculate $q(x_{t+\Delta t} | x_t) = \text{Cat}(x_{t+\Delta t};\ \bar{Q}_{t+\Delta t|t}^\top x_t)$.
For a fixed state $x_t = e_k$, the probability of transitioning to state $y$ is the $y$-th entry of $\bar{Q}_{t+\Delta t|t}^\top e_k$:

$$
\begin{align}
q(x_{t+\Delta t} = y | x_t) &= \left[\bar{Q}_{t+\Delta t|t}^\top e_k\right]_y  \label{eq:forward-rate-proof-1} \\
&= \mathbb{I}(y = x_t) + \Delta t\, \frac{\dot{\alpha}_t}{\alpha_t}\left[\left(I - \mathbf{1}\mathbf{e}_m^\top\right)^\top\right]_{ky} + o(\Delta t) \label{eq:forward-rate-proof-2}
\end{align}
$$

Identifying the coefficient of $\Delta t$ with $[\dot{Q}_t(x_t)]_y$ yields:

$$
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:q-dot-mask}
\dot{Q}_t = \frac{\dot{\alpha}_t}{\alpha_t}\left(I - \mathbf{1}\mathbf{e}_m^\top\right) = \sigma(t)\left(\mathbf{1}\mathbf{e}_m^\top - I\right)
\end{equation}
$$

where $\sigma(t) = -\dot{\alpha}_t / \alpha_t$. Since $\alpha_t$ is decreasing, $\dot{\alpha}_t < 0$, so $\sigma(t)$ is indeed positive.

</div>
</details>
*/}

#### Reverse process

In the previous section, we have described the forward process of score-based discrete diffusion models. To generate new data samples, we need to reverse the forward process.
Similarly to continuous-space diffusion, where the reverse process is obtained by reversing the forward process, in discrete space, the reverse process is obtained by reversing the forward Markov chain, and is also a continuous-time Markov chain.

<Statement kind="Proposition" label="reverse-rate" title="Reverse rate $\dot{R}_t$">
Interestingly, the reverse process is also a continuous-time Markov chain, and its transition probabilities can be expressed as:
$$
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:reverse-rate}
p(x_{t-\Delta t} = y | x_t) = \mathbb{I}(y = x_t) + \Delta t \, [\dot{R}_t(x_t)]_y + o(\Delta t)
\end{equation}
$$

Where the generator matrix $\dot{R}_t$ is defined as:
$$
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:reverse-rate-mask}
[\dot{R}_t(x_t)]_y =
\begin{cases}
[\dot{Q}_t(y)]_{x_t} \frac{q(X_t = y)}{q(X_t = x_t)} & \text{if } y \neq x_t \\
- \sum_{y' \neq x_t} [\dot{R}_t(x_t)]_{y'} & \text{if } y = x_t
\end{cases}
\end{equation}
$$
</Statement>

<details>
<summary>Proof</summary>
<div>

Using Bayes' theorem, for $y \neq x_t$:
$$
\begin{align}
p(x_{t-\Delta t} = y | x_t) &= \frac{q(x_t | x_{t-\Delta t} = y) q(x_{t-\Delta t} = y)}{q(x_t)}  \label{eq:reverse-rate-proof-1} \\
&= \frac{\big([\dot{Q}_t(y)]_{x_t}\Delta t + o(\Delta t)\big) \big(q(X_t = y) + O(\Delta t)\big)}{q(X_t = x_t)}  \label{eq:reverse-rate-proof-2} \\
&= \left( [\dot{Q}_t(y)]_{x_t} \frac{q(X_t = y)}{q(X_t = x_t)} \right)\Delta t + o(\Delta t) \label{eq:reverse-rate-proof-3}
\end{align}
$$

By identifying this result with the definition of the reverse local transition $p(x_{t-\Delta t} = y | x_t) = \Delta t [\dot{R}_t(x_t)]_y + o(\Delta t)$, we conclude for $y \neq x_t$.
For $y = x_t$, we use the fact that probabilities sum to one, so the rows of a valid transition rate matrix must sum to zero, leading to $[\dot{R}_t(x_t)]_{x_t} = - \sum_{y' \neq x_t} [\dot{R}_t(x_t)]_{y'}$.

</div>
</details>

In continuous-space diffusion, the score function $\nabla_x \log p_t(x_t)$ governs the reverse SDE, i.e. the generative process.
In the discrete space, the analog to the score function is the **concrete score** $s_t(x_t)_y$, defined as the ratio of marginal probabilities between a candidate destination state and the current state:
$$
s_t(x_t)_y \;=\; \frac{q(X_t = y)}{q(X_t = x_t)} \quad (y \neq x_t).
$$
Recalling @eq:reverse-rate-mask, the concrete score is precisely the quantity that converts the (known) forward rate $\dot{Q}_t$ into the reverse rate $\dot{R}_t$:
$$

\begin{equation}
\label{eq:concrete-score}
s_t(x_t)_y \;=\; \frac{[\dot{R}_t(x_t)]_y}{[\dot{Q}_t(x_t)]_y}.
\end{equation}
$$
Then the reverse process can be simulated using the concrete score as follows:
$$
\begin{align}
\label{eq:score-sampling}
x_{t-h} &\sim \mathrm{Cat}\left(
\mathbf{e}_{x_t}
+ h\,\dot{R}_t(x_t)
+ o(h)
\right) \\
&=
\mathrm{Cat}\left(
\mathbf{e}_{x_t}
+ h\,\big([\dot{Q}_t(x_t)]_{\cdot}\odot s_t(x_t)_{\cdot}\big)
+ o(h)
\right), \label{eq:score-sampling-cat}
\end{align}
$$

where $\odot$ denotes the elementwise product. The forward rate $\dot{Q}_t$ is known because it is a design choice of the noising process.
However, the concrete score $s_t(x_t)_y$ (or equivalently the reverse rate $\dot{R}_t$) depends on the noisy-data marginals $q(X_t=\cdot)$ which are unknown and untractable.
This is why, in practice, we learn a neural approximation of it, denoted by $s_{\theta, t}(x_t)_y$.


#### Training

In the forward and reverse process sections, we showed how these processes can be simulated with respectively the forward rate $\dot{Q}_t$ @eq:forward-rate and the reverse rate $\dot{R}_t$ @eq:reverse-rate, and that the unknown quantity connecting them is the concrete score $s_t(x_t)_y$ @eq:concrete-score.
However, this last is untractable and therefore we need to rewrite it before approximating it with a neural network $s_{\theta,t}$. To do so, we apply the _marginalisation trick_, meaning we express the concrete score $s_t(x_t)_y$ as a conditional expectation of the conditional concrete score $s_t(x_t | x_0)_y$ over $x_0$:

$$
\begin{align}
s_t(x_t)_y \;=\; \frac{p_t(y)}{p_t(x_t)}
&\;=\; \frac{\sum_{x_0} p_t(y|x_0)\,p(x_0)}{p_t(x_t)} \label{eq:concrete-score-cond-exp-1} \\
&\;=\; \sum_{x_0} \frac{p_t(y|x_0)}{p_t(x_t|x_0)}\;
\underbrace{\frac{p_t(x_t|x_0)\,p(x_0)}{p_t(x_t)}}_{q(x_0 | x_t)\ \text{by Bayes}} \label{eq:concrete-score-cond-exp-2} \\
&\;=\; \mathbb{E}_{x_0 \sim q(\cdot|x_t)}\!\left[\,s_t(x_t|x_0)_y\,\right] \label{eq:concrete-score-cond-exp-3}
\end{align}
$$

This gives the characterization:<a href="#generalized-kl" class="dfm-note-ref"></a>

$$
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:score-kl-argmin}
s_t \;=\; \underset{s}{\operatorname{argmin}}\ \mathbb{E}_{\substack{x_0 \sim p_0 \\ x_t \sim q(\cdot|x_0)}}\!\Bigg[ \sum_{y \neq x_t} \mathrm{KL}\!\left(s_t(x_t | x_0)_y,\, s(x_t)_y\right) \Bigg]
\end{equation}
$$

{/* Approximating $s_t$ by a neural network $s_{\theta,t}$ and weighting each term by the forward rate $[\dot{Q}_t(x_t)]_y$ (which leaves the minimizer unchanged) yields the loss: */}
By weighting each term by the forward rate $[\dot{Q}_t(x_t)]_y$ (which leaves the minimizer unchanged), we can derived a new loss function to train the concrete score approximation $s_{\theta,t}$:

$$
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:score-kl-loss}
L_t(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{\substack{x_0 \sim p_0 \\ x_t \sim q(\cdot|x_0)}} \Bigg[ \sum_{y \neq x_t} [\dot{Q}_t(x_t)]_y\, \mathrm{KL}\!\left(s_t(x_t | x_0)_y,\, s_{\theta,t}(x_t)_y\right) \Bigg]
\end{equation}
$$

Finally, by expanding the KL divergence and dropping $\theta$-independent terms, we obtain an explicit form for the score-based discrete diffusion loss:

<Statement kind="Proposition" label="concrete-score-matching" title="Concrete score matching $s_t$">
The concrete score $s_t$ is the minimizer (over $s_{\theta,t}$) of
$$
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:score-loss-prop}
L_t(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{\substack{x_0 \sim p_0 \\ x_t \sim q( \cdot |x_0)}} \Bigg[ \sum_{y \neq x_t} [\dot{Q}_t(x_t)]_y \bigg( s_{\theta,t}(x_t)_y - \frac{p_t(y|x_0)}{p_t(x_t|x_0)} \log s_{\theta,t}(x_t)_y \bigg) \Bigg] + C
\end{equation}
$$
where $C$ is a constant independent of $\theta$ [<a class="cite-ref" href="#ref-4">4</a>].
</Statement>

### Equivalence between viewpoints

In the previous sections, we introduced two discrete diffusion perspectives for transporting an initial distribution to a target discrete distribution: the [variational formulation](#variational-discrete-diffusion) and the [score-based formulation](#score-based-discrete-diffusion).
We will show these two viewpoints are equivalent, and then we connect them to [discrete flow matching](#equivalence-with-discrete-flow-matching)<a href="#dfm-blog" class="dfm-note-ref"></a>.

#### Equivalence between the two discrete diffusion perspectives

Discrete diffusion models can be framed from two different perspectives: variational inference and score matching. These two viewpoints, while appearing different, are in fact equivalent. We now demonstrate this equivalence by first showing that their sampling schemes coincide, and then that their training objectives are equivalent.

__Sampling:__ We first connect the two views of discrete diffusion at the sampling level. The reverse rate for score-based discrete diffusion can be written in terms of the true denoiser $D_t^i(\mathbf{x})_v=\mathbb{P}(X_0^i=v | \mathbf{X}_t=\mathbf{x})$:

$$
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:denoiser-reverse-rate}
\dot{R}_t^i(\mathbf{x})_v
=
\mathbb{I}(x^i=m)\,
\frac{-\dot{\alpha}_t}{1-\alpha_t}
\left(
D_t^i(\mathbf{x})_v
- \mathbb{I}(v=m)
\right).
\end{equation}
$$

<details>
<summary>Proof</summary>
<div>

For $s=t-h$ and a masked token $x^i=m$, @eq:masked-diffusion-denoiser-sampling gives:
$$
\begin{align}
\mathbb{P}(X_{t-h}^i=v | \mathbf{X}_t=\mathbf{x})
&=
\frac{\alpha_{t-h}-\alpha_t}{1-\alpha_t}D_t^i(\mathbf{x})_v
+
\frac{1-\alpha_{t-h}}{1-\alpha_t}\mathbb{I}(v=m). \label{eq:denoiser-reverse-rate-proof-1}
\end{align}
$$

Since $\alpha_{t-h}-\alpha_t=-h\dot{\alpha}_t+o(h)$, we also have $1-\alpha_{t-h}=1-\alpha_t+h\dot{\alpha}_t+o(h)$. Therefore:
$$
\begin{align}
\mathbb{P}(X_{t-h}^i=v | \mathbf{X}_t=\mathbf{x})
&=
\mathbb{I}(v=m)
+
h\frac{-\dot{\alpha}_t}{1-\alpha_t}
\left(D_t^i(\mathbf{x})_v-\mathbb{I}(v=m)\right)
+o(h). \label{eq:denoiser-reverse-rate-proof-2}
\end{align}
$$

Comparing with the continuous-time reverse-rate definition:
$$
\begin{equation}
\mathbb{P}(X_{t-h}^i=v | \mathbf{X}_t=\mathbf{x})
=
\mathbb{I}(v=x^i)+h\dot{R}_t^i(\mathbf{x})_v+o(h),
\label{eq:denoiser-reverse-rate-proof-3}
\end{equation}
$$
and using $x^i=m$ gives @eq:denoiser-reverse-rate. If $x^i\neq m$, the token is already unmasked and the reverse masked process leaves it fixed, so the rate is zero; this gives the prefactor $\mathbb{I}(x^i=m)$.

</div>
</details>

Then, we show that the two discrete diffusion frameworks are equivalent by showing that their sampling schemes are the same, i.e. connecting @eq:masked-diffusion-denoiser-sampling and @eq:score-sampling-cat. Starting from @eq:masked-diffusion-denoiser-sampling and taking $s=t-h$ and letting $h \to 0$, for a masked token $x_t^i=m$:

$$
\begin{align}
x_{t-h}^i
&\sim \mathrm{Cat}\left(
\frac{\alpha_{t-h}-\alpha_t}{1-\alpha_t}D_t^i(\mathbf{x}_t)
+ \frac{1-\alpha_{t-h}}{1-\alpha_t}\mathbf{e}_m
\right) \label{eq:masked-denoiser-euler-1} \\
&= \mathrm{Cat}\left(
\frac{-h\dot{\alpha}_t + o(h)}{1-\alpha_t}D_t^i(\mathbf{x}_t)
+ \frac{1-\alpha_t + h\dot{\alpha}_t + o(h)}{1-\alpha_t}\mathbf{e}_m
\right) \label{eq:masked-denoiser-euler-2} \\
&= \mathrm{Cat}\left(
\mathbf{e}_m
+ h\,\frac{-\dot{\alpha}_t}{1-\alpha_t}
\underbrace{\left(D_t^i(\mathbf{x}_t)-\mathbf{e}_m\right)}_{
\frac{1-\alpha_t}{-\dot{\alpha}_t}\dot{R}_t^i(\mathbf{x}_t)
}
+ o(h)
\right) \label{eq:masked-denoiser-euler-3} \\
&= \mathrm{Cat}\left(
\mathbf{e}_{x_t^i}
+ h\,\dot{R}_t^i(\mathbf{x}_t)
+ o(h)
\right). \label{eq:masked-denoiser-euler-4}
\end{align}
$$
using $\alpha_{t-h} - \alpha_t = -h\dot{\alpha}_t + o(h)$ in the second line and @eq:denoiser-reverse-rate in the third line.
If $x_t^i\neq m$, both sampling processes keep the token fixed, i.e., once denoised, a token cannot be masked again or change token value.

__Training Objectives__: Now we turn to the corresponding training objectives. The [variational perspective](#variational-discrete-diffusion) gives the ELBO loss @eq:discrete-elbo-1 (which can be rewritten as @eq:mdm-loss for Masked Diffusion), while the [score-based perspective](#score-based-discrete-diffusion) gives the concrete score matching loss @eq:score-loss-prop.
We now show that these two objectives are in fact the same: taking the continuous-time limit of the variational ELBO produces, after a change of variable, exactly the concrete score matching loss @eq:score-loss-prop.

Starting from the variational loss @eq:discrete-elbo-1, in the continuous-time limit $T \to \infty$, each discrete transition $q(x_s | x_t)$ is replaced by an instantaneous rate $\dot{R}_t(x_t)$. This motivates introducing the conditional reverse rate $\dot{R}_t(x_t | x_0)$, defined by the first-order Taylor expansion:

$$
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:reverse-kernel}
q(y | x_t, x_0) = \mathbb{I}(y = x_t) + \Delta t\, [\dot{R}_t(x_t | x_0)]_y + o(\Delta t), \quad y \in \mathcal{V}
\end{equation}
$$

For Masked Diffusion, this conditional reverse rate takes the explicit form (proof below in the spoiler):

$$
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:reverse-kernel-mask}
\dot{R}_t(x_t | x_0) =
\begin{pmatrix}
 & & & 0 \\
 & \mathbf{0}_{|\mathcal{V}|} & & \vdots \\
 & & & 0 \\
0 & \dots \, r_t \, \dots & 0 & -r_t
\end{pmatrix}
= r_t\, \mathbf{e}_m (\mathbf{e}_{x_0} - \mathbf{e}_m)^\top
\end{equation}
$$

where $r_t = -\dot{\alpha}_t / (1 - \alpha_t)$ is the instantaneous unmasking rate, and the entry $r_t$ in the last row appears at the column index corresponding to $x_0$. Intuitively, this rate captures the instantaneous tendency of a masked token to revert to the clean token $x_0$.

<details>
<summary>Construction of $\dot{R}_t(x_t | x_0)$</summary>
<div>

To derive $\dot{R}_t(x_t | x_0)$, we write the reverse transition probabilities $q(x_s | x_t, x_0)$ for arbitrary $s < t$ using a matrix $R_{s|t}^{x_0}$, and then write the first-order Taylor expansion as $\Delta t = t - s \to 0$ of this matrix.

Using @eq:bridge-mask, the reverse transition $q(x_s | x_t, x_0)$ can be written as a categorical distribution governed by a reverse matrix $R_{s|t}^{x_0}$:
$$
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:bridge-cat}
q(x_s | x_t, x_0) = \mathrm{Cat}\!\left(x_s;\ (R_{s|t}^{x_0})^\top x_t\right)
\end{equation}
$$
and:
$$
\begin{align}
R_{s|t}^{x_0} =
\left(
\begin{array}{ccc|c}
 & & & 0   \\
 & I_{|\mathcal{V}|} & & \vdots  \\
 & & & 0  \\
\hline
0 & \dots \quad \frac{\alpha_s - \alpha_t}{1 - \alpha_t} \quad \dots & 0 & \frac{1 - \alpha_s}{1 - \alpha_t}
\end{array}
\right) \in \mathbb{R}^{K \times K} \label{eq:bridge-matrix}
\end{align}
$$

where the entry $\frac{\alpha_s - \alpha_t}{1 - \alpha_t}$ in the last row appears at the column corresponding to $x_0$, and can be compactly written as $R_{s|t}^{x_0} = I + \frac{\alpha_s - \alpha_t}{1 - \alpha_t} \mathbf{e}_m(\mathbf{e}_{x_0} - \mathbf{e}_m)^\top$.

Taking the first-order Taylor expansion (noting that $\alpha_{t - \Delta t} - \alpha_t \approx -\dot{\alpha}_t \Delta t$):

$$
\begin{align}
R_{t - \Delta t | t}^{x_0} &= I + \frac{-\dot{\alpha}_t \Delta t}{1 - \alpha_t} \mathbf{e}_m(\mathbf{e}_{x_0} - \mathbf{e}_m)^\top + o(\Delta t)  \label{eq:bridge-rate-1} \\
&= I + \Delta t\, \dot{R}_t(x_t | x_0) + o(\Delta t) \label{eq:bridge-rate-2}
\end{align}
$$

which identifies the conditional reverse rate as $\dot{R}_t(x_t | x_0) = r_t\, \mathbf{e}_m(\mathbf{e}_{x_0} - \mathbf{e}_m)^\top$ with $r_t = -\dot{\alpha}_t / (1 - \alpha_t)$.

</div>
</details>

<Statement kind="Proposition" label="continuous-time-elbo" title="Variational and score-based diffusion loss equivalence">
In the continuous-time limit, optimizing the variational ELBO @eq:discrete-elbo-1 is equivalent to minimizing the loss: <a href="#ratio-loss-kl" class="dfm-note-ref"></a>

$$
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:ratio-loss}
L_t(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{\substack{x_0 \sim p_0 \\ x_t \sim q( \cdot |x_0)}} \Bigg[ \sum_{y \neq x_t} \bigg( [\dot{R}_{\theta,t}(x_t)]_y - [\dot{R}_t(x_t | x_0)]_y \log [\dot{R}_{\theta,t}(x_t)]_y \bigg) \Bigg].
\end{equation}
$$

</Statement>

#### Equivalence with discrete flow matching

We now connect discrete diffusion to discrete flow matching<a href="#dfm-blog" class="dfm-note-ref"></a>. In discrete flow matching, a probability velocity $u_t$ defines the Euler sampling scheme $x_{t+h} \sim \mathrm{Cat}(\mathbf{e}_{x_t} + h\,u_t(x_t))$, and we show that this sampling rule is equivalent to sampling in discrete diffusion (@eq:reverse-rate).


__Sampling__: To align these two sampling equations directly, we must first account for the main discrepancy between the frameworks: their time conventions. In discrete diffusion, $t = 0$ corresponds to clean data, and $t = 1$ corresponds to the fully masked state. Discrete flow matching uses the exact opposite convention.

We can therefore perfectly translate between the two frameworks using a simple time reversal: $t_{\text{FM}} = 1 - t_{\text{DD}}$. Concretely, if $(x_t)_{t \in [0,1]}$ denotes the discrete diffusion reverse process, then the time-reversed process $\tilde{x}_{t'} := x_{1-t'}$ is a valid discrete flow matching process: it has the same law as the flow matching process with velocity field:

$$
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:time-reversal}
u_{t'}^{\text{FM}} = \dot{R}_{1-t'}
\end{equation}
$$

In particular, sampling from discrete diffusion (running the reverse CTMC from $t=1$ to $t=0$) is equivalent to sampling from this flow matching process (running a CTMC from $t'=0$ to $t'=1$): the two procedures produce trajectories with identical distributions.

<details>
<summary>Proof</summary>
<div>

We start from the infinitesimal reverse-time transition of the discrete diffusion process @eq:reverse-rate.

We first perform the variable change $t' = 1 - t$ in @eq:reverse-rate. We have $t = 1 - t'$, so $x_t = x_{1-t'}$ and $x_{t - \Delta t} = x_{1 - t' - \Delta t}$. The rate evaluates at $\dot{R}_{1-t'}$, giving:

$$
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:time-change}
p(x_{1-t'-\Delta t} = y | x_{1-t'}) = \mathbb{I}(y = x_{1-t'}) + \Delta t \, [\dot{R}_{1-t'}(x_{1-t'})]_y + o(\Delta t)
\end{equation}
$$

Since $t_{\text{FM}} = 1 - t_{\text{DD}}$, we identify $x^{\text{FM}}_{t'} = x_{1-t'}$. We deduce $x^{\text{FM}}_{t' + \Delta t} = x_{1 - t' - \Delta t}$. Substituting these identities into @eq:time-change:

$$
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:fm-step}
p(x^{\text{FM}}_{t' + \Delta t} = y | x^{\text{FM}}_{t'}) = \mathbb{I}(y = x^{\text{FM}}_{t'}) + \Delta t \, [\dot{R}_{1-t'}(x^{\text{FM}}_{t'})]_y + o(\Delta t)
\end{equation}
$$

Recall the definition of a probability velocity: a velocity field $u_{t'}$ generates a process $x^{\text{FM}}_{t'}$ if and only if $ p(x^{\text{FM}}_{t' + \Delta t} = y | x^{\text{FM}}_{t'} = x) = \mathbb{I}(y = x^{\text{FM}}_{t'}) + \Delta t \, [u_{t'}(x^{\text{FM}}_{t'})]_y + o(\Delta t) $.

Identifying the first-order terms in @eq:fm-step with the probability-velocity definition directly gives:

$$
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:time-reversal-velocity}
[u_{t'}^{\text{FM}}(x)]_y = [\dot{R}_{1-t'}(x)]_y
\end{equation}
$$

and therefore $u_{t'}^{\text{FM}} = \dot{R}_{1-t'}$.

</div>
</details>

__Training Objectives__: Now we show how their training objectives line up in the masked case. The [variational diffusion perspective](#variational-discrete-diffusion) gives the Masked Diffusion objective @eq:mdm-loss. In discrete flow matching, the velocity can be parameterized through a denoiser $P_{\theta,t}(X_1^i = x_1^i | X_t=\mathbf{x})$, trained with the cross-entropy objective:

$$
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:cdm-loss}
L_{\text{CDM}}(\theta) = -\mathbb{E}_{\substack{t \sim \mathcal{U}(0,1) \\ \mathbf{x}_1 \sim p_{\text{data}} \\ \mathbf{x} \sim p_t( \cdot | \mathbf{x}_1)}} \left[ \sum_{i=1}^S \log P_{\theta,t}(X_1^i = x_1^i | X_t = \mathbf{x}) \right].
\end{equation}
$$

We now compare these objectives.

To directly compare the two training losses, we first note that the denoiser used by discrete flow matching and the denoiser used by variational diffusion in @eq:mdm-loss are exactly the same object. Applying the time reversal $t_{\text{FM}} = 1 - t_{\text{DD}}$ and identifying the clean data $x_1=x_0$, we get:

$$
\begin{align}
D_{\theta,t_{\text{DD}}}^i(\mathbf{x})_{x_0^i}
&= P_{\theta,t_{\text{DD}}}(X_0^i=x_0^i | X_{t_{\text{DD}}}=\mathbf{x}) \label{eq:denoiser-time-identification-1} \\
&= P_{\theta,t_{\text{FM}}}(X_1^i=x_1^i | X_{t_{\text{FM}}}=\mathbf{x}). \label{eq:denoiser-time-identification-2}
\end{align}
$$

<Statement kind="Proposition" label="masked-mdm-cdm-loss-equivalence" title="Masked diffusion/CDM loss equivalence">
Under the time reversal $t_{\text{FM}}=1-t_{\text{DD}}$ and the denoiser identification @eq:denoiser-time-identification-1, the Masked Diffusion training loss $L_{\text{MDM}}(t)$ from @eq:mdm-loss is directly comparable to the denoising loss $L_{\text{CDM}}(t)$ from @eq:cdm-loss. At fixed flow-matching time $t$:

$$
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:mdm-cdm-scale}
L_{\text{MDM}}(t) \approx \frac{1}{1-t} \, L_{\text{CDM}}(t).
\end{equation}
$$
</Statement>

<details>
<summary>Derivation</summary>
<div>

Under the time reversal above, the weight in @eq:mdm-loss satisfies $-\dot{\alpha}_{t_{\text{DD}}}/(1-\alpha_{t_{\text{DD}}})=1/t_{\text{DD}}=1/(1-t_{\text{FM}})$. Therefore the Masked Diffusion loss @eq:mdm-loss becomes the weighted masked cross-entropy:

$$
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:mdm-mask-loss-fm-time}
L_{\text{MDM}}(t) = \mathbb{E}\!\left[\,\sum_{i=1}^S \mathbb{I}(x_t^i = m) \cdot \frac{1}{1-t} \cdot \left(-\log P_{\theta,t}(X_1^i = x_1^i | \mathbf{x}_t)\right)\right].
\end{equation}
$$

Since $P(x_t^i = m) = 1-t$, applying the law of total expectation,
$\mathbb{E}[Z]=P(x_t^i=m)\mathbb{E}[Z|x_t^i=m]+P(x_t^i\neq m)\mathbb{E}[Z|x_t^i\neq m]$:

$$
\begin{align}
L_{\text{MDM}}(t) \label{eq:mdm-t-1}
  &= (1-t) \cdot \frac{1}{1-t} \cdot \mathbb{E}\!\left[ \label{eq:mdm-t-2}
      -\log P_{\theta,t}(X_1^i = x_1^i | \mathbf{x}_t) \label{eq:mdm-t-3}
      | x_t^i = m \label{eq:mdm-t-4}
    \right]  \label{eq:mdm-t-5} \\
  &\quad + t \cdot
    \underbrace{\mathbb{E}\!\left[
      \mathbb{I}(x_t^i=m)\frac{1}{1-t}
      \left(-\log P_{\theta,t}(X_1^i = x_1^i | \mathbf{x}_t)\right)
      | x_t^i \neq m
    \right]}_{0\ \text{since }x_t^i\neq m} \notag \\
  &= \mathbb{E}\!\left[ \label{eq:mdm-t-6}
      -\log P_{\theta,t}(X_1^i = x_1^i | \mathbf{x}_t) \label{eq:mdm-t-7}
      | x_t^i = m \label{eq:mdm-t-8}
    \right]. \label{eq:mdm-t-9}
\end{align}
$$

We decompose $L_{\text{CDM}}$ using the same law of total expectation, splitting over masked and unmasked positions:

$$
\begin{align}
L_{\text{CDM}}(t) \label{eq:cm-t-1}
  &= (1-t) \cdot \mathbb{E}\!\left[ \label{eq:cm-t-2}
      -\log P_{\theta,t}(X_1^i = x_1^i | \mathbf{x}_t) \label{eq:cm-t-3}
      | x_t^i = m \label{eq:cm-t-4}
    \right]  \label{eq:cm-t-5} \\
  &\quad + t \cdot \mathbb{E}\!\left[ \label{eq:cm-t-6}
      -\log P_{\theta,t}(X_1^i = x_1^i | \mathbf{x}_t) \label{eq:cm-t-7}
      | x_t^i \neq m \label{eq:cm-t-8}
    \right]. \notag
\end{align}
$$

For unmasked positions $i$, a well trained denoiser should just copy the current token $x_t^i=x_1^i$ it sees, therefore $P_{\theta,t}(X_1^i=x_1^i|\mathbf{x}_t)=1$, and the second term vanishes, giving:

$$
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:cm-mask-loss}
L_{\text{CDM}}(t) \approx (1-t) \cdot \mathbb{E}\!\left[
  -\log P_{\theta,t}(X_1^i = x_1^i | \mathbf{x}_t)
  | x_t^i = m
\right].
\end{equation}
$$

Both expressions share the same conditional expectation. Eliminating it yields @eq:mdm-cdm-scale.

</div>
</details>

**Disclaimer** In general, learning a velocity field $u$ (or equivalently a reverse rate $\dot{R}$) with @eq:ratio-loss is not equivalent to learning a denoiser with @eq:cdm-loss for arbitrary source distributions or paths, for instance under uniform diffusion.
